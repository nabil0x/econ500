Question 1: Define social welfare. Critically explain the following criteria as a
measure of social welfare: (i) Growth of GNP, (ii) Bentham's Criteria, (iii) Cardinalist
approach.
Social welfare refers to the overall well-being of a society as a whole. Welfare economics, as defined by
Koutsoyiannis, is concerned with the evaluation of alternative economic situations (states, configurations) from the
point of view of the society's well-being. To illustrate, suppose total welfare in a country is W, but given factor
endowments and technology, welfare could potentially be larger, say W*. The tasks of welfare economics are: (a) to
show that in the present state W < W*, and (b) to suggest ways of raising W to W*. The measurement of social
welfare requires some ethical standard and interpersonal comparisons, both of which involve subjective value
judgements. Objective comparisons of the deservingness or worthiness of different individuals are virtually
impossible. Various criteria of social welfare have been suggested by economists at different times.

(i) Growth of GNP
Adam Smith implicitly accepted the growth of the wealth of a society — that is, the growth of the gross national
product — as a welfare criterion. He believed that economic growth resulted in an increase in social welfare because
growth increased employment and the goods available for consumption by the community. To Adam Smith,
economic growth meant bringing W closer to W*.
The growth criterion, however, implies an acceptance of the status quo of income distribution as 'ethical' or 'just'.
This is a strong normative assumption that may not be defensible. Consider a society where economic growth is
driven by a small elite while the majority of the population experiences stagnant or declining real incomes. The
aggregate GNP may increase substantially, but if the distribution of the additional income is highly skewed, the
majority may experience a deterioration in their well-being despite the growing GNP.
Furthermore, growth may lead to a reduction in social welfare depending on who benefits most from it. For
example, industrial growth that creates environmental pollution, urban congestion, and health hazards may increase
GNP while simultaneously reducing the quality of life for large segments of the population. The growth criterion also
fails to account for the composition of output — an economy may produce more military goods at the expense of
healthcare and education, raising GNP without necessarily improving social welfare.
Despite these limitations, the growth criterion highlights the importance of economic efficiency in social welfare
analysis. Given that social welfare depends on the amount of goods and services produced (as well as on their
distribution), efficiency is a necessary prerequisite for the maximisation of the level of welfare. Economic efficiency
can be defined objectively, and modern welfare economics is mainly concerned with the examination (comparison)
of the Pareto-efficiency of different economic situations. However, efficiency, although a necessary condition, is not
sufficient to guarantee the maximisation of social welfare. Efficiency does not dispose of the need for having an
ethical standard of comparing alternative economic states.

(ii) Bentham's Criterion
Jeremy Bentham, an English economist and philosopher, argued that welfare is improved when 'the greatest good (is
secured) for the greatest number'. Implicit in this dictum is the assumption that the total welfare is the sum of the
utilities of the individuals of the society. The application of Bentham's ethical system to economics has serious
shortcomings.
To illustrate the pitfalls, assume that society consists of three individuals, A, B, and C, so that
                                                  𝑊 = 𝑈𝐴 + 𝑈𝐵 + 𝑈𝐶
According to Bentham ∆𝑊 > 0 if (∆𝑈𝐴 + ∆𝑈𝐵 + ∆𝑈𝐶 ) > 0. However, consider a change where A's and B's utility
increases while C's utility decreases, but the sum of the gains to A and B exceeds the loss to C: (𝛥𝑈𝐴 + 𝛥𝑈𝐵 ) >
 |𝛥𝑈𝐶 |. Under Bentham's criterion, two individuals are better-off while the third is worse-off, but the total utility has
increased, so the change is declared a welfare improvement. This obviously implies that A and B have a greater

'worthiness' than C. Thus, implicit in Bentham's criterion is an interpersonal comparison of the deservingness of the
members of society — precisely the kind of subjective judgement that welfare economics seeks to avoid.
There is another difficulty with the application of Bentham's criterion: it cannot be applied to compare situations
where 'the greatest good' and the 'greatest numbers' do not exist simultaneously. For example, assume that in
situation 1, UA = 200, UB = 50, UC = 30, so that total utility is 280. In situation 2, after a redistribution, UA = 100, UB =
80, UC = 80, so that total utility is 260. The first situation has 'the greatest good' (280 > 260), but the second involves
a more even distribution of a smaller total good among the 'greatest number'. Bentham's criterion provides no basis
for choosing between these two fundamentally different configurations.

(iii) Cardinalist Approach
Several economists proposed the use of the 'law of diminishing marginal utility' as a criterion of welfare. Their
argument runs as follows. Assume that society consists of three individuals: A has an income of £1,000, while B and C
have an income of £500 each. Consumer A can buy double the quantities of goods as compared to B and C. However,
given the law of diminishing marginal utility, A's total utility is less than double the total utility of either B or C,
because A's marginal utility of money is less than that of B or C. Thus, W < W* in this situation. To increase social
welfare, income should be redistributed from A to B and C. In fact, cardinal welfare theorists would maintain that
social welfare would be maximised if income were equally distributed among all members of society.
The cardinalist approach to welfare has a serious flaw: it assumes that all individuals have identical utility functions
for money, so that with an equal income distribution, all would have the same marginal utility of money. This
assumption is too strong. Individuals differ in their attitudes toward money. A rich person may still derive more
utility from additional income due to different preferences, family circumstances, health needs, or entrepreneurial
ambitions. Conversely, a poor person may not experience the same increase in utility from additional income if, for
instance, their consumption needs are already being met through social programmes.
Furthermore, the cardinalist approach ignores the incentive effects of redistribution. If income is redistributed to
achieve equality, the incentive to work, save, and invest may be diminished, potentially reducing the total national
income available for distribution. As the textbook notes, excessive redistribution may result in Pareto inefficiency in
the use of resources and a reduction in social welfare, even though it achieves a more equal distribution of income.

at least one worse-off is a decrease in social welfare. A situation is defined as Pareto-optimal (or Pareto-efficient) if it

Why Necessary but Not Sufficient
Welfare maximisation occurs at a point on the grand utility possibility frontier (UU'). The grand utility frontier is the
envelope of all utility possibility curves, each derived from a different Pareto-efficient product-mix on the
production possibility curve. Since every point on the grand utility frontier satisfies all three marginal conditions of
Pareto efficiency, any welfare-maximising point must necessarily be Pareto-optimal. This establishes necessity: one
cannot maximise social welfare without first achieving Pareto efficiency.




Figure – The Point of Bliss (Tangency of Social Indifference Contour with Grand Utility Frontier)
There exists a very large number of Pareto-optimal states. All points on the production possibility curve are
Pareto-efficient, but they represent vastly different allocations of resources, different output combinations, and
different distributions of income between consumers A and B. The Pareto criterion alone cannot rank these
alternative Pareto-optimal states because ranking them requires an interpersonal comparison of utility —
precisely what the Pareto criterion avoids.
                                                           Page 4
To choose among Pareto-optimal states, a social welfare function is required. Consider a point N on the grand utility
frontier that is Pareto-optimal, and a point L that is NOT Pareto-optimal (lying below the grand frontier). As shown in
the Figure, point L may lie on a higher social indifference contour than point N, meaning that the non-Pareto-optimal
state yields higher social welfare than the Pareto-optimal state. However, it can be shown that given any inefficient
point (below the grand utility frontier), there will exist some point(s) on the grand utility frontier that represents an
improvement in social welfare over that inefficient point.
The point of maximum social welfare — the 'bliss point' w* — occurs where the grand utility possibility frontier is
tangent to the highest attainable social indifference contour. At this point, and only at this point, all three Pareto
conditions are satisfied AND social welfare is maximised.

Question 3: Explain the underlying assumption about the marginal utility of money
for the Hicks-Kaldor compensation criterion to be correct.
Nicholas Kaldor and John Hicks suggested an approach to evaluating welfare changes that involves the concept of
hypothetical compensation. Assume that a change in the economy is being considered, which will benefit some
('gainers') and hurt others ('losers'). One asks the 'gainers' how much money they would be prepared to pay in order
to have the change implemented, and the 'losers' how much money they would be prepared to pay in order to
prevent the change. If the amount of money of the 'gainers' is greater than the amount of the 'losers', the change
constitutes an improvement in social welfare, because the 'gainers' could compensate the 'losers' and still be left
with some 'net gain'. Thus, the criterion states: a change constitutes an improvement in social welfare if those who
benefit from it could compensate those who are hurt, and still be left with some 'net gain'.

The Critical Assumption — Equal Marginal Utility of Money
The Kaldor-Hicks criterion evaluates alternative situations on the basis of monetary valuations of different persons.
In doing so, it implicitly assumes that the marginal utility of money is the same for all individuals in the society. The
logic is as follows: when comparing the amount that gainers are willing to pay with the amount that losers would
require as compensation, we are effectively comparing utils (units of utility). For monetary amounts to be
comparable across individuals in utility terms, each unit of money must represent the same amount of utility for
each person. If the marginal utility of money differs across individuals, then a gain of £1,000 to a millionaire
represents far fewer utils than a loss of £1,000 to a poor person, and the comparison becomes meaningless.

Why This Assumption Is Unrealistic
In the real world, income distribution is highly unequal, and the marginal utility of money varies significantly across
individuals. Consider an economy with two individuals: A, who is a millionaire, and B, who has an income of £4,000.
Suppose the government considers a change that will benefit A and hurt B. A is willing to pay £2,000 for this change,
while B would pay £1,000 to prevent it. According to the Kaldor-Hicks criterion, the change improves welfare (net
gain of £1,000 after hypothetical compensation). However, the gain of £2,000 provides very little additional utility to
the millionaire A, whose marginal utility of money is extremely low, while the loss of £1,000 represents a substantial
reduction in well-being for B, whose marginal utility of money is very high. The total welfare may actually decrease,
not increase, if the change takes place.

Additional Criticisms
The Kaldor-Hicks criterion also suffers from the Scitovsky paradox: it is possible for situation K to be declared
superior to situation J by the Kaldor criterion, while simultaneously J is declared superior to K by the same criterion.
This intransitivity arises because the distribution of income differs between the two situations, affecting the marginal
utility of money and hence the willingness to pay or accept compensation.
Furthermore, the criterion is based on hypothetical compensation, not actual compensation. The 'gainers' are not
required to actually compensate the 'losers'; the test is purely hypothetical. This means that the criterion can declare
a change a welfare improvement even when the losers are made significantly worse-off in reality. The criterion
ignores the existing income distribution and makes implicit interpersonal comparisons by assuming that the same
amounts of money have the same utility for individuals with vastly different incomes. Only if the marginal utility of
money were equal for all individuals would the Kaldor-Hicks criterion be a 'correct' welfare measure — but this
condition can only hold under perfectly equal income distribution.

Question 4: Define social welfare function. How could it be used as a criterion to
measure social welfare? Explain.
The social welfare function (SWF) was introduced by Abram Bergson as an explicit set of ethical value judgements
that permits a unique preference-ordering of all possible economic states (configurations) in which different
individuals enjoy different utility levels. If the economy consists of two individuals A and B, the social welfare
function can be written as

                                                    𝑊 = 𝑓(𝑈𝐴 , 𝑈𝐵 )
where 𝑈𝐴 and 𝑈𝐵 are the utility levels (in ordinal indexes) of consumers A and B, respectively.

Graphical Representation




Figure – Bergson's Welfare Contours (Social Indifference Curves in Utility Space)
The social welfare function can be represented by a set of social indifference contours (also called Bergson welfare
contours) in utility space. On a graph whose axes measure the utilities of A and B, each social indifference contour
is the locus of combinations of (UA, UB) that yield the same level of social welfare. The contours have the standard
properties of indifference curves: they are downward-sloping (more utility for one individual must be offset by less
for the other to maintain the same welfare level), they do not intersect, and higher contours (further to the
northeast) represent higher levels of social welfare.
With such a set of social indifference contours, alternative economic states can be unambiguously evaluated. For
example, a change which would move the society from point b to point c (or d) increases social welfare. A change
moving the society from point a to point b leaves the level of social welfare unaltered.

Use as a Criterion for Measuring Social Welfare
The social welfare function serves as a comprehensive welfare criterion when used in conjunction with the grand
utility possibility frontier. The procedure is as follows:
 Step 1: Derive the grand utility possibility frontier, which shows the maximum utility attainable by one consumer
    given the utility of the other, across all possible product-mixes and efficient distributions.
 Step 2: Superimpose the social indifference contours on the grand utility frontier.
 Step 3: The social welfare-maximising point is the point of tangency between the grand utility frontier and the
    highest attainable social indifference contour. This point is called the 'bliss point'.
The social welfare function thus provides a complete and unambiguous method for selecting among all Pareto-
optimal states. While the Pareto criterion can identify efficient states, it cannot rank them. The SWF fills this gap by
incorporating ethical judgements about the relative deservingness of individuals into the ranking mechanism.




                                                          Page 7
Determination of Welfare-Maximising Variables
At the bliss point, the welfare-maximising values of all ten unknowns in the 2×2×2 model are determined uniquely:
the total quantities of the two products (X* and Y*), the quantities consumed by each consumer (𝑋𝐴∗ , 𝑋𝐵∗ , 𝑌𝐴∗ , 𝑌𝐵∗ ), and
the allocation of resources between industries (𝐿∗𝑥 , 𝐿∗𝑦 , 𝐾𝑥∗ , 𝐾𝑦∗ )). The bliss point is associated with a unique
commodity-mix on the PPC, a unique distribution of commodities on the Edgeworth contract curve of exchange, and
a unique allocation of factors on the Edgeworth contract curve of production.

The Problem of Construction
The fundamental limitation of the social welfare function is that there is no objective method of constructing it. Its
existence is axiomatically assumed in welfare economics. The SWF requires somebody to compare various
individuals or groups and rank them according to what they think their worthiness is — an inherently subjective
exercise involving value judgements. A democratically elected government could be assumed to make such value
judgements acceptable to the society as a whole, but this remains an implicit assumption.
Arrow's Impossibility Theorem demonstrates that no social welfare function can be constructed from individual
preference orderings that simultaneously satisfies a set of reasonable conditions (unrestricted domain, Pareto
principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship). This further underscores the difficulty of
constructing a consistent and democratic SWF.
It should also be noted that the social welfare function cannot be used to derive community indifference curves in
output space without taking into account the distribution of income among individuals. The community MRS at a
given point in commodity space depends on how the goods are distributed among individuals, i.e., on which utility
combination on the Edgeworth contract curve of exchange is chosen.

Question 5: What are the major assumptions of social welfare maximization in a
2×2×2 model?
The social welfare maximisation problem in the 2×2×2 model is formulated under the following set of assumptions,
each of which plays a critical role in ensuring that the model yields a determinate solution:
1. There are two factors, labour L, and capital K, whose quantities are given (in perfectly inelastic supply).
   These factors are homogeneous and perfectly divisible.
2. Two products, X and Y, are produced by two firms. Each firm produces only one commodity. The
   production functions give rise to smooth isoquants, convex to the origin, with constant returns to scale.
   Indivisibilities in the production processes are ruled out.
3. There are two consumers whose preferences are represented by indifference curves, which are
   continuous, convex to the origin and do not intersect.
4. The goal of consumers is utility maximisation and the goal of firms is profit maximisation.
5. The production functions are independent. This rules out joint products and external economies and
   diseconomies in production.
6. The utilities of consumers are independent. Bandwagon, snob and Veblen effects are ruled out. There
   are no external economies or diseconomies in consumption.
7. The ownership of factors, that is, the distribution of the given L and K between the two consumers, is
   exogenously determined.
8. A social welfare function, W = f(UA, UB ), exists. This permits a unique preference-ordering of all
   possible states, based on the positions of the two consumers in their own preference maps. This welfare
   function incorporates an ethical valuation of the relative deservingness or worthiness of the two
   consumers.
The problem is to determine the welfare-maximizing values of ten variables: the total quantities of X and Y, their
distribution between the two consumers (𝑋𝐴, 𝑋𝐵, 𝑌𝐴, 𝑌𝐵), and the welfare-maximising allocation of resources
(𝐿𝑥 , 𝐿𝑦, 𝐾𝑥 , 𝐾𝑦).

Question 7: Critically explain the three key things that Pigou dealt with in analyzing
social welfare.
A.C. Pigou, in his seminal work 'The Economics of Welfare' (1920), laid the foundations of welfare economics by
identifying three fundamental dimensions of social welfare analysis. His three contributions — efficiency, equity
through redistribution, and correcting externalities — remain the pillars of welfare economic analysis. The three key
aspects that Pigou dealt with in analyzing social welfare are:

1. The Size of the National Dividend (Economic Efficiency)
Pigou's first concern was with the aggregate size of national output. He argued that social welfare depends
fundamentally on the total quantity of goods and services available for consumption — the 'national dividend' in
Pigou's terminology. A larger national dividend means that, in principle, more goods and services are available to
satisfy the needs and wants of society's members. This aligns with the GNP growth criterion. Pigou emphasised that
increasing the total output of the economy is a prerequisite for improving social welfare. In the language of the
2×2×2 model, this means moving the economy to the production possibility frontier and operating at a point where
all resources are efficiently employed. The efficiency dimension is captured by the three marginal conditions of
Pareto optimality: efficiency in exchange, efficiency in production, and efficiency in the product-mix.
However, Pigou recognised that efficiency alone is not sufficient. The size of the national dividend tells us nothing
about how the output is distributed among members of society. Two economies with the same GNP can have vastly
different levels of social welfare depending on how income is distributed.

2. The Distribution of the National Dividend (Equity through Redistribution)
Pigou's second concern was with the equitable distribution of income. He argued that, given the law of diminishing
marginal utility, redistributing income from the rich to the poor would increase total social welfare even if total
output remained unchanged. This is because the marginal utility of an additional pound of income is higher for a
poor person than for a rich person. This is the cardinalist approach to welfar. Cardinal welfare theorists maintained
that social welfare would be maximised if income were equally distributed among all members of society, since
equal distribution would equalise the marginal utility of income across all individuals.
Pigou's analysis of distribution directly connects to the social welfare function. The choice of a particular point on the
grand utility possibility frontier — the selection of the bliss point — inherently involves an ethical judgement about
the distribution of welfare between individuals A and B. Different social welfare functions, reflecting different ethical
values, would select different bliss points and hence different distributions of income and output.

3. Externalities and the Divergence Between Private and Social Costs/Benefits
(Correcting Externalities)
Pigou's third major contribution was his analysis of externalities — situations where the actions of an individual
economic agent create costs or benefits for others that are not reflected in market prices.
When private cost is less than social cost, the condition for optimal resource allocation is violated. For example,
consider a firm producing alcohol in a perfectly competitive market. The firm is in equilibrium when 𝑀𝐶𝑥 = 𝑃𝑥 ,
where 𝑀𝐶𝑥 is the private marginal cost. This does not include the cost of pollution, health hazards, and accidents
caused by alcohol consumption. The marginal social cost is: 𝑀𝑆𝐶𝑥 = 𝑀𝐶𝑥 + 𝑀𝐸𝐶, where MEC is the marginal
external cost. Since 𝑀𝐶𝑥 = 𝑃𝑥 , it follows that 𝑃𝑥 < 𝑀𝑆𝐶𝑥 , which implies that the allocation of resources to the
production of alcohol is not socially optimal. The firm produces too much because it does not bear the full social cost
of its production.
Pigou's solution was to impose a tax (a 'Pigouvian tax') equal to the marginal external cost, so that the firm's
effective marginal cost becomes equal to the social marginal cost. The firm would then produce where: 𝑃𝑥 =
𝑀𝑆𝐶𝑥 = 𝑀𝐶𝑥 + 𝑀𝐸𝐶. This is the socially optimal level of output. Similarly, when there are external benefits

While Pigou's framework was pioneering, it has limitations. The cardinalist approach to income distribution assumes

The "duality theorem" of welfare economics states that welfare maximization is independent of prices. However,
implicit in the purely technocratic formulation is a set of constants that can be the prices of a perfectly competitive
economy. When individual profit maximizers and utility maximizers act in response to these prices, their behavior

pursuing self-interest, is led to promote the general welfare.
However, perfect competition is only one way of attaining welfare maximization. A decentralized socialist system
(Lerner-Lange type), where the government estimates "shadow prices" and directs economic units to maximize
their gains, can in principle achieve the same results. The model collapses when its assumptions are violated
(externalities, increasing returns, indivisibilities, absence of social welfare function).

Question 9: Graphically explain the problems of existence, uniqueness and stability in
connection to general equilibrium.
Three fundamental problems arise in connection with general equilibrium:

1. Existence Poblem
A general equilibrium solution exists if each market is cleared at a positive price, with each consumer maximising
satisfaction and each firm maximising profit. In the partial-equilibrium context, an equilibrium exists when the
demand and supply curves intersect at a positive price where 𝑄𝐷 = 𝑄𝑆 .
The excess demand function 𝐸(𝑃) = 𝑄𝐷(𝑃) − 𝑄𝑆(𝑃) provides a powerful analytical tool. An equilibrium exists when
𝐸(𝑃) = 0, i.e., when the excess demand curve intersects the vertical price-axis. If 𝐸(𝑃) lies entirely above the axis
at all prices, excess demand is always positive and no equilibrium price exists (the market cannot clear). If 𝐸(𝑃) lies
entirely below the axis, excess supply always prevails.
In general equilibrium, Arrow and Debreu (1954) provided a rigorous proof of existence for perfectly competitive
markets with no indivisibilities and no increasing returns to scale. The proof relies on fixed-point theorems
(Brouwer's or Kakutani's) rather than simple equation counting. Arrow and Hahn (1971) extended the proof to
economies with limited increasing returns and monopolistic competition, without indivisibilities. The key takeaway
from the existence literature is that the mere equality of the number of equations and unknowns in the Walrasian
system is neither necessary nor sufficient for existence. The existence proof requires well-behaved (continuous,
convex) production and demand functions.




Figure – No Equilibrium Exists (Demand and Supply Do Not Intersect)




Figure – No Equilibrium Exists in Excess Demand Function

2. Uniqueness Problem
If an equilibrium exists, it may not be unique. Multiple equilibria arise when the excess demand function intersects
the vertical price-axis more than once. In this case, there are several distinct price-quantity combinations at which
markets clear. The number of equilibria equals the number of intersections of E(P) with the price-axis. In a partial
equilibrium context, multiple equilibria can arise from non-linear demand and supply functions that cross more
than once. In general equilibrium, multiple equilibria are more common because of the interdependence among
markets.


                                                         Page 16
Uniqueness can be guaranteed under certain conditions. If all commodities are gross substitutes (i.e., the cross-price
effect on excess demand is positive), then the equilibrium is unique. Uniqueness can also be guaranteed if the weak
axiom of revealed preference holds in the aggregate.




Figure – Multiple Equilibria




Figure – Multiple Equilibria in Excess Demand Space

3. Stability Problem
If an equilibrium exists, it may be stable or unstable. An equilibrium is stable if, following a disturbance, the system
converges back to the equilibrium. Stability depends on the dynamic adjustment process — the mechanism by
which prices change in response to excess demand or supply.
In terms of the excess demand function, stability requires that the slope of E(P) at the equilibrium point be negative.
When the slope is negative: if price is above equilibrium, E(P) < 0 (excess supply), which pushes price down; if price
is below equilibrium, E(P) > 0 (excess demand), which pushes price up. This self-correcting mechanism drives the
system back to equilibrium. When the slope is positive: if price is above equilibrium, E(P) > 0 (excess demand), which
pushes price further up; if price is below equilibrium, E(P) < 0 (excess supply), which pushes price further down. The
system diverges from equilibrium — the equilibrium is unstable.
Walras proposed the tâtonnement (groping) process as the adjustment mechanism: an external auctioneer adjusts
prices iteratively, increasing prices in markets with excess demand and decreasing prices in markets with excess
supply. Walras argued that the system would eventually converge because supply and demand respond more to
own-price changes than to other-price changes. However, the tâtonnement process is unsatisfactory for several
reasons: it is essentially timeless (no transactions occur until equilibrium is reached), it evades the real-time
adjustment issue, and there is no guarantee of convergence in all cases. The textbook discusses two important
stability theorems: (a) if all commodities are strict gross substitutes, the equilibrium is stable; and (b) if the weak
axiom of revealed preference holds in the aggregate, the system is globally stable.

Question 10: Define consumption externality. Explain by Bandwagon effect and Snob
effect.
A consumption externality exists when the utility level (satisfaction) of one consumer is directly affected by the
consumption pattern of another consumer. In the standard 2×2×2 model, it is assumed that consumer choices are
independent — the consumption patterns of A do not affect B's utility, and vice versa. When this assumption of
independence is violated, the standard conditions for Pareto optimality — particularly the equalisation of the
marginal rate of substitution across consumers — do not ensure Pareto-optimal outcomes. The textbook explicitly
states that when externalities in consumption are present, the equalisation of the marginal rate of substitution of
commodities among consumers does not lead to Pareto optimality. This is because the private marginal rate of
substitution no longer reflects the true social marginal rate of substitution when one consumer's utility depends on
another's consumption.

Bandwagon Effect
The bandwagon effect occurs when a consumer's demand for a commodity increases because other consumers are
also purchasing the same commodity. This is an imitation or conformity effect driven by fashion trends, social
pressure, or the desire to belong to a group. When a new style of clothing becomes popular, for example, individuals
may purchase it not because of its intrinsic utility but because they observe others wearing it and wish to conform.
In terms of the formal model, the bandwagon effect means that consumer A's utility function depends positively on
consumer B's consumption of good X. If A's indifference map is drawn in (X, Y) space, a change in B's consumption of
X shifts A's entire indifference map — specifically, an increase in B's consumption of X shifts A's indifference curves
outward (representing a positive externality in consumption). This creates an external economy for consumer A
arising from B's consumption. Economically, the bandwagon effect means that the market demand curve for the
commodity is more elastic than it would be in the absence of the externality. A small price decrease triggers a larger
quantity increase because the initial purchasers attract additional purchasers through the imitation effect. This can
lead to instability in the market and may result in overconsumption relative to the social optimum.

Snob Effect
The snob effect is the reverse of the bandwagon effect: a consumer's demand for a commodity decreases because
others are also purchasing it. Snobs derive utility from exclusivity and uniqueness — they value goods precisely
because few others possess them. If a luxury item (such as a designer handbag or a rare artwork) becomes widely
adopted, a snob consumer's valuation of it decreases because it no longer serves as an effective status signal. In the
formal model, consumer A's utility function depends negatively on consumer B's consumption of good X. An increase
in B's consumption of X shifts A's indifference curves inward (representing a negative externality — an external
diseconomy in consumption). The market demand curve under the snob effect is less elastic than it would be
otherwise, because increased popularity of the good reduces the quantity demanded by snob consumers.

Implication for Welfare
When consumption externalities are present, the standard first-order conditions for Pareto optimality are no longer
valid. The textbook illustrates this with a detailed example using following Figures. Suppose there is a consumption
externality in commodity X (for example, tobacco smoking): A's consumption of X creates a negative externality for

equilibrium? Examine with example.

Definition of General Equilibrium
A general equilibrium is defined as a state in which all markets and all decision-making units are in simultaneous
equilibrium. More formally, a general equilibrium exists if each market in the economy is cleared at a positive price
(i.e., there is neither excess demand nor excess supply), with each consumer maximising satisfaction (utility) and
each firm maximising profit. The scope of general equilibrium analysis is the examination of how this state can be
reached — that is, how prices are determined simultaneously in all markets so that the plans of all economic agents
are mutually consistent.
General equilibrium emerges from the solution of a simultaneous equation model. In the Walrasian system, the
unknowns are the prices of all factors and all commodities, and the quantities purchased and sold by each consumer
and each producer. The equations are of two types: behavioural equations (demand and supply functions derived
from optimisation) and clearing-the-market equations. The system captures the fundamental interdependence of all
markets: consumers' demands depend on incomes, incomes depend on factor prices, factor prices depend on
demand for inputs by firms, and firms' input demands depend on demand for final goods.

Definition of Partial Equilibrium
Partial equilibrium analysis, developed by Alfred Marshall, concentrates on decisions in a particular segment of the
economy in isolation, under the ceteris paribus assumption. In this approach, the price and quantity in each market
are determined by demand and supply curves drawn on the ceteris paribus clause. Each market is regarded
independently of the others. The partial equilibrium approach is also known as the 'Marshallian approach'.

Key Differences
 1. Scope: Partial equilibrium examines one market at a time (e.g., the market for good X), holding all other market
     conditions constant. General equilibrium examines all markets simultaneously (e.g., markets for X, Y, labour,
     and capital together).
 2. Interdependence: Partial equilibrium ignores interdependence between markets. General equilibrium explicitly
     accounts for the circular interdependence: consumers' demands depend on incomes, incomes depend on factor
     prices, factor prices depend on demand for inputs by firms, and firms' demands depend on demand for final
     goods.
 3. Price determination: In partial equilibrium, price is determined by supply and demand in a single market. In
     general equilibrium, prices in all markets are simultaneously determined as a solution to a system of
     simultaneous equations.
 4. Number of variables: Partial equilibrium deals with a few variables (price and quantity in one market). General
    equilibrium involves millions of variables (prices and quantities of all commodities and factors, and their
    allocation among all consumers and producers).
 5. Completeness of Results: Partial equilibrium results are valid only for the market under study. General
     equilibrium results account for all cross-market effects.

Example
Partial equilibrium analysis:
A tax on commodity X shifts the supply curve upward. The equilibrium price rises (but by less than the full tax), the
quantity falls, consumer surplus decreases, producer surplus decreases, and the government collects tax revenue.
Deadweight loss is created. The analysis stops here.



                                                        Page 29
General equilibrium analysis:
In addition to the direct effects on the X market, the tax triggers a cascade of adjustments across the economy: (a)
The market for substitute good Y: Demand for Y increases (since X is now more expensive). PY rises and QY increases.
(b) Factor markets: As X production decreases and Y production increases, the demand for factors shifts. If X and Y
have different factor intensities, factor prices change. If X is labour-intensive and Y is capital-intensive, the demand
for labour decreases and the demand for capital increases, leading to a decrease in wages and an increase in the
rental rate of capital. (c) Income distribution: The change in factor prices affects the distribution of income between
labour and capital owners. Workers (whose wages have fallen) reduce their demand for both X and Y. Capital owners
(whose rental income has risen) increase their demand. These income effects feed back into the product markets.
(d) General equilibrium: A new set of equilibrium prices (𝑃𝑋∗ , 𝑃𝑌∗ , 𝑤 ∗ , 𝑟 ∗ ) emerges, at which all four markets clear
simultaneously. The final effects on prices and quantities differ from the partial equilibrium predictions because of
the cross-market feedback effects.

Question 15: Present a 2×2×2 Walrasian system. Does the general equilibrium
solution exist in such a model? Explain.

The 2×2×2 Walrasian System
The Walrasian system, developed by the French economist Léon Walras (1834–1910), is the most ambitious general
equilibrium model. In the simplified 2×2×2 version, the economy consists of: two consumers (A and B) who buy
commodities and supply factor services; two commodities (X and Y), each produced by one firm; two factors of
production (K and L) owned by the consumers.

Unknowns (18 total):
 Quantities demanded of X and Y by consumers: 2 × 2 = 4
 Quantities supplied of K and L by consumers: 2 × 2 = 4
 Quantities demanded of K and L by firms: 2 × 2 = 4
 Quantities of X and Y supplied by firms: 2
 Prices of commodities (Px, Py): 2
 Prices of factors (w, r): 2

Equations (18 total):
 Consumer demand functions: 2 × 2 = 4
 Consumer factor supply functions: 2 × 2 = 4
 Firm factor demand functions: 2 × 2 = 4
 Firm commodity supply functions: 2
 Commodity market clearing: 2
 Factor market clearing: 2

Does a Solution Exist?
Although the number of equations equals the number of unknowns (18 each), a general equilibrium solution is not
guaranteed for several reasons:
 1. Redundant equation: One equation is not independent of the others (proved via Walras's Law: if aggregate
     expenditures equal aggregate income as an identity, then if N−1 markets clear, the Nth must also clear). This
     means the number of independent equations is 17, less than the 18 unknowns. Prices can only be determined
     as ratios.
 2. Numeraire device: By choosing one commodity as numeraire (setting its price = 1), we reduce unknowns to 17,
    matching the 17 independent equations. However, this determines only relative prices, not absolute price
    levels.
 3. Existence proof: Arrow and Debreu (1954) proved that a general equilibrium exists in perfectly competitive
     markets with no indivisibilities and no increasing returns to scale, provided production and demand functions
     are continuous and well-behaved (convex). The proof relies on fixed-point theorems rather than simple
     equation counting.
 4. Counting is insufficient: Equality of equations and unknowns is neither necessary nor sufficient for existence.
     The system may have no solution, a unique solution, or multiple solutions depending on the shapes and
     properties of the underlying functions.
Under the assumptions of the model (perfect competition, convex preferences, constant returns to scale, no
externalities), a general equilibrium solution does exist in the 2×2×2 model, as proved by Arrow and Debreu.
However, this existence depends critically on the restrictive assumptions. The system determines relative prices and
quantities uniquely; absolute prices require the introduction of money.

Question 16: What are the assumptions of a 2×2×2 model? How does general

Assumptions
 1. Two factors of production (L and K) in given (fixed) quantities; homogeneous and perfectly divisible.
 2. Two commodities (X and Y) produced by two firms; technology given with smooth, convex-to-origin isoquants
     and constant returns to scale. Production functions are independent (no externalities).
 3. Two consumers (A and B) with ordinal indifference curves convex to the origin, exhibiting diminishing MRS.
     Consumer choices are independent (no bandwagon, snob, or Veblen effects).
 4. Consumers maximize utility subject to income constraint; firms maximize profit subject to production function
     constraint.
 5. Factors are owned by consumers.
 6. Full employment of factors; all incomes are spent.
 7. Perfect competition in commodity and factor markets; all agents face the same prices (Px, Py, w, r).

How General Equilibrium Is Reached
The process begins from an initial equilibrium state that is disturbed by an exogenous change (e.g., a shift in
consumer tastes).

1. Product market adjustment
Suppose demand for X increases. Px rises, creating excess profits. Demand for substitute Y decreases; Py falls,
creating losses. Firms in X expand output; firms in Y contract. The economy moves along the PPC from the initial to a
new product-mix.

2. Factor market adjustment
Expansion of X increases demand for L and K in industry X (𝐷𝐿𝑥 and 𝐷𝐾𝑥 shift right; wx and rx rise). Contraction of Y
reduces demand for L and K in industry Y (DLy and DKy shift left; wy and ry fall).

3. Factor mobility
Factor owners withdraw services from Y and seek employment in X where factor prices are higher. Supply of factors
in X shifts rightward (reducing wx and rx); supply in Y shifts leftward (increasing wy and ry). This process continues
until factor prices are equalized: wx = wy = w*; rx = ry = r*.

Intuition
The MRPT shows the rate at which the economy can transform Y into X. To produce one more unit of X, the
economy must absorb additional factors whose cost equals MCx. Simultaneously, to release these factors, the
economy must reduce Y by an amount whose cost saving equals MCy. The ratio MCx/MCy precisely captures the rate
at which Y must be sacrificed (in cost terms) to obtain an additional unit of X.
