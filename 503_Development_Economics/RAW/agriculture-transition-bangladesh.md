## Part A -- The Stylised Fact: Agriculture's Declining GDP Share (6 marks)

### 1. The Empirical Pattern

The declining share of agriculture in GDP is one of the most robust empirical regularities in development economics. Bangladesh exemplifies this pattern dramatically: agriculture contributed approximately **60% of GDP** at independence in 1971, but by 2023 this had fallen to **~11-12%** (World Bank, 2024).

**Table 1: Bangladesh -- Agriculture Value Added as % of GDP (Select Years)**

| Year | Agriculture share of GDP (%) | Source / Notes |
|------|------------------------------|----------------|
| 1971 | ~55-60 | Post-independence, war-disrupted economy |
| 1975 | 61.95 | Peak recorded (World Bank) |
| 1980 | ~45 | Early Green Revolution phase |
| 1990 | ~30 | Structural adjustment period |
| 2000 | ~25 | Rising RMG sector |
| 2010 | ~17 | Accelerated industrialisation |
| 2015 | ~14 | Continued service-sector expansion |
| 2020 | 12.65 | COVID-19 year (CEIC) |
| 2023 | 11.00 | Record low (World Bank) |
| 2024 | 11.17 | Modest uptick (World Bank) |
| 2025 | 11.42 | (CEIC estimate) |

*Data sources: World Bank WDI (NV.AGR.TOTL.ZS); CEIC; BBS National Accounts.*

### 2. The Employment Side of the Story

While agriculture's GDP share has collapsed, its **employment share** has declined far more slowly -- a classic symptom of **labour productivity gaps** between sectors.

**Table 2: Bangladesh -- Employment in Agriculture (% of Total Employment)**

| Year | Employment share (%) | Notes |
|------|---------------------|-------|
| 1971 | ~80 (est.) | Overwhelmingly agrarian labour force |
| 1991 | 69.92 | ILO modelled estimate -- series high |
| 2000 | ~62 | Gradual decline begins |
| 2010 | ~52 | Accelerating rural-urban migration |
| 2017 | 40.60 | Series low (LFS 2016-17) |
| 2020 | ~42 | COVID-19 reverse migration |
| 2022 | 45.33 | BBS Labour Force Survey |
| 2023 | 44.74 | ILO modelled estimate |
| 2025 | 44.26 | ILO modelled estimate |

*Data sources: ILO modelled estimates (SL.AGR.EMPL.ZS); BBS Labour Force Survey 2022.*

**Key observation:** In 2025, agriculture still employs **~44% of the labour force** while producing only **~11% of GDP**. This implies that agricultural labour productivity is roughly **one-quarter** of the economy-wide average -- a massive productivity gap that signals both a problem (underemployment, low earnings) and an opportunity (productivity-raising interventions have high potential returns).

### 3. Theoretical Underpinnings: Structural Change

The pattern is consistent with the **Clark-Fisher hypothesis** (1940) and the **Lewis dual-sector model** (1954):

- **Clark-Fisher:** As per capita income rises, labour shifts from primary (agriculture) to secondary (manufacturing) to tertiary (services) sectors. Bangladesh has followed this sequence, albeit with a slower-than-desired shift out of agriculture.
- **Lewis model:** Agriculture acts as a "reserve army" of surplus labour with near-zero marginal product. Modern-sector industrialisation draws this labour into higher-productivity manufacturing and services, raising economy-wide output. The declining GDP share of agriculture is a direct consequence of this intersectoral transfer.

However, Bangladesh's case deviates from the classic Lewis path in one important respect: the **employment share has not fallen as fast as the GDP share**, indicating that labour has been "stuck" in low-productivity agriculture even as the sector's relative output declined. This is the **structural transformation puzzle** that continues to occupy development economists studying Bangladesh.

## Part B -- The Transition the Sector Has Undergone (6 marks)

Despite its declining share of GDP, the agriculture sector has undergone a profound **qualitative transformation** across multiple dimensions.

### 4. The Green Revolution (1960s-1980s)

Bangladesh's agricultural transformation began with the **Green Revolution**, which involved the introduction of:

- **High-Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds:** The Bangladesh Rice Research Institute (BRRI), established in 1971, released BR-3 (the "BR-revolution" variety) which rapidly spread across all three rice seasons (Aus, Aman, Boro). By the 2000s, HYV seeds covered ~66% of rice land and contributed ~73% of total rice output.
- **Chemical fertilisers and pesticides:** Urea, TSP, MoP fertiliser use expanded from negligible levels in the 1960s to ~5 million nutrient tonnes annually by 2020. Pesticide use also increased sharply, raising productivity but creating environmental concerns.
- **Irrigation expansion:** Irrigated area expanded from ~0.9 million acres in the early 1970s to over **5 million acres** by the 1980s, driven by tube wells and low-lift pumps. By 2024, approximately 80% of total cropped area was irrigated.
- **Mechanisation:** Adoption of power tillers, tractors, combine harvesters, rice transplanters, and threshers accelerated from the 1980s onward. By 2025, power tillers and tractors are widely used across most regions, though smallholders still face affordability constraints.

### 5. Crop Diversification

From a near-monoculture of rice and jute at independence, Bangladesh's crop profile has diversified substantially:

| Crop | 1971 Production | Recent Production | Change |
|------|----------------|-------------------|--------|
| Rice (paddy) | ~15 M tonnes | ~55 M tonnes (2024) | ~3.7x increase |
| Maize | Negligible | ~6 M tonnes | Major new crop (poultry feed) |
| Potatoes | ~1 M tonnes | ~10 M tonnes | 10x increase |
| Vegetables | Limited | ~5 M tonnes | Major expansion |
| Fisheries (inland) | ~0.5 M tonnes | ~4.8 M tonnes | Nearly 10x |
| Livestock | Subsistence | Commercialised | Major shift |

*Sources: BBS, FAO, IRRI.*

**Drivers of diversification:**
- Rising demand for protein (poultry, eggs, milk, fish) from a growing middle class
- Price incentives and market integration
- Government policy support (subsidies, credit, extension)
- Private-sector investment in agro-processing (feed mills, cold storage, processing plants)

### 6. Institutional and Policy Changes

| Domain | Pre-1971 Situation | Post-Independence Transformation |
|--------|-------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Agricultural research** | Minimal | BRRI, BINA, BARI -- over 120 rice varieties released |
| **Extension services** | Weak, top-down | DAE network, NGO partnerships (BRAC, CARE) |
| **Rural credit** | Informal moneylenders (high interest) | Grameen Bank (1976), BRAC, Krishi Bank, microfinance |
| **Input subsidies** | None organised | Fertiliser subsidies, diesel subsidies, electricity for irrigation |
| **Land reform** | Zamindari system (abolished 1950) | Land ceiling laws, sharecropping regulation (though implementation weak) |
| **Seed distribution** | Farmer-saved seed dominant | BADC seed distribution, hybrid seed market growth |
| **Market liberalisation** | State-controlled | Private-sector input markets, contract farming |

### 7. Climate Adaptation

Bangladesh is one of the most climate-vulnerable countries globally. The agricultural sector has responded with significant adaptation:

- **Saline-tolerant rice varieties:** BRRI dhan47, BRRI dhan53, BRRI dhan54, BRRI dhan61 and later BRRI dhan109 (tidal flood tolerance), Joli-3 (prolonged submergence) -- enabling cultivation in coastal zones previously unsuitable for HYV rice.
- **Flood-tolerant rice:** Sub1 gene introgression into popular varieties (BRRI dhan51, BRRI dhan52) -- withstands 14-18 days of complete submergence.
- **Drought-tolerant varieties:** Developed for the rainfed Aman season in the Barind tract.
- **Disaster preparedness:** Early warning systems, cyclone shelters protecting livestock, crop insurance pilots.
- **System of Rice Intensification (SRI):** Adopted by hundreds of thousands of farmers -- reduces water use by 30-40% while increasing yields 15-35%.

## Part C -- Continued Significant Contributions (6 marks)

### 8. Food Security and Rice Self-Sufficiency

The single most dramatic achievement of Bangladesh's agricultural transition is its journey from chronic food deficit to **near self-sufficiency in rice**.

**Table 3: Bangladesh Rice Production and Population**

| Year | Rice Production (M tonnes, milled) | Population (M) | Per Capita Production (kg) | Food Security Status |
|------|-----------------------------------|----------------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| 1971 | 10.6 | 70.9 | 149 | Massive deficit, dependent on imports/aid |
| 1990 | 26.8 | 108.0 | 248 | Still importing substantial amounts |
| 2000 | 37.6 | 129.6 | 290 | Approaching self-sufficiency |
| 2010 | 50.1 | 148.7 | 337 | Self-sufficiency achieved (~2012) |
| 2020 | 54.9 | 167.4 | 328 | Surplus production |
| 2023 | 55.0+ (paddy: ~39M milled) | ~172 | ~320+ | Third-largest rice producer globally |

*Sources: Wikipedia (rice production table), IRRI, BBS.*

Bangladesh's rice output has **more than quintupled** since 1971, while population has merely doubled (+145%). The country went from being described by Cambridge economist Austin Robinson in 1973 as "the textbook example of Malthusian stagnation" to achieving **self-sufficiency in its staple food** by 2012. This is arguably the country's greatest developmental achievement.

### 9. Employment and Rural Livelihoods

Despite the decline in GDP share, agriculture remains the **single largest employer** in Bangladesh:

- **~44% of the labour force** operates in agriculture (2025: ~35 million workers)
- **48% of rural employment** comes from rice cultivation alone (IRRI)
- **16.5 million farming families** depend on agriculture for their primary livelihood
- Agriculture provides the **primary income source** for an estimated 60-70% of the rural population
- The sector acts as a **social safety net of last resort** -- when urban jobs disappear (as during COVID-19), workers return to family farms

### 10. Poverty Reduction -- Agriculture's Disproportionate Impact

A growing body of evidence demonstrates that **agricultural growth reduces poverty more effectively than non-agricultural growth**, because the poor are disproportionately concentrated in agriculture.

**Table 4: Growth Elasticity of Poverty -- Agriculture vs Non-Agriculture (Bangladesh)**

| Period | Agricultural Growth Elasticity | Non-Agricultural Elasticity | Ratio (Ag/Non-Ag) |
|--------|------------------------------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| 2016-2022 | -2.26 | -0.72 | **3.14x** |
| 2005-2010 | -1.82 | -0.65 | 2.80x |

*Source: Ahmed (2024), IFPRI-Bangladesh, using BBS HIES data.*

**Interpretation:** A 10% increase in per capita agricultural GDP is associated with a **22.6% reduction in poverty** (headcount rate), whereas the same 10% increase in non-agricultural GDP reduces poverty by only **7.2%**. Agricultural growth is **more than three times as effective** at reducing poverty as growth in other sectors.

The reasons are intuitive:
- The poor own few assets and rely primarily on agricultural labour and smallholder farming
- Agricultural growth raises wages for landless labourers (the poorest group)
- Higher agricultural output reduces food prices, benefiting net food buyers (most poor households)
- Agricultural growth creates multiplier effects in the rural non-farm economy

### 11. Forward and Backward Linkages to Industry

Agriculture supplies essential **raw materials** to several major industrial sectors:

| Industrial Sector | Agricultural Raw Material Source | Significance |
|-------------------|--------------------------------|--------------|
| **Textiles & RMG** | Cotton (imported), jute fibre | Jute sector employs ~1M workers |
| **Leather & Footwear** | Livestock (hides, skins) | 3% of global leather market; ~$1B exports |
| **Agro-processing** | Vegetables, fruits, cereals, fish | 1,000+ processors, 250 exporters |
| **Poultry & Feed** | Maize, soybean | Rapidly growing sector, 16.6% export growth p.a. |
| **Frozen Food** | Shrimp, fish, vegetables | $329M domestic market (2025), growing at 7% CAGR |
| **Pharmaceuticals** | Agricultural raw materials | 98% of domestic demand met locally |

**Backward linkages** are equally important: agriculture creates demand for fertiliser (domestic urea plants), pesticides, machinery (power tillers, irrigation pumps), seed production, and financial services (agricultural credit).

### 13. Synthesis and Concluding Analysis

The narrative of "agriculture in decline" is misleading if it stops at GDP share. Bangladesh's agriculture has undergone a **textbook structural transformation** in the following sense:

1. **Declining GDP share** -- from ~60% (1971) to ~11% (2024) -- is exactly what development theory predicts and is a sign of **successful economic diversification**, not failure.
2. **But the transition is incomplete**: ~44% of workers remain in agriculture, producing only 11% of GDP. This implies a **large and persistent labour productivity gap** -- the central challenge of Bangladesh's next phase of development.
3. **The sector itself has been transformed**: Green Revolution technology, crop diversification, mechanisation, climate adaptation, and institutional innovations have made it far more productive than in 1971.
4. **Agriculture still powers food security, employment, poverty reduction, and export diversification** -- it is far from a "sunset sector."

The key policy challenge going forward is to **raise agricultural labour productivity** so that employment share can fall toward GDP share, releasing labour for higher-productivity manufacturing and services -- completing the Lewis transformation that the Clark-Fisher hypothesis predicts.

### 14. Exam Tips

**Structure your answer as follows:**
- **Part A (6 marks):** State the stylised fact clearly. Give the data: 60% GDP share (1971) to 11% (2023). Employment share: 80% to 44%. Cite Clark-Fisher and Lewis as theoretical frameworks. Note the productivity gap puzzle.
- **Part B (6 marks):** Four sub-themes -- Green Revolution (HYV seeds, irrigation, fertiliser, mechanisation); crop diversification (rice to maize-veg-fish-livestock); institutional change (BRRI, credit, extension, subsidies); climate adaptation (salt-tolerant, flood-tolerant rice). Use a table for efficiency.
- **Part C (6 marks):** Five sub-themes -- food security (rice: 10.6M to 55M tonnes); employment (44% of labour force); poverty reduction (agricultural growth elasticity = -2.26, 3x non-agricultural); industrial linkages (jute, leather, agro-processing, frozen food); export earnings ($1B+ agro-exports). End with synthesis.
- **Synthesis (2 marks):** Show that declining GDP share is normal; the real story is transformation + continued significance.

**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Confusing "declining share of GDP" with "declining sector" -- the sector grew in absolute terms throughout.
- Omitting specific data points -- the exam expects numbers (60%, 11%, 44%, 10.6M tonnes, 55M tonnes, -2.26 elasticity).
- Describing Bangladesh as having a "pre-modern" agriculture -- it has modernised substantially; make this clear.
- Treating the Green Revolution as only a historical event -- its institutional legacy (BRRI, BADC, DAE) continues to shape the sector.
- Forgetting the social safety net function -- this is particularly Bangladesh-specific and impressive.

### 15. Professor's Corner

> This question tests your ability to hold **two seemingly contradictory facts in mind simultaneously**:
>
> 1. Agriculture's GDP share has fallen dramatically (the "stylised fact").
> 2. The sector remains profoundly important (the "transition + continued contribution" twist).
>
> The best answers will not treat these as contradictory but as **two sides of the same process of structural transformation**. Bangladesh is following a path that every industrialised economy has walked before -- the share of agriculture in both output and employment declines as development proceeds.
>
> What makes Bangladesh's case worth studying is:
>
> **a) The speed of the GDP-share decline.** From 60% to 11% in five decades is rapid by historical standards. Britain took over a century to see a similar shift.
>
> **b) The stickiness of the employment share.** At 44% employment share with 11% GDP share, Bangladesh has one of the largest agricultural productivity gaps in the developing world. This suggests either (i) manufacturing and services have not grown fast enough to absorb surplus labour, or (ii) the agricultural labour force is underemployed rather than productively employed, or (iii) both.
>
> **c) The self-sufficiency narrative is genuinely impressive.** Bangladesh is perhaps the only case in modern history where a densely populated, resource-poor, climate-vulnerable country has achieved staple-food self-sufficiency starting from a position of acute deficit. Austin Robinson's "Malthusian nightmare" prediction has been spectacularly refuted.
>
> **d) The agricultural poverty elasticity finding (3x non-agricultural)** is now being replicated in other developing countries (India, Ethiopia, Vietnam) and has major policy implications. It implies that **public investment in agriculture is an efficient poverty-reduction strategy**, even as the sector's GDP share shrinks. This is a non-trivial finding: it challenges the notion that development policy should simply "let agriculture shrink" and focus on industry.
>
> **e) The feminisation of agriculture** is an important subtext not directly asked in this question but highly relevant. As men migrate to cities and to international labour markets (Gulf states, Malaysia), women are increasingly the primary agricultural labour force. This has implications for productivity (women have less access to credit, land, extension), for poverty, and for gender equity. See the separate answer on [feminisation of agriculture](./T1Q4-feminization-agriculture-barriers.md).
>
> **f) Climate vulnerability remains the biggest threat.** Much of Bangladesh's agricultural progress could be undone by sea-level rise, increased flooding, and more frequent cyclones. The development of climate-resilient varieties is a race against time.
>
