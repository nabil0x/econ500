## 1. Why Bangladesh's Macro Story Matters

Bangladesh presents a **fascinating case for a foreign audience** because it defies conventional development expectations:

- A country that was classified as a **"basket case"** by Henry Kissinger in 1971 is now one of the world's fastest-growing economies.
- Sustained **6%+ GDP growth** for two decades, lifting 25+ million out of poverty.
- Yet the growth model faces **structural limits** — extreme export concentration, low fiscal capacity, incomplete structural transformation.
- LDC graduation in 2026 marks a **turning point** — the end of the old model and the beginning of a new, uncertain phase.

## 2. Six Indicator Clusters for a Foreign Audience

| Cluster | Indicators | Why This Matters for a Foreign Audience |
|---------|-----------|----------------------------------------|
| **Growth** | GDP growth rate (%), GDP per capita ($), sectoral composition (agri/industry/services) | Shows overall economic trajectory — the headline story |
| **Employment** | LFPR (overall, male, female), unemployment rate (%), sectoral employment shares (agri/industry/services), NEET, informal employment | Labour markets are the transmission mechanism from growth to welfare — tells you who benefits |
| **Trade** | Trade-to-GDP (%), RMG share of exports (%), tariff rate (%), FDI-to-GDP (%), remittances ($) | Export concentration is the key vulnerability — external shocks matter |
| **Social** | Fertility rate, infant mortality (per 1000), female life expectancy, sex ratio at birth, HCI | Bangladesh's outlier performance — the most distinctive development story |
| **Fiscal** | Revenue-to-GDP (%), fiscal deficit (% of GDP), public debt (% of GDP) | Fiscal constraints limit policy space — can the government respond to shocks? |
| **Monetary** | Inflation rate (%), foreign exchange reserves ($), exchange rate regime, NPL ratio (%) | External and financial sector stability — crisis resilience |

## 3. Growth Story — Trajectory and Structure

### Growth Trajectory

| Period | Average GDP Growth | Key Driver |
|--------|-------------------|------------|
| 1990s | ~4.5% | Agriculture reforms, RMG take-off |
| 2000s | ~5.5% | RMG expansion, remittances, services |
| 2010–2019 | 6.5–7.9% | Domestic demand, infrastructure (Padma Bridge), stable macro |
| 2020 (COVID) | 3.4% | Pandemic shock |
| 2021–2023 | 6–7% | Post-COVID recovery, inflation pressures |
| 2024 (est.) | 4.2% | Post-election adjustment, global inflation |

### Sectoral Composition (2023)

| Sector | Share of GDP | Share of Employment | Productivity Gap |
|--------|-------------|---------------------|------------------|
| Agriculture | ~12% | 45.4% | **Very low productivity** — surplus labour |
| Industry | ~17% | ~20% | Moderate — dominated by RMG |
| Services | ~51% | 37.6% | Low productivity informal services |

> **Key structural insight:** Agriculture employs 45.4% of workers but produces only ~12% of GDP. This is the classic Lewis dual-economy pattern — surplus labour that needs to move to higher-productivity sectors.

## 4. Employment and Structural Transformation

### Labour Force Indicators (2023)

| Indicator | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Labour force participation rate (LFPR) | 61.2% |
| Male LFPR | 80% |
| Female LFPR | 42.77% |
| Unemployment rate (official) | 3.53% |
| Youth unemployment (15–29) | Higher (NEET ~30%) |
| Own-account workers (self-employed) | 50.6% |
| Employment in agriculture | 45.4% |
| Employment in industry | ~17% |
| Employment in services | ~37.6% |

### The Structural Transformation Challenge

- **Incomplete transformation:** Labour moving out of agriculture but into **low-productivity informal services** (street vending, domestic work, rickshaw pulling), not high-productivity manufacturing.
- **Gender gap:** Female LFPR (42.77%) is roughly half the male rate (80%), indicating untapped labour potential.
- **Informality:** 50.6% of workers are own-account (self-employed) — meaning limited formal sector job creation.
- **Understated unemployment:** The official 3.53% rate uses a narrow definition — substantial underemployment exists.

## 5. Trade Dynamics — The Vulnerability

| Indicator | Bangladesh | Vietnam (comparator) | Global Average |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|----------------|
| Trade-to-GDP (2022) | 33.8% | >150% | ~60% |
| RMG share of exports | 86% | 12–15% | — |
| FDI-to-GDP | 0.33% | 4.3% | ~2% |
| Average tariff | 27% | ~6% | 6% |
| Remittances (2024) | ~$24 billion | — | — |

> **The core concern:** Bangladesh is becoming **less trade-open** over time (trade-to-GDP fell from 48.1% in 2012 to 33.8% in 2022), while competitors are becoming more open. Combined with extreme export concentration (86% RMG), this creates a vulnerability to both external shocks and long-term competitiveness erosion.

## 6. Social Progress — The Bangladesh Paradox

| Indicator | Bangladesh | India | Pakistan | Low-Income Country Avg |
|-----------|-----------|-------|----------|----------------------|
| Fertility rate | **2.2** | 2.6 | 3.4 | 3.8 |
| Infant mortality (per 1000) | **37** | 47 | 59 | 54 |
| Female life expectancy | **70 yrs** | 67 yrs | 66 yrs | 63 yrs |
| Sex ratio at birth | **105:100** (normal) | 111:100 (abnormal) | Rising | — |
| Human Capital Index | 0.48 | 0.49 | 0.41 | 0.44 |

This is the **single most distinctive feature** of Bangladesh's development story — and the one most likely to surprise a foreign audience. Bangladesh has achieved First-World social indicators on a Third-World income. See [Naila Kabeer — Positive Outlier](naila-kabeer-positive-outlier.md) for the explanation.

## 7. Fiscal Constraints

| Indicator | Value | Comparison |
|-----------|-------|------------|
| Revenue-to-GDP | ~8–9% | Lowest in South Asia; developed countries: 30–40% |
| Tax-to-GDP | ~8.5% | Low-income avg: ~13%; global avg: ~15% |
| Fiscal deficit | ~5% of GDP | Moderate but constrained by low revenue |
| Public debt | ~35% of GDP | Moderate but rising |
| Development spending | Constrained by revenue | Infrastructure gaps persist |

**The fiscal story:** Bangladesh's revenue-to-GDP ratio is among the **lowest in the world**. The tax base is narrow — most workers are in the informal sector, outside the income tax net. This severely constrains the government's ability to invest in infrastructure, health, education, and social protection. LDC graduation (2026) will further squeeze fiscal space by ending concessional financing.

## 8. Monetary and External Sector

| Indicator | Value (2024 est.) | Trend |
|-----------|-------------------|-------|
| Inflation (CPI) | ~10.5% | Elevated (global food/fuel prices, domestic demand) |
| Foreign exchange reserves | ~$20 billion | Down from $48B (2021) — import cover ~4 months |
| Remittance inflows | ~$24 billion/year | Stable — key external buffer |
| Current account balance | Deficit | Pressure from import costs |
| Exchange rate | Managed float (BDT depreciating) | Pressure from reserve drawdown |
| Non-performing loans | ~27% | Banking sector fragility |

**Key concern:** Foreign exchange reserves have fallen sharply from $48 billion (2021) to ~$20 billion (2024) — enough for only ~4 months of imports. This is a warning signal for external vulnerability.

## 9. Regional Comparisons

### Bangladesh vs Vietnam — The Most Revealing Comparison

| Indicator | Bangladesh | Vietnam |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| GDP growth (2020s avg) | 6–7% | 6–7% |
| GDP per capita (2023) | ~$2,820 | ~$4,300 |
| Trade-to-GDP | 33.8% | >150% |
| FDI-to-GDP | 0.33% | 4.3% |
| Export diversification | RMG 86% | Electronics, textiles, footwear, agriculture |
| FTAs | 1 (Bhutan) | CPTPP, EU-Vietnam, multiple bilateral |

**Lesson:** Vietnam's superior trade integration and diversification explain its higher per capita income and greater resilience. Bangladesh's growth model is domestically driven and — while it has worked — faces limits that Vietnam has already overcome.

### Bangladesh vs Pakistan

| Indicator | Bangladesh | Pakistan |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| GDP per capita (2023) | ~$2,820 | ~$1,500 |
| Fertility rate | 2.2 | 3.4 |
| Foreign exchange reserves | ~$20B | ~$8B |
| RMG exports | ~$47B | ~$2B |

**Contrast:** In 1971, Bangladesh and Pakistan had similar income levels. Bangladesh now has nearly **double the per capita income** and far superior social indicators. The difference is largely attributable to Bangladesh's RMG sector, women's labour force participation, and better social policy (family planning, girls' education).

## 10. Post-LDC Graduation Outlook (2026+)

### Challenges

| Challenge | Impact |
|-----------|--------|
| **Loss of EU GSP preferences** | RMG exports face tariffs of 9–12% in EU |
| **Loss of IDA concessional financing** | Need to borrow from IBRD at higher rates |
| **TRIPS compliance** | Pharmaceuticals — a growing export sector — loses LDC exemption |
| **Competition from lower-cost LDCs** | Ethiopia, Cambodia may attract RMG buyers |

### Opportunities

| Opportunity | Potential |
|------------|-----------|
| **Demographic dividend** | Median age 27.6 — 15–20 year window for accelerated growth |
| **Pharmaceuticals** | $150M exports; potential $1B by 2030 |
| **IT/ICT** | $1.5B exports; 60%+ internet penetration; growing startup ecosystem |
| **Leather and footwear** | $1.2B exports; abundant raw materials |
| **Green manufacturing** | 200+ LEED-certified RMG factories — world's largest green factory stock |

## 11. Exam Tips

### Structure for (5 + 15) Mark Split

**Part A — Selection of Indicators (5 marks):**
- List the six clusters — do not just name indicators, explain *why* each matters.
- Justify each cluster's relevance for a foreign audience.

**Part B — Macro Overview (15 marks):**
- Use the six clusters as a framework, then **tell a story**.
- Suggested narrative arc: "Bangladesh has sustained rapid growth through domestic demand and RMG exports, but structural transformation remains incomplete. The economy faces three binding constraints: export concentration, fiscal limitations, and an unfinished demographic transition. Yet its social achievements — the Bangladesh paradox — are genuinely exceptional."

### Key Data Points to Memorise

| Data Point | Value |
|------------|-------|
| GDP growth (pre-COVID peak) | 7.9% (2019) |
| GDP per capita | ~$2,820 (2023) |
| Trade-to-GDP | 33.8% |
| RMG share of exports | 86% |
| Revenue-to-GDP | ~8–9% |
| Female LFPR | 42.77% |
| Employment in agriculture | 45.4% |
| Remittances | ~$24 billion/year |
| Foreign exchange reserves | ~$20 billion |

### Common Mistakes

- Ignoring the (5+15) mark split — Part A needs a justification, not just a list.
- Presenting data without interpretation — "why this matters" is the key.
- Forgetting the Bangladesh paradox — this is the most distinctive part of the story.
- Overlooking post-LDC graduation challenges.
- Presenting an uncritical picture — acknowledge the vulnerabilities.

## 12. Professor's Corner

> The macroeconomic overview question tests your ability to **synthesise** multiple course themes (growth theory, trade, labour economics, social development) into a coherent country assessment. It rewards students who can move beyond description to **analysis**.
>
> **The three analytical layers an A-grade answer should demonstrate:**
>
> 1. **Growth model diagnosis**: Bangladesh has a domestic-demand-led growth model (consumption ~70% of GDP) powered by remittances and RMG exports. This differs from the East Asian export-led model and has different structural implications (less FDI dependence, less trade integration, more sustainability questions).
>
> 2. **Structural transformation assessment**: Use the Lewis model lens — surplus labour in agriculture, low-productivity services absorbing labour, limited formal manufacturing growth beyond RMG. The question of whether Bangladesh will complete its structural transformation or fall into premature deindustrialisation is open.
>
> 3. **The Bangladesh paradox as a macro variable**: Social progress is not just a welfare outcome — it is a **productive asset**. Healthy, educated women entering the labour force powered RMG competitiveness and fertility decline. The social indicators are not separate from the growth story; they are part of it.
