## A. Comprador Groups (06 marks)

### Definition

The **comprador group** (from Portuguese *comprador* = "buyer") refers to a domestic elite class in developing countries that serves as an intermediary for foreign capital. Todaro & Smith define comprador groups as "local elites who act as fronts for foreign investors" (p. 132). These are **not** passive victims of external exploitation — they are active collaborators whose class interests align with foreign capital against national development.

### Composition

The comprador group includes:
- Import-export merchants and trading intermediaries
- Local partners and managers of multinational corporations (MNCs)
- Political elites and military rulers who facilitate foreign investment
- Landlords who supply export-oriented agricultural commodities
- Salaried public officials who benefit from foreign aid and IFI contracts
- Domestic entrepreneurs whose profits derive from MNC supply chains

### Role in Dependency Theory

Within the **neocolonial dependence model** (Frank 1967, Dos Santos 1970), the comprador class is a critical mechanism of external control:

1. **Interest alignment** — Their wealth depends on maintaining the dependent relationship, not on autonomous national industrialisation. They profit from commissions on foreign trade, imported luxury goods, and positions as local managers of foreign firms.

2. **Blocking reform** — They actively resist policies that would reduce external dependence: trade protection, capital controls, industrial policy, land reform, or progressive taxation. Any genuine reform effort that threatens the international capitalist system of inequality is inhibited by their domestic political power.

3. **Surplus extraction channel** — They facilitate the outflow of economic surplus from the periphery to the centre, operating as the local transmission belt for unequal exchange, profit repatriation, and debt servicing.

### Historical Roots

The term originates from colonial Asia (especially China and Portuguese colonies), where local agents facilitated European colonial trade. After political independence, the comprador structure persisted because:
- The inherited export-oriented economy required intermediaries
- The new national elite inherited colonial trade networks
- IFI loans and foreign aid created new channels of dependency

### Contemporary Relevance (Bangladesh Context)

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| RMG intermediaries | Local agents who link garment factories to foreign buyers, benefiting from the export-orientation without investing in backward linkages |
| Import-export merchants | Those who profit from Bangladesh's high import dependence (capital machinery, intermediate goods, fuels) |
| Political elites | Those connected to foreign aid flows and IFI-negotiated projects |
| MNC local partners | Agents of pharmaceutical, mining, and telecom multinationals |

### Why Comprador Group Matters for Development

The existence of a comprador class explains why dependent countries struggle to pursue autonomous development — the local elite has a **vested interest in the status quo**. They will oppose structural transformation even when it would benefit the broader population. This is the domestic face of neocolonial control.

## B. Neocolonial Dependence Model — Explaining Underdevelopment (14 marks)

### Core Thesis

The **Neocolonial Dependence Model** is an indirect outgrowth of Marxist thinking (Todaro & Smith, Ch. 3). It does **not** view underdevelopment as an original condition, a temporary lag, or a stage that all countries pass through. Instead:

> **Underdevelopment is actively produced and maintained** by the historical evolution of a highly unequal international capitalist system.

Andre Gunder Frank's famous formulation — **"the development of underdevelopment"** — captures this precisely: the same historical process that developed the core (centre) simultaneously underdeveloped the periphery.

### The Centre-Periphery Structure

| | Centre (Core) | Periphery |
|---|---|---|
| **Examples** | US, Western Europe, Japan | Africa, Latin America, South Asia |
| **Economic structure** | Diversified industrial economy | Primary commodity production |
| **Technological position** | Innovation and R&D | Technology adoption (obsolete) |
| **Capital** | Capital exporter | Capital importer/debtor |
| **Power** | Dominant in global institutions | Subordinate, policy-taker |

The world economy is structurally bifurcated: the centre develops **through self-impulsion** (endogenous growth driven by its own technological and financial dynamism), while the periphery can **only expand as a reflection** of expansion in the dominant countries (Dos Santos, 1970).

> **Dos Santos (1970) — the definitive statement:**
> *"Underdevelopment, far from constituting a state of backwardness prior to capitalism, is rather a consequence and a particular form of capitalist development known as dependent capitalism.… Dependence is a conditioning situation in which the economies of one group of countries are conditioned by the development and expansion of others. A relationship of interdependence… becomes a dependent relationship when some countries can expand through self-impulsion while others, being in a dependent position, can only expand as a reflection of the expansion of the dominant countries."*

---

### Mechanisms of Surplus Extraction (How Underdevelopment is Produced)

The model identifies **five interconnected mechanisms** through which the centre extracts surplus from the periphery, perpetuating underdevelopment:

#### 1. Unequal Exchange (Prebisch-Singer Thesis)

- Periphery exports **primary commodities** (agriculture, minerals, oil) with declining terms of trade
- Imports **manufactured goods** with rising relative prices
- Result: the periphery must export **more and more** to import the **same quantity** of manufactures
- The productivity gains from technical progress in the centre are captured as higher wages/profits; in the periphery, they are passed on as **lower prices** (export earnings fall)

#### 2. Profit Repatriation by Multinational Corporations

- MNCs establish subsidiaries in the periphery to access cheap labour and raw materials
- Profits are repatriated to the home country, not reinvested in the host economy
- This represents a net outflow of capital from the periphery to the centre
- The "branch-plant" economy: peripheral countries host low-value-added segments of global value chains, while R&D, design, and marketing remain in the centre

#### 3. Technology Dependency

- The periphery relies on the centre for **all** advanced technology
- Pays royalties, licensing fees, and patent rents
- Receives **obsolete** or adapted technology, never frontier innovation
- The centre uses **intellectual property regimes** (TRIPS, WTO) to lock in this advantage
- Result: the periphery cannot develop indigenous technological capability — it is locked into a perpetual follower position

#### 4. Debt and IFI Conditionality

- IMF and World Bank loans create a cycle of **debt dependency**
- Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs) impose policies that:
  - Open peripheral economies to foreign imports (destroying local industry)
  - Remove subsidies and protective tariffs
  - Privatise state enterprises (often purchased by foreign capital)
  - Prioritise debt repayment over social spending
- Conditionality ensures peripheral governments maintain policies favourable to centre capital, even at the cost of domestic welfare

#### 5. The Comprador Class (Domestic Transmission Belt)

- Already discussed in Part A: local elites who benefit from dependency
- They enforce the system domestically — resisting reforms, maintaining openness to foreign capital, suppressing nationalist economic policies
- Without this domestic ally, neocolonial control would be far more difficult to sustain

### Historical Formation: Colonial Origins → Neocolonial Continuity

#### Colonial Period (Direct Control)

- Colonial powers **deliberately created** extractive, commodity-exporting economies
- Infrastructure (railways, ports) was built to **export raw materials**, not to integrate domestic markets
- Administrative, legal, and educational systems served colonial interests
- No industrialisation was permitted — colonies were markets for metropolitan manufactures

#### Independence (Political Sovereignty without Economic Autonomy)

- Political independence transferred flag and anthem, but:
  - The **economic structure** remained unchanged (commodity exporter)
  - **Trade patterns** continued to link the former colony to the former coloniser
  - **Currency and financial systems** remained tied to the centre
  - The **new elite** inherited the comprador role

#### Neocolonial Control (Contemporary Mechanisms)

The model argues that colonialism was replaced by **neocolonialism** — indirect control through:

| Channel | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| MNCs | Control strategic sectors (oil, mining, pharmaceuticals, telecoms) |
| IFIs | IMF/World Bank impose policy frameworks via conditionality |
| Trade regimes | WTO rules favour established industrial powers (tariff escalation, TRIPS, agricultural subsidies) |
| Technology patents | IP regimes prevent local technological learning |
| Unequal voting | IMF/World Bank voting shares give centre countries veto power |
| Cultural/education | Training of elite in Western universities instils neoclassical orthodoxy |

---

### The Continuance of Underdevelopment: Why It Persists

The model offers a structural explanation for **persistence** — underdevelopment is not accidental or temporary:

#### 1. Mutual Reinforcement of Mechanisms

The five mechanisms (unequal exchange, profit repatriation, technology dependency, IFI conditionality, comprador class) are **mutually reinforcing**. Technology dependency ensures the periphery cannot diversify; lack of diversification perpetuates unequal exchange; debt servicing requires foreign exchange, which requires more commodity exports; the comprador class blocks industrial policy that might break the cycle.

#### 2. The Interests Served

The system persists because it serves the interests of:
- **Centre countries**: Cheap raw materials, profitable investment outlets, markets for manufactures, geopolitical leverage
- **Centre-based firms**: MNCs extract super-profits; banks earn interest on sovereign debt; technology firms collect patent rents
- **Comprador elites**: They gain wealth, status, and political power from the dependent relationship

#### 3. Blocked Transformation

Any attempt at autonomous development faces overwhelming opposition:
- **External**: IFI loan conditionality, capital flight threats, diplomatic pressure, trade sanctions, even military intervention (historical examples: Chile 1973, Nicaragua 1980s)
- **Internal**: Comprador elites sabotage reforms through capital flight, hoarding, political destabilisation

#### 4. The "Development of Underdevelopment"

Frank's key insight: underdevelopment is not **non-development** (the absence of development) but a **positive condition produced by the same historical process** that generated development elsewhere. The periphery is not "behind" — it is structurally impoverished by its position in the global system.

---

### Critiques of the Model (For Balance — Professor's Corner)

The neocolonial dependence model has been criticised for:

1. **Determinism** — Leaves little room for successful catch-up (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, now Vietnam and Bangladesh)
2. **Empirical counter-examples** — Some countries escaped the periphery through strategic state intervention
3. **Blaming external factors** — Underplays domestic causes of underdevelopment (bad governance, corruption, civil conflict)
4. **Policy nihilism** — If external factors determine everything, what can poor countries do?
5. **East Asian success** — South Korea and Taiwan had higher per-capita income than most of Africa in 1960 but grew rapidly through state-led industrialisation, export promotion, and land reform — policies that *defied* neoclassical orthodoxy

**Model's defence:** Success cases achieved growth by *defying* neocolonial prescriptions — strategic state intervention, controlled capital accounts, infant industry protection, land reform. These were not free-market successes but **autonomous development against the grain** of neocolonial pressures.
