## 1. Coordination Failure: Definition and Core Concept

Coordination failure is a situation in which the inability of economic agents to coordinate their behaviour (choices) leads to an outcome (equilibrium) that leaves all agents worse off than in an alternative situation that is also an equilibrium (Todaro & Smith). This may occur even when all agents are fully informed about the preferred alternative equilibrium — they simply cannot get there because of difficulties of coordination, sometimes because people hold different expectations and sometimes because everyone is better off waiting for someone else to make the first move.

The concept is rooted in the presence of **complementarities**. A complementarity arises when one agent's choice of an action increases the incentive for other agents to take that same sort of action (Ray, Ch. 5). In the growth context, one firm's accumulation of capital might raise another firm's productivity, which encourages further accumulation. When complementarities are pervasive, they create the **possibility of multiple equilibria** — the same economy could find itself in two (or more) very different states of development, each of which is self-reinforcing.

### 1.1 Coordination Failure vs Other Market Failures

| Concept | Nature | Key Feature |
|---------|--------|-------------|
| **Coordination failure** | Agents cannot coordinate to reach a superior equilibrium | Multiple equilibria exist, Pareto-ranked; no incentive to defect once at an equilibrium |
| **Prisoners' dilemma** | Individual incentives lead to a unique, inferior outcome | Only one Nash equilibrium; defection incentive exists even if cooperation is achieved |
| **Where-to-meet dilemma** | All parties want to coordinate but lack a mechanism | No incentive to cheat once coordinated; pure coordination problem |

Source: Todaro & Smith, Ch. 4; Ray, Ch. 5.

## 2. Multiple Equilibria and the Poverty Trap

The central insight of the coordination failure approach is that an economy can be **stuck in a "bad" equilibrium** (a **poverty trap** or **low-level equilibrium trap**) despite the existence of a "good" equilibrium that is potentially reachable. This is not because the bad equilibrium is a mistake — it is a self-sustaining outcome in which all agents are optimising given the choices of others. The problem is structural: individual rationality does not aggregate to social optimality.

### 2.1 The Rosenstein-Rodan Shoe Factory Parable

The classic illustration comes from Rosenstein-Rodan (1943) via the parable of the shoe factory (Ray, Ch. 5.2.2):

- Imagine a region where output must be sold **within the region** (no exports).
- A giant shoe factory produces USD 1 million of shoes, creating USD 1 million of income in wages, rents, and profits.
- **The problem:** The shoe factory cannot survive alone. The recipients of the new income will not spend all of it on shoes — they want food, clothing, and other goods too. The shoe factory's output cannot be fully absorbed.
- **The solution:** If enterprises are set up in the **correct ratios** (matching consumer expenditure shares — say 50:30:20 for food:clothing:shoes), the conjunction of all three enterprises would be **jointly viable** in a way that each individual enterprise was not.
- **The coordination problem:** No single entrepreneur is large enough to invest in more than one enterprise. Each entrepreneur invests **only if they believe others will invest too**. In the absence of such optimistic beliefs, no one invests, and the region remains underdeveloped.

Thus the parable yields **two equilibria**:
1. A **bad equilibrium**: zero investment, the region remains stagnant.
2. A **good equilibrium**: all three enterprises invest in the correct proportions, output and income are high, and the economy prospers.

Whether the coordinated equilibrium arises depends on **expectations** held by each entrepreneur about the others. Expectations driven by past history can lock a region into stagnation even when another region with identical fundamentals flourishes.

### 2.2 Why the Bad Equilibrium Persists: Complementarities and Circular Causation

The mechanism that creates the poverty trap is **circular causation**:

```
Low investment by others  -->  Low demand for my product  -->  Low incentive for me to invest
                                         ^                          |
                                         |                          |
                                         +---  Low demand for their products <---+
```

Each agent's inaction reinforces every other agent's inaction. This is the hallmark of a coordination failure: the outcome is internally consistent and individually rational, but socially suboptimal.

### 3.2 Interpreting the Diagram

**The axes:**
- **X-axis:** Expected decision by other agents (e.g., the average level of investment that a firm expects all other firms to undertake).
- **Y-axis:** Privately rational decision for an individual agent (e.g., the profit-maximising level of investment for a single firm).

**The S-shaped curve:** Represents the "privately rational decision function" — how much a representative agent chooses to invest, given what they expect others to invest. The shape reflects the nature of complementarities:
- **Initially flat (shallow slope):** When very few others invest, each agent is isolated — spillovers are minimal, so increasing others' investment by a small amount has little effect on the individual's incentive.
- **Then steep (increasing slope):** After a threshold of agents invest, a **snowball effect** occurs. Many agents begin to provide spillover benefits to neighbouring agents, so the curve climbs rapidly — the "take-off" region.
- **Finally flattening:** After most potential investors have been positively affected and the most important gains have been realised, the rate of increase slows.

**The 45-degree line (dashed):** Represents points where actual investment equals expected investment — i.e., where expectations are fulfilled. Equilibria occur where the S-shaped curve crosses this line.

**The three equilibrium points:**
- **D1 (stable low equilibrium / poverty trap):** At the leftmost intersection. Here, agents expect very low investment from others and respond with very low investment themselves — a self-fulfilling prophecy. The economy is stuck in a **poverty trap**.
- **D2 (unstable equilibrium / the threshold):** The middle intersection, where the S-shaped curve cuts the 45-degree line **from below**. This is the **critical threshold** or "valley" that creates the coordination problem. It is unstable because a small perturbation to the left pushes the economy back to D1, while a small perturbation to the right pushes it toward D3.
- **D3 (stable high equilibrium):** The rightmost intersection. Here, agents expect high investment from others, respond in kind, and the economy enjoys high output and welfare. This is the **desirable equilibrium** that is Pareto-superior to D1.

**Stability condition:** In each of the two stable equilibria (D1 and D3), the S-shaped function cuts the 45-degree line **from above** — a hallmark of a stable equilibrium. At D2, the function cuts **from below**, signalling instability (Todaro & Smith).

### 3.3 The "Valley" / Threshold and the Coordination Problem

The region between D1 and D2 represents the "valley" that traps the economy. To move from D1 (low equilibrium) to D3 (high equilibrium), the economy must somehow be pushed beyond D2, the critical threshold. The difficulty is:

- **Individual agents cannot do this alone.** A single firm that invests more when others do not will lose money (demand is insufficient, costs are too high).
- **The market, left to itself, will not do this either.** The low equilibrium is stable — there is no automatic market mechanism that moves the economy to the superior equilibrium.
- **What is required is a coordinated push** — simultaneous investment by many sectors/agents — that crosses the threshold and allows the economy to tip into the high-investment regime.

This is precisely the **coordination failure**: the economy is trapped at D1 despite the existence of D3, which all agents would prefer.

## 4. The Big Push: Rosenstein-Rodan (1943)

Paul Rosenstein-Rodan introduced the idea of the **big push** — a policy that simultaneously creates a coordinated investment in many different sectors of the economy, sufficient to overcome the threshold and shift the economy from D1 to D3 (Ray, Ch. 5.2.3).

### 4.1 Key Features of the Big Push

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Massive investment** | Requires a large (presumably public) investment in many sectors simultaneously |
| **Sectoral allocation** | Needs accurate knowledge of consumer expenditure shares to get the investment proportions right |
| **Joint viability** | No single investment is profitable alone; the **whole package** is profitable |
| **Self-sustaining after the push** | Once D3 is reached, the good equilibrium is self-sustaining; the policy need not continue |

### 4.2 The Big Push Mechanism

In the Murphy-Shleifer-Vishny (1989) formalisation:

1. The economy has a **traditional sector** (constant returns, low productivity, wage = 1) and a **modern sector** (increasing returns, higher productivity, wage W > 1).
2. Modern-sector production requires a **fixed cost** (a minimum of F workers) — this creates increasing returns because average cost declines as output expands.
3. The economy produces N goods; each good receives an equal share (1/N) of national income.
4. If only **one** firm industrialises, the demand from its own workers is insufficient to absorb its output — the firm makes losses.
5. If **many** firms industrialise **simultaneously**, the wage income of all modern-sector workers creates sufficient aggregate demand for each firm's output — all firms become profitable.
6. **Result:** Two equilibria — no industrialisation (D1) or full industrialisation (D3) — with a coordination problem in between.

The model demonstrates formally that coordination failure can prevent industrialisation even when all agents know that the high-investment equilibrium exists and would make them all better off.

### 4.3 Diagram of the Big Push (Todaro & Smith, Fig. 4.2)

```
    Output          Modern-sector
    per good        production function
      ^                 (increasing returns)
      |                 /
      |                / W3
      |               /  
      |              /   W2
      |             /    |
      |            /     |           Traditional-sector
      |           /      W1          production function
      |          /       |           (constant returns)
      |         /        |
      |        /         |
      |       /          |
      |      /           |
      |     /            |
      |    /             |
      |   /              |
      |  /               |
      | /                |
      |/                 |
      +-----------------+------------------------>
                         F                   Labor
                                 L/N
```

**Figure 2: The Big Push Model** (after Todaro & Smith, Fig. 4.2; Murphy-Shleifer-Vishny, 1989)

**Explanation:**
- The modern-sector production function has an intercept at F (the fixed labour cost) and then slopes upward more steeply than the traditional-sector line (higher marginal productivity).
- At low levels of labour (below F), the modern firm cannot produce at all — the fixed cost is a barrier.
- A single modern firm entering alone produces at point A (low output, Q1), insufficient to cover costs.
- With simultaneous industrialisation across many sectors, the economy reaches point B (high output, Q2), where modern-sector production dominates.
- The wage differentials W1, W2, W3 represent increasing wages as more sectors industrialise and compete for labour.

## 5. Key Theorists and Their Contributions

### 5.1 Paul Rosenstein-Rodan (1943)

- **Seminal paper:** "Problems of Industrialisation of Eastern and South-Eastern Europe," *Economic Journal* (1943).
- **Core idea:** Industrialisation requires simultaneous, coordinated investment across multiple sectors because of demand complementarities.
- **Origin of the "big push" concept:** A large-scale, public-policy-led investment programme to overcome coordination failure.
- **Context:** Post-war European reconstruction; argued that the Marshall Plan should fund a coordinated industrialisation programme rather than piecemeal investments.

### 5.2 Albert O. Hirschman (1958)

- **Key work:** *The Strategy of Economic Development* (1958).
- **Alternative to the big push:** "Unbalanced growth" — selectively promote key sectors with strong **backward and forward linkages**, and let market forces pull the rest of the economy along.
- **Forward linkage:** One sector lowers the cost of production for another (e.g., cheaper steel reduces shipbuilding costs).
- **Backward linkage:** One sector raises demand for another's output (e.g., steel expansion raises coal demand).
- **Policy implication:** Governments should invest in **leading sectors** with maximum "punch per buck" — the largest number and strongest strength of linkages.

### 5.3 Murphy, Shleifer, and Vishny (1989)

- **Key paper:** "Industrialization and the Big Push," *Journal of Political Economy* (1989).
- **Contribution:** Formalised Rosenstein-Rodan's intuition into a rigorous mathematical model — the first clear demonstration of the formal logic of coordination failure in industrialisation.
- **Key mechanism:** Demand complementarities — the profitability of one modern-sector firm depends on the income generated by other modern-sector firms.
- **Role of wages:** If modern-sector wages exceed traditional-sector wages, the demand externality is amplified because industrialisation increases the overall income of workers, raising demand for all modern-sector goods.
- **Influence:** The paper revived interest in the big push concept and became the classic model of coordination failure in the "new development economics" of the 1990s.

## 6. Sources of Complementarities That Create Multiple Equilibria

Complementarities can arise through several distinct channels, all of which can generate multiple equilibria and coordination failure (Ray, Ch. 5; Todaro & Smith, Ch. 4):

### 6.1 Demand Complementarities

The classic Rosenstein-Rodan channel. When a firm industrialises, it pays wages to workers; those workers spend their income on the products of other firms. The profitability of each firm depends on the spending of workers in **other** firms. This creates a **pecuniary externality** — a spillover effect on revenues that is transmitted through market prices rather than technology.

**Example:** A shoe factory can only survive if there are also food factories and clothing factories whose workers buy shoes. No single factory can generate enough demand for its own product alone.

### 6.2 Skills / Matching Complementarities

Firms will not invest in a location if workers lack the necessary skills; workers will not acquire those skills if there are no firms to employ them. This is a **chicken-and-egg problem** — the complementary investments must occur simultaneously.

**Formal structure (Ray, Fig. 5.4):**
- Two sectors: Old (low-skill, flat return schedule) and New (high-skill, upward-sloping return schedule).
- At the initial allocation (history), the return in Old (r0) exceeds the return in New (rN), even though New is "better" in the long run.
- The economy can be trapped with everyone in Old, even though everyone would be better off if all moved to New — but no individual will move alone because the return in New is too low until a critical mass is reached.

### 6.3 Learning Externalities

When one firm adopts a new technology, it gains experience that may spill over to other firms through labour mobility, imitation, or informal knowledge diffusion. This lowers the cost of adoption for later firms. If too few firms adopt initially, the spillover never materialises, and the economy stays with the old technology.

**Example:** The QWERTY keyboard persisted long after the mechanical typewriters that created the need for it disappeared — because everyone else uses QWERTY, it pays for any individual to do the same (Ray, Ch. 5.2.1). Dvorak might be a superior system, but no one can unilaterally switch.

### 6.4 Infrastructure and Fixed Costs

Many types of infrastructure (railways, ports, power grids) have high fixed costs that can only be recovered if a sufficient number of industrial users exist. But those industrial users will not set up without the infrastructure. This is a classic **coordination problem** that may require a big push.

## 7. Expectations and Equilibrium Selection

An important question is: what determines which equilibrium the economy ends up in? The answer involves the interaction of **history** and **expectations** (Ray, Ch. 5.2.4).

### 7.1 History Dominates When Adjustment Is Slow

When it takes time for returns to adjust (as in the skills example: learning new skills takes years), history exerts a powerful lock-in effect. Even if everyone expects everyone else to move to the new sector, no individual will move immediately — they will wait until the return in the new sector actually exceeds the return in the old one. But if everyone waits, no one moves, and the bad equilibrium persists.

This is the **delay problem** (Ray): "Everybody wants everybody else to go first. There is no solution to the problem." This explains why changing fashions is easier than escaping a coordination failure — fashion innovators **gain** from being first (they are seen as trendsetters), whereas economic first-movers **lose** money.

### 7.2 Expectations Can Overcome History Under Certain Conditions

If there is an advantage to moving early (lower entry costs, better job positions, cheaper housing), then expectations can overcome history. Agents may trade off lower current returns against higher future costs, and if they believe others will also move, a self-fulfilling migration to the good equilibrium can occur.

### 7.3 Policy Implications

| If history dominates | If expectations can overcome history |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------|
| A big push is likely necessary | A focal policy or signal may suffice |
| Government must coordinate massive simultaneous investment | Government can catalyse private coordination |
| Large foreign aid may be needed | A "deep intervention" that shifts expectations may be enough |
| India's heavy-industry-led planning (1950s) | The "Latin American Time" reset campaign (Todaro & Smith, Box 4.1) |

### 7.4 The "Deep Intervention" Concept

A **deep intervention** (Todaro & Smith) is a government policy that can move the economy to a preferred equilibrium or even to a higher permanent rate of growth, which can then be **self-sustaining** so that the policy need no longer be enforced. The once-off character of deep interventions makes government policy potentially very powerful — but also very consequential, because a bad policy could mire an economy in a bad equilibrium for years.

## 9. Exam Tips

**For the 7-mark question:**
- Start with a **clear definition** of coordination failure — the inability to coordinate leads to an inferior equilibrium despite a superior one being available.
- Explain the **mechanism** — complementarities create a situation where each agent's optimal action depends on what others do.
- Use the **Rosenstein-Rodan shoe factory parable** as a concrete illustration.
- **Draw the multiple equilibria diagram** (Figure 1) and explain each element: the S-shaped curve, the 45-degree line, D1 (trap), D2 (threshold), D3 (good equilibrium).
- Explain the **big push** as the policy solution: simultaneous coordination to cross the threshold.
- Mention **Murphy-Shleifer-Vishny (1989)** as the formalisation of the big push intuition.
- Conclude with the **interaction of history and expectations** in determining which equilibrium prevails.

**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Confusing coordination failure with **prisoners' dilemma** — in coordination failure, everyone wants to coordinate; in PD, there is an incentive to cheat.
- Forgetting to explain the **shape of the S-curve** — why it is initially flat, then steep, then flat again.
- Omitting the **instability condition** at D2 (curve cuts 45-degree line from below).
- Neglecting the role of **expectations** in equilibrium selection.
- Treating the big push as always necessary — note Hirschman's alternative (unbalanced growth through linkages).

## 10. Professor's Corner

> Coordination failure theory represents a fundamental challenge to the neoclassical view that markets, left to their own devices, will guide an economy to an efficient outcome. It shows that **multiple equilibria can exist** for the same set of fundamentals, and that which equilibrium prevails depends on history and expectations rather than on underlying endowments or technology alone.
>
> **Key insights for exam purposes:**
>
> 1. **Coordination failure is not the same as market failure in the usual sense.** In standard welfare economics, market failures (externalities, public goods, imperfect competition) create a gap between private and social returns. Coordination failure is broader: it arises even when all agents are rational, fully informed, and pursuing their self-interest, because their rationality is **interdependent** — what is optimal for me depends on what I expect you to do, and vice versa.
>
> 2. **The S-shaped function is the key diagrammatic device** — master it. It captures the idea that complementarities are weak at low levels of participation (everyone is isolated), strong at intermediate levels (the snowball/take-off effect), and saturated at high levels. The threshold D2 is where the snowball effect begins to dominate.
>
> 3. **The big push vs unbalanced growth debate** is a classic exam tension. Rosenstein-Rodan argued for simultaneous balanced investment; Hirschman countered that governments lack the information to plan such a push and should instead rely on linkage-driven unbalanced growth. These are not necessarily contradictory — the right approach depends on the specific configuration of the economy, the strength of linkages, and the government's informational and administrative capacity.
>
> 4. **The Murphy-Shleifer-Vishny model (1989) revived the big push concept** by providing formal microfoundations. They showed that with increasing returns, monopolistic competition, and demand complementarities, multiple equilibria arise naturally. The formal model matters for exams because it demonstrates that the intuition is **rigorous**, not merely metaphorical.
>
> 5. **Empirical relevance:** The coordination failure approach has been applied to explain:
>    - The East Asian miracle (particularly South Korea's coordinated industrial policy)
>    - China's Special Economic Zones (as a big push in selected regions)
>    - The persistence of underdevelopment in Sub-Saharan Africa
>    - The middle-income trap (countries that reach middle-income status but cannot make the transition to high-income innovation-driven growth)
>
> 6. **Connection to other course topics:**
>    - Links to **endogenous growth theory** (complementarities in R&D and human capital accumulation create multiple growth paths)
>    - Links to **false paradigm model** (structuralist critiques of market-led development)
>    - Links to **neoclassical counterrevolution** (scepticism about government's ability to coordinate better than markets)
>    - Links to **institutions** (deep interventions require capable state institutions)
>
> **Must-read references for deeper understanding:**
> - Rosenstein-Rodan, P.N. (1943). Problems of Industrialisation of Eastern and South-Eastern Europe. *Economic Journal*, 53(210), 202–211.
> - Hirschman, A.O. (1958). *The Strategy of Economic Development*. Yale University Press.
> - Murphy, K., Shleifer, A., & Vishny, R. (1989). Industrialization and the Big Push. *Journal of Political Economy*, 97(5), 1003–1026.
> - Ray, D. (1998). *Development Economics*. Princeton University Press. Ch. 5 ("History and Expectations").
> - Todaro, M. & Smith, S. (2020). *Economic Development*, 13th ed. Pearson. Ch. 4 ("Contemporary Models of Development and Underdevelopment").
