## Part A: How does the False Paradigm Model of International Dependence argue against structural models of economic development? (10 marks)

---

### 1. Definition and Theoretical Lineage (2 marks)

The **False Paradigm Model** is a component of the International Dependence Revolution articulated in Todaro & Smith (Chapter 3, Section 3.5.3). It argues that underdevelopment is perpetuated not merely by external economic exploitation (as the neocolonial dependence model holds) but by the **uncritical application of inappropriate Western theoretical frameworks** to developing-country contexts.

The model emerges from the broader dependence school of the 1960s–1970s and is closely associated with **Michael Todaro** himself, alongside structuralist economists such as **Gunnar Myrdal**, **Paul Streeten**, and **Albert Hirschman**. Its distinctive contribution is its focus on **intellectual dependence** — the idea that patterns of thought, rather than patterns of trade or capital flows, can be the primary mechanism of external control.

> **Core proposition:** The most powerful form of neocolonial domination is not military or economic but **intellectual** — the colonisation of the mind through the internalisation of Western economic categories, assumptions, and policy prescriptions.

---

### 2. The Core Argument: Inappropriate Western Models (2.5 marks)

The False Paradigm Model rests on a **three-link causal chain** that explains why structural transformation fails in many developing countries:

```
Western university training of economists
        ↓
Internalisation of models built on Western assumptions
        ↓
Application of these models to structurally different economies
        ↓
Policy failure, wasted resources, and perpetuated underdevelopment
```

#### 2.1 The Training Mechanism

Economists from developing countries who receive postgraduate training in Western universities (or in Western-style institutions within their own countries) learn a body of theory developed to explain the historical experience of Western Europe and North America. These theories carry **implicit assumptions** that are treated as universal but in fact reflect the particular institutional, historical, and structural conditions of industrialised economies:

| Western Assumption | Developing-Country Reality |
|--------------------|----------------------------|
| Full employment of resources | Widespread unemployment and underemployment |
| Perfect information and foresight | Radical uncertainty, information asymmetries |
| Competitive markets with many buyers and sellers | Monopoly/monopsony power, concentrated ownership |
| Elastic supply responses to price signals | Supply constraints (infrastructure, credit, skills) |
| Established legal and institutional frameworks | Weak or dysfunctional institutions, corruption |
| Individualistic utility-maximising behaviour | Extended-family obligations, social networks, patron-client relations |
| Integrated national markets | Fragmented markets with high transaction costs |
| Autonomous national economic policy space | IFI conditionality, MNC leverage, global trade rules |

#### 2.2 The Mechanistic Application

Because these models are learned as **formal, mathematical, and apparently universal** frameworks, returning economists apply them mechanically to their home countries. The False Paradigm Model argues that this application is not a neutral technical exercise but a **political act** — it systematically biases policy in directions that favour the status quo and external interests:

- Models that assume competitive equilibrium recommend **price liberalisation** even when markets are monopolised
- Models that assume flexible labour markets recommend **removing minimum wages** even when labour has no bargaining power
- Models that assume well-functioning capital markets recommend **removing credit controls** even when banks lend only to large firms
- Models that assume comparative advantage works through trade recommend **specialisation in primary commodities** even when the Prebisch-Singer thesis predicts declining terms of trade

---

### 3. Specific Critiques of Structural Models (3 marks)

The False Paradigm Model does not critique Western theory in the abstract — it identifies **specific structural models** that have been harmfully misapplied in developing countries.

#### 3.1 Rostow's Stages of Growth (Linear-Stages Model)

| Aspect | Rostow's Claim | False Paradigm Critique |
|--------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Universal path** | All societies pass through five stages: traditional → preconditions → take-off → maturity → high mass consumption | Assumes a single linear trajectory; ignores that early industrialisers achieved growth through colonialism (extraction from the periphery) and that late developers face fundamentally different external conditions |
| **Take-off prerequisites** | High savings (15%+ of GDP) and investment in leading sectors | Savings rates are endogenous — they depend on income, which depends on growth. The Harrod-Domar circularity: you need savings to grow but need income to save. Foreign capital can fill the gap but creates debt dependency |
| **Demonstration effect** | Developing countries can simply imitate the path | The same path is no longer available — the global economy has changed (protection of intellectual property, WTO rules, climate constraints, monopolised global value chains) |

#### 3.2 Lewis Dual-Sector Model (Structural-Change Model)

| Aspect | Lewis Assumption | False Paradigm Critique |
|--------|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Unlimited labour supply** | Traditional sector has surplus labour at zero marginal product that can be transferred to the modern sector at a constant institutional wage | (a) Seasonality means rural labour is not surplus year-round — harvest periods face labour shortages. (b) The institutional wage is not constant — urban wages rise due to union pressure, minimum wage legislation, and political factors |
| **Automatic modern-sector absorption** | All surplus labour will eventually be absorbed by the expanding modern sector | In practice, modern-sector growth in many developing countries has been **capital-intensive** (skill-biased technological change) and has created far fewer jobs than the growing labour force. The Lewis turning point recedes indefinitely |
| **Constant urban wage** | The modern sector pays a fixed mark-up over subsistence | Urban wages in many developing countries are **two to four times** rural incomes, creating: (a) rural-urban migration beyond what the labour market can absorb, (b) urban unemployment (the Todaro model), (c) an informal sector that Lewis's binary framework cannot handle |

#### 3.3 Harrod-Domar Growth Model

| Aspect | Harrod-Domar Claim | False Paradigm Critique |
|--------|--------------------|------------------------|
| **Growth formula** | g = s/k (growth = savings rate ÷ capital-output ratio) | Mechanically prescribes raising savings and investment without addressing: (a) **absorptive capacity constraints** — can the economy productively absorb new capital? (b) **sectoral complementarities** — investment in one sector requires coordinated investment in others (the Big Push argument), (c) **institutional prerequisites** — legal systems, property rights, and governance matter as much as capital |
| **Capital fundamentalism** | Capital accumulation is the binding constraint on growth | (a) Many aid-receiving countries had high investment but low growth (the "investment without growth" puzzle). (b) The capital-output ratio is not constant — it rises as diminishing returns set in. (c) Human capital, technology, and institutions are omitted but critical |

#### 3.4 Import Substitution Industrialisation (ISI)

| Aspect | ISI Assumption | False Paradigm Critique |
|--------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Industrialisation via protection** | Protect infant industries behind tariff walls until they become competitive | (a) **Indiscriminate protection** — all domestic production is protected, not just genuinely promising industries. (b) **Perpetual infancy** — without competitive pressure, firms never achieve international efficiency. (c) **Anti-export bias** — overvalued exchange rates and protection make exporting unprofitable. (d) **Capital intensity** — protected industries use imported capital goods (subsidised by overvalued exchange rates), worsening unemployment |
| **Latin American model universalised** | ISI worked for large Latin American economies (Brazil, Argentina, Mexico) and should be adopted everywhere | (a) Small economies (most of Africa, the Caribbean) lack the internal market size to support domestic manufacturing. (b) The state capacity to administer selective protection is assumed but absent in many contexts. (c) ISI was always accompanied by authoritarian political structures — the institutional prerequisites are not transferable |

#### 3.5 Neoclassical Price Liberalisation (The Counterrevolution)

| Aspect | Neoliberal Prescription | False Paradigm Critique |
|--------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Get prices right** | Remove subsidies, tariffs, and price controls; let markets clear | (a) "Getting prices right" assumes competitive markets exist — in many developing countries, markets are thin, fragmented, and dominated by monopolies. Liberalised prices reflect **monopoly power**, not social scarcity. (b) Price liberalisation without institutional reform can lead to **speculation, hoarding, and price volatility** that harms the poor |
| **Trade liberalisation** | Remove trade barriers to realise comparative advantage | (a) Comparative advantage in primary commodities locks countries into low-value, volatile export patterns. (b) **Infant industries** are destroyed before they can mature. (c) The empirical evidence on trade liberalisation and growth is contested — many successful developers (East Asia) used **selective protection** |
| **Privatisation** | State-owned enterprises are inefficient and should be privatised | (a) In the absence of competitive markets, privatisation replaces public monopoly with private monopoly — no efficiency gain. (b) Foreign buyers acquire strategic assets at fire-sale prices (capital flight via asset transfer). (c) Regulatory capacity to oversee privatised industries is assumed but often absent |

---

### 4. Consequences of False Paradigms (1 mark)

The systematic application of inappropriate Western models produces **five categories of harm**:

1. **Policy failure** — Structural adjustment programmes based on neoclassical assumptions led to declining growth, rising inequality, and reduced social spending in Africa and Latin America (the "lost decade" of the 1980s).

2. **Wasted resources** — Scarce foreign exchange and aid budgets allocated to projects justified by faulty models (large dams, capital-intensive industrial plants) that fail to generate expected returns.

3. **Destruction of indigenous institutions** — Traditional mutual insurance networks, community-based resource management, and informal credit systems are displaced by formal institutions designed on Western models that do not function effectively.

4. **Inappropriate technology transfer** — Capital-intensive technologies appropriate for industrialised countries are transferred to labour-abundant economies, worsening unemployment and income inequality.

5. **Reinforced dependency** — When policies based on false paradigms fail, the response is typically to call for more external expertise, more conditional lending, and more Western training — deepening rather than breaking the intellectual dependency.

---

### 5. The Proposed Solution: Context-Specific Development Theory (1 mark)

The False Paradigm Model does not advocate abandoning economic theory altogether. Its prescription is more nuanced:

| Principle | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Context-specific analysis** | Development theory must be grounded in the specific historical, institutional, and structural realities of each country — not deduced from universal axioms |
| **Interdisciplinary approach** | Economics must be complemented by political science, sociology, anthropology, and history to understand development constraints |
| **Listening to local knowledge** | The grassroots, the poor, and the informal sector possess knowledge that formal models miss — participatory development and qualitative research matter |
| **Adapted institutions** | Rather than importing Western institutional forms (legal systems, financial markets, property rights), build on indigenous institutional foundations |
| **Pragmatic eclecticism** | Use the appropriate tool for the context — sometimes the market, sometimes the state, sometimes community-based mechanisms |
| **Endogenous theory-building** | Developing countries must develop their own economic theory rooted in their own experience (the institutionalist tradition of Myrdal, Streeten, and the Latin American structuralists) |

> **The deepest irony of the False Paradigm:** The very concept originates from Todaro himself — a Western-trained economist whose textbook (used in universities worldwide, including in developing countries) has been criticised for perpetuating the paradigm it critiques. This reflexivity is central to understanding the model's enduring relevance.

## Part B: What are the key arguments embraced by the Dualistic Development Thesis? (10 marks)

---

### 1. Definition and Intellectual Origins (1.5 marks)

The **Dualistic Development Thesis** is a foundational concept in structuralist development economics, first systematically articulated by **J.H. Boeke** (1953) in his study of the Indonesian economy under Dutch colonialism. The thesis was later formalised by **Arthur Lewis** (1954), who won the Nobel Prize in Economics for his dual-sector model, and elaborated by **Benjamin Higgins** (1968) and other structuralist economists.

Todaro & Smith (Chapter 3) present dualism as a defining structural characteristic of developing economies: the **coexistence of two fundamentally different economic systems** within a single national boundary — a small, modern, capital-intensive sector and a large, traditional, labour-intensive sector — with **limited interaction** between them.

> **Core proposition:** Developing countries are not simply "poorer versions" of industrialised economies. They are **structurally different** — their economies are segmented into sectors that operate under fundamentally different rules, and this segmentation is itself a primary cause of underdevelopment.

The thesis directly challenges the neoclassical assumption that factor prices equalise across sectors over time, and the linear-stages assumption that growth in one sector automatically diffuses to the other.

---

### 2. The Four Key Arguments (5 marks)

The Dualistic Development Thesis rests on **four interconnected arguments**, each of which describes a distinct dimension of structural segmentation.

#### Argument 1: Coexistence of Two Sectors

The most basic claim of dualism: every developing economy contains **two distinct sectors** operating side by side:

| Dimension | Modern Sector | Traditional Sector |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Location** | Urban, industrial enclaves | Rural, agricultural, and urban informal |
| **Technology** | Capital-intensive, imported, advanced | Labour-intensive, indigenous, rudimentary |
| **Organisation** | Formal firms, wage labour, contracts | Family enterprises, self-employment, customary arrangements |
| **Market orientation** | Export-oriented and domestic formal market | Subsistence and local markets (often non-monetised) |
| **Financing** | Formal banking, FDI, MNC capital | Informal credit, moneylenders, family savings, rotating savings clubs |
| **Labour conditions** | Wage employment, labour laws, union presence | Unpaid family labour, sharecropping, piece-rate, no formal protections |
| **Property rights** | Formal legal title, corporate ownership | Customary tenure, common property, informal claims |
| **State engagement** | Taxed, regulated, subsidised, protected | Largely ignored or actively taxed (via commodity price controls) |

The two sectors are not merely different in degree (one being simply "more advanced" than the other) but **different in kind** — they embody distinct modes of production, social relations, and institutional logics. This is what Boeke called "social dualism": the clash between an imported Western capitalist system and an indigenous pre-capitalist system, each with its own spirit, organisation, and techniques.

**Importantly, the sectors are not static.** The modern sector expands over time at the expense of the traditional sector — but this expansion is uneven, partial, and contested, producing what development economists call **structural heterogeneity** rather than smooth transformation.

#### Argument 2: Central Supremacy of the Modern Sector

The two sectors do not coexist on equal terms. The modern sector exercises **asymmetric dominance** over the traditional sector in multiple dimensions:

**a) Policy bias:** Government policy systematically favours the modern sector:
- Subsidised credit and foreign exchange are allocated to large formal firms
- Infrastructure investment (roads, electricity, ports) prioritises urban-industrial areas
- Tax incentives, export subsidies, and EPZ benefits accrue to modern-sector firms
- Exchange rate overvaluation (to cheapen imported capital goods for industry) taxes agricultural exporters

**b) Financial supremacy:**
- Formal banks direct the vast majority of credit to modern-sector firms (large, collateralised, low-risk borrowers)
- The traditional sector relies on informal credit at interest rates 10–50× higher than formal rates
- Rural savings are channelled through the banking system to urban investment — a net **financial drain** from the traditional to the modern sector

**c) Political supremacy:**
- Modern-sector interests are organised (chambers of commerce, industry associations, labour unions) and have political voice
- The traditional sector is dispersed, unorganised, and politically marginalised
- Policy reforms that hurt the modern sector (devaluation, subsidy removal, tax increases) face organised resistance; reforms that hurt the traditional sector face none

**d) Ideological supremacy:**
- "Modernisation" is equated with industrialisation, urbanisation, and formal-sector expansion
- Traditional-sector activities (subsistence farming, petty trade, artisanal production) are stigmatised as "backward"
- Development policy is implicitly oriented toward **replacing** the traditional sector rather than **improving** it

This supremacy means that even well-intentioned government policies can reinforce dualism. A policy to support "the economy" automatically supports the modern sector, because that is where the data, the data-collection infrastructure, and the political constituency are concentrated.

#### Argument 3: Limited Interaction Between Sectors

The neoclassical expectation is that factor flows (labour, capital, technology) will over time equalise returns across sectors, eliminating dualism. The Dualistic Development Thesis argues that this equalisation does **not** occur because interactions between sectors are systematically limited:

| Type of Interaction | Neoclassical Expectation | Dualistic Reality |
|---------------------|-------------------------|-------------------|
| **Labour mobility** | Workers move freely from low-wage (traditional) to high-wage (modern) sectors until wages equalise | Migration is constrained by skills gaps, housing costs, information asymmetries, social networks, and discrimination. The Todaro model shows that expected (not actual) wage differentials drive migration, and that urban unemployment acts as an equilibrating mechanism — but one that sustains dualism rather than eliminating it |
| **Capital flows** | Capital moves from capital-abundant (modern) to capital-scarce (traditional) sectors where returns are higher | Formal financial institutions rarely lend to traditional-sector activities (high transaction costs, lack of collateral, perceived risk). Capital flows *from* the traditional to the modern sector via financial intermediation and terms of trade |
| **Technology transfer** | Modern-sector technology diffuses to the traditional sector through demonstration effects, labour turnover, and vertical linkages | Imported modern-sector technology is **inappropriate** for traditional-sector conditions (capital-intensive, large-scale, skill-biased). It cannot be easily adapted, and knowledge spillovers are limited by educational barriers |
| **Market integration** | Common price signals coordinate activity across the economy | Traditional and modern sectors respond to different price signals. The modern sector is integrated into global markets; the traditional sector faces local prices, high transport costs, and different relative prices |

**Consequence of limited interaction:** The dual economy does not converge toward a unified equilibrium. Instead, the two sectors can develop along **divergent trajectories** — the modern sector growing rapidly (global demand, FDI, technology) while the traditional sector stagnates or grows slowly (neglect, credit constraints, adverse terms of trade).

#### Argument 4: Lack of Diffusion (Enclave Growth)

The most policy-relevant argument: **growth in the modern sector does not automatically spread to the traditional sector.** This directly contradicts the linear-stages assumption (Rostow: the modern sector's growth will eventually "trickle down") and the neoclassical assumption (factor price equalisation will eventually benefit all).

**Why diffusion fails — five structural barriers:**

1. **Enclave structure:** The modern sector is often an **export-oriented enclave** with limited backward and forward linkages to the domestic economy. Export processing zones (EPZs), mining operations, and plantation agriculture import inputs (capital goods, intermediate inputs, even food for workers) and export output — the "enclave" touches the domestic economy only through wages paid to a small number of workers.

2. **Capital intensity:** Modern-sector technology is capital-intensive and skill-biased. It generates few jobs relative to the size of the labour force, and those jobs require skills that traditional-sector workers do not possess. The employment multiplier is small.

3. **Demonstration effect on consumption, not production:** The main channel of influence from modern to traditional sectors is often through consumption: traditional-sector households observe and aspire to modern-sector consumption patterns (imported goods, processed foods, urban lifestyles). This creates **import dependence** and **wage pressure** without corresponding productivity improvements.

4. **Sectoral segmentation of labour markets:** The labour market is not unified. Modern-sector jobs are often allocated through educational credentials, social networks, and non-market mechanisms. Traditional-sector workers are **rationed out** of modern-sector employment regardless of their willingness to accept lower wages.

5. **Backwash effects (Myrdal, 1957):** Gunnar Myrdal's concept of **cumulative causation** explains how growth in one region/sector can actually **impoverish** other regions/sectors:
   - **Capital drain:** Savings from the traditional sector flow to the modern sector
   - **Brain drain:** The most educated, entrepreneurial, and dynamic workers migrate
   - **Competition for land:** Modern-sector expansion (plantations, industrial zones, real estate) displaces traditional agriculture
   - **Adverse terms of trade:** Modern-sector firms exercise monopsony power in buying traditional-sector outputs (agricultural commodities) and monopoly power in selling inputs

**Diagrammatic summary of dualistic dynamics:**

```
                    MODERN SECTOR                    TRADITIONAL SECTOR
                          │                                 │
    ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐   ┌───────┴────────┐
    │   Capital-intensive │  High productivity   │   │ Labour-intensive│
    │   Export-oriented   │  Formal institutions │   │  Subsistence     │
    │   Global prices     │  Organised labour    │   │  Local prices    │
    └──────────┬──────────┴──────────┬──────────┘   └───────┬────────┘
               │                     │                      │
               │   Limited labour    │   Capital drain ─────┤
               │   absorption        │                      │
               │                     │   Brain drain ───────┤
               │                     │                      │
               │   Limited backward  │   Adverse terms ─────┤
               │   linkages          │   of trade           │
               │                     │                      │
               └─────────────────────┘               Underdevelopment
               Rapid growth (enclave)                 persists/stagnates
```

### 3. The Lewis Model: Formalising Dualism (1.5 marks)

Arthur Lewis's dual-sector model (1954, "Economic Development with Unlimited Supplies of Labour") provides the **formal theoretical foundation** for the Dualistic Development Thesis. The model traces development as a process of **structural transformation** — the movement of labour and surplus from the traditional to the modern sector.

#### 3.1 The Model in Brief

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Traditional sector** | Subsistence agriculture with surplus labour (marginal product of labour = 0 or < subsistence wage). Workers are paid an **institutional wage** equal to average product, not marginal product |
| **Modern sector** | Capitalist industrial sector with profit-maximising firms. Labour is hired at a constant wage (≈ 30–50% above subsistence) |
| **Unlimited labour supply** | At the constant wage, the modern sector can hire as many workers as it wants — the supply curve of labour is horizontal (perfectly elastic) |
| **Surplus reinvestment** | Capitalist profits are reinvested, expanding the modern sector's capital stock, shifting the labour demand curve outward, and absorbing more workers from the traditional sector |
| **Structural transformation** | The process continues until all surplus labour is absorbed, at which point the "Lewis turning point" is reached and wages begin to rise in both sectors |

#### 3.2 What the Lewis Model Explains

- **Rural-urban migration:** The wage differential drives migration from agriculture to industry
- **Suppressed urban wages:** The unlimited labour supply keeps wages low in the modern sector, enabling high profits and rapid capital accumulation
- **Dualistic growth:** The modern sector grows while the traditional sector stagnates — this is not a failure but the **engine of development**
- **The turning point:** Once surplus labour is exhausted, wages rise, income distribution improves, and the economy becomes a unified, developed system

#### 3.3 Limitations and Critiques (from the False Paradigm Model perspective)

The Lewis model itself can be critiqued through the False Paradigm lens:

1. **Surplus labour assumption:** Empirical evidence contradicts the assumption of zero marginal product in agriculture — seasonality creates labour shortages during harvest periods, and removing labour can reduce output (Schultz, 1964)

2. **Constant urban wage:** Urban wages in many developing countries rise **faster** than productivity due to institutional factors (unions, minimum wage laws, civil service pay scales) — the Todaro model incorporates this critique

3. **Capitalist reinvestment assumption:** Lewis assumes capitalists reinvest all profits — in reality, profits may be repatriated (MNCs), spent on luxury consumption (comprador class), or held as foreign assets (capital flight)

4. **Full employment at the turning point:** The model assumes the modern sector absorbs all surplus labour — in practice, modern-sector growth is often too **capital-intensive** to absorb the growing labour force, producing an expanding urban informal sector

5. **Gender blindness:** The model ignores women's work in both sectors — women in the traditional sector (subsistence farming, household production, care work) are invisible in the model, yet their labour dynamics are central to understanding dualism

### 4. Empirical Relevance: The Bangladesh Case (1 mark)

Bangladesh presents one of the most striking contemporary examples of dualistic development:

#### 4.1 The Productivity Gap

| Indicator | Agriculture (Traditional) | Industry & Services (Modern) |
|-----------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Share of GDP** | ~11.3% | ~88.7% |
| **Share of employment** | 45.4% (LFS 2022) | 54.6% |
| **Labour productivity** (value added per worker) | Low (~$1,200/year) | High (~$6,500/year) |
| **Formal-informal split** | Nearly 100% informal | ~60% informal (services); RMG is formal |

The ratio of employment share to GDP share in agriculture (45.4/11.3 ≈ 4.0) reveals that agriculture employs roughly **four times** its share of output — a classic dualistic productivity gap. If agricultural productivity matched the economy-wide average, output would be roughly 4× current levels.

#### 4.2 The RMG Sector as Dualistic Enclave

Bangladesh's ready-made garment (RMG) sector — the second-largest garment exporter globally — exemplifies dualistic dynamics:

| Dualistic Feature | RMG Sector Evidence |
|-------------------|--------------------|
| **Enclave structure** | High import dependence (85%+ of textile inputs are imported); limited backward linkages to domestic agriculture or industry |
| **Limited diffusion** | Despite employing 4+ million workers (mostly women from rural areas), the RMG sector has not catalysed broad-based industrial transformation outside of garments |
| **Capital intensity** | The RMG sector is relatively labour-intensive, but forward/backward linkages (textile mills, dyeing, finishing, fashion design) are more capital-intensive and skill-biased |
| **Labour market segmentation** | RMG workers earn more than agricultural workers but remain in a distinct labour market — low wages, limited bargaining power, gender-based wage discrimination |
| **Urban-rural divide** | Remittances from RMG workers to rural areas do occur, but are relatively small compared to the sector's output and export earnings |

#### 4.3 The Urban Informal Sector

Bangladesh's urban labour force is characterised by a large and growing **informal sector** that complicates the binary dualistic framework:

- **50.6%** of workers are classified as own-account workers (self-employed, no formal employer)
- Urban informal employment includes: rickshaw pullers, street vendors, domestic workers, day labourers, small repair shops
- These workers exist **between** the modern and traditional sectors — they are urban and market-oriented but lack the capital, technology, and institutions of the modern sector
- This **semi-formal or intermediate sector** is increasingly recognised as a third category in dualistic analysis

### 5. Critical Assessment of the Dualistic Development Thesis (1 mark)

#### 5.1 Strengths

| Strength | Explanation |
|----------|-------------|
| **Structural realism** | Captures a fundamental feature of developing economies that neoclassical models miss — market segmentation is not a temporary imperfection but a structural condition |
| **Policy relevance** | Explains why growth often fails to reduce poverty or transform the economy — "trickle-down" is blocked by structural barriers |
| **Empirical fit** | The productivity gap between agriculture and non-agriculture is one of the most robust empirical regularities in development |
| **Historical grounding** | Dualism is rooted in the historical experience of colonisation and the superimposition of capitalist economies on pre-capitalist societies |
| **Interdisciplinary** | Connects economics with sociology, political science, and anthropology in explaining development |

#### 5.2 Critiques

| Critique | Response |
|----------|----------|
| **Too binary** | Reality is a continuum — there are semi-modern, semi-formal, intermediate, and transitional sectors. The formal-informal distinction captures some of this nuance but itself is often vague |
| **Traditional sector not static** | The traditional sector is not backward or stagnant — it innovates, adapts, and responds to incentives (the "efficient but poor" hypothesis). Schultz (1964) showed that peasant farmers are rational optimisers within their constraints |
| **Interdependence underestimated** | Dualism may understate the links between sectors: agriculture provides food and labour to industry; rural savings finance urban investment; rural demand absorbs urban output. Some degree of interdependence is inevitable |
| **Lewis turning point in practice** | Some developing countries (South Korea, Taiwan, China) have passed through the Lewis turning point and achieved unified labour markets — dualism is not permanent if the right policies are followed |
| **Ahistorical and depoliticised** | The dualistic framework can obscure the political economy of dualism — who benefits from maintaining segmentation? Comprador groups, MNCs, and political elites may have stakes in preserving the dual structure |
| **Policy nihilism?** | If the modern sector cannot absorb the traditional sector and diffusion is blocked, what is to be done? The thesis is better at diagnosis than prescription |

#### 5.3 Synthesis: Beyond Dualism

Contemporary development economics has moved beyond the simple binary dualism of Boeke and Lewis toward more nuanced frameworks:

- **Structural transformation** (Timmer, 1988) — focuses on the process and sequencing of sectoral change
- **Informal sector analysis** (ILO, De Soto, Chen) — recognises the complexity and heterogeneity of activities outside the formal sector
- **Global value chain analysis** (Gereffi, Kaplinsky) — traces how developing countries are integrated into the global economy at different nodes
- **Inclusive growth** (World Bank, UNDP) — asks how growth can be structured to benefit all sectors, not just the modern enclave

These approaches retain the core insight of dualism — that developing economies are structurally segmented — while rejecting the overly rigid and static binary that the original thesis proposed.

## Professor's Corner

These two questions together form the **structuralist critique** of mainstream development economics. The False Paradigm Model asks: "Why have the wrong policies been applied?" The Dualistic Development Thesis answers: "Because developing economies are structurally different from the textbook models Western economists learned."

The **real insight** is their complementarity. The False Paradigm explains the *intellectual origins* of bad policy; dualism explains the *structural reality* that makes those policies fail. Together, they constitute a powerful case for **context-specific, structurally aware** development economics — an approach that Todaro himself advocated.

**Contemporary relevance (2026):**

- The False Paradigm Model is echoed in debates about **decolonising development economics** — the demand that theory be grounded in local realities, not imported from Washington or Cambridge
- The **randomised controlled trial (RCT) controversy** (Banerjee & Duflo vs. Deaton, Rodrik) mirrors the False Paradigm critique: do micro-experiments impose a new form of intellectual dependence, or are they a corrective to old false paradigms?
- **Dualism** remains visible in every developing country: the productivity gap between agriculture and industry, the formal-informal divide, the urban-rural income gap, the exclusion of women from modern-sector employment
- **Bangladesh's graduation from LDC status (2026)** — the structural transformation required for graduation (rising manufacturing share, declining agriculture share, formalisation) is precisely the process that dualistic theory describes, and the persistence of informal employment (50.6% own-account workers) is a reminder that transformation is incomplete
- **Global value chains** have created new forms of dualism — workers in the same global factory may earn vastly different wages depending on their position in the value chain and the country they work in

**The crucial exam point:** Both questions are asking you to critique mainstream development theory from a structuralist/dependence perspective. Do not simply describe the models — **explain how they critique other models**. The False Paradigm critiques structural models; dualism critiques neoclassical convergence assumptions. This critical, comparative approach is what earns top marks.
