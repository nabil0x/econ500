> **Question:** Critically evaluate the impact of Bangladesh's Female Secondary Stipend Programme on female literacy rates.

## 1. What Is the Female Stipend Programme? Programme Origins and Structure

### 1.1 Historical Background

Bangladesh's Female Secondary Stipend and Assistance Programme (FSSAP) is one of the earliest and most celebrated conditional cash transfer (CCT) programmes in the developing world, targeting girls' secondary education in rural areas. Its evolution spans four decades:

| Phase | Period | Description |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| **Pilot** | 1982–1993 | Small-scale experiments in selected _thanas_ (sub-districts); results showed girls' secondary enrolment rising from 8% to 14% in project areas and dropout rates falling from 15% to under 4% |
| **National expansion** | 1994 | Four simultaneous projects launched: FSSAP (IDA-funded, 118 _thanas_), FSSP (government-funded, 282 _thanas_), SEDP (ADB-funded, 53 _thanas_), FSEP (NORAD-funded, 7 _thanas_) |
| **Consolidation** | 2008–2018 | Harmonised into a single national programme; stipend amount revised upward |
| **Current phase** | 2018–2023 | Integrated within the Secondary Education Development Programme (SEDP); Adolescent Girls' Programme added |

The programme emerged from a recognition that, in the late 1980s, Bangladesh had among the lowest female educational attainment in the world. The gross enrolment ratio for girls at secondary level was approximately 6–10% in 1981, and the adult female literacy rate stood at just 17.97% (UNESCO, 1981).

### 1.2 Policy Mechanism: The CCT Design

FSSAP is structured as a **conditional cash transfer** with three core eligibility criteria:

**Condition 1:** Attend at least **75% of school days** per academic year
**Condition 2:** Attain a minimum of **45% marks** in annual class-level examinations
**Condition 3:** Remain **unmarried** until completion of the Secondary School Certificate (SSC) examination

Girls meeting these conditions received:

| Grade | Monthly Stipend (1994 value) |
|-------|------------------------------|
| Grade 6 | ~US$ 12 (Tk. 25) |
| Grade 7 | ~US$ 13 (Tk. 28) |
| Grade 8 | ~US$ 15 (Tk. 31) |
| Grade 9 | ~US$ 24 (Tk. 50) |
| Grade 10 | ~US$ 30 (Tk. 60) |

Additionally, all eligible girls received a **tuition fee waiver** — schools participating in the programme were reimbursed directly, eliminating upfront fee payments for female students. The stipend was disbursed directly into recipients' bank accounts.

The total cost of the programme's first phase (1994–1999) was approximately US$71 million, equivalent to just **1.3% of Bangladesh's education budget** and roughly **6% of per capita income** per beneficiary. At its peak, FSSAP supported over **2 million girls annually**.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: Why CCTs for Girls' Education?

The programme rests on a well-established human capital rationale:

1. **Credit market failures:** Poor rural households face liquidity constraints that prevent investment in girls' education, despite high private returns. The stipend relaxes this constraint directly.

2. **Opportunity cost:** Girls' labour is valuable in household production (domestic work, childcare, agricultural labour). The cash transfer offsets the income forgone by keeping girls in school.

3. **Positive externalities:** Female education generates intergenerational benefits — educated mothers invest more in children's health and education, have lower fertility, and participate more effectively in the labour market. The subsidy internalises this social return.

4. **Gender-specific barriers:** Social norms regarding _purdah_ (seclusion), early marriage, and the perceived lower value of girls' education create demand-side distortions. The stipend acts as both an economic incentive and a signal of the value of girls' schooling.

The conditionality (75% attendance, passing grades, unmarried status) addresses **moral hazard** — ensuring that the transfer induces the desired behaviour rather than substituting for household education expenditure.

## 3. Key Outcomes: The Evidence

### 3.1 Female Secondary School Enrolment: A Structural Break

The introduction of FSSAP in 1994 produced a dramatic shift in girls' secondary enrolment. Prior to the programme, female gross enrolment at secondary level was stagnant at very low levels. After 1994, the trend reversed sharply.

| Year | Female Gross Enrolment Ratio (Secondary, %) | Male Gross Enrolment Ratio (Secondary, %) | Notes |
|------|---------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|-------|
| 1981 | 6.41 | 17.8 | Pre-pilot baseline; lowest on record |
| 1990 | ~18.0 | ~30.0 | Eve of national expansion |
| 1995 | ~25.0 | ~35.0 | Early FSSAP impact |
| 2000 | ~39.0 | ~38.0 | Girls overtake boys in absolute terms |
| 2005 | ~48.9 | ~42.2 | Gender gap reverses decisively |
| 2010 | ~55.0 | ~48.0 | MDG gender parity target achieved |
| 2015 | ~67.6 | ~57.0 | Female enrolment continues rising |
| 2020 | ~72.0 | ~62.0 | Pre-COVID peak |
| 2021 | 75.09 | ~60.0 | All-time high |
| 2024 | 70.47 | — | Post-COVID moderation |

**Key observation:** Female secondary enrolment grew at approximately **13% per annum** after 1994, compared with just **2.5% for boys** (ADB, 2021). By 2000, girls had surpassed boys in secondary enrolment, and by 2021, the gender parity index (GPI) for secondary education reached **1.24** — meaning 124 girls were enrolled for every 100 boys.

### 3.2 Female Literacy Rate: The Cumulative Impact

Female literacy (ages 15+) is a _stock_ variable that responds slowly to policy interventions — it reflects the accumulated educational attainment of the entire female population. However, the FSSAP-driven surge in girls' secondary enrolment from the mid-1990s onward produced a steady upward shift in female literacy, particularly visible from 2011 onward as the first FSSAP cohorts reached adulthood.

| Year | Female Literacy Rate (%) | Total Literacy Rate (%) | Male Literacy Rate (%) |
|------|-------------------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| 1981 | 17.97 | 39.73 | — |
| 1991 | 25.84 | 44.31 | — |
| 2001 | 40.82 | 53.90 | — |
| 2007 | 43.74 | 49.83 | — |
| 2011 | 55.12 | 62.48 | — |
| 2012 | 54.24 | 61.54 | — |
| 2013 | 57.79 | 64.21 | — |
| 2014 | 57.86 | 64.37 | — |
| 2015 | 62.25 | 68.06 | — |
| 2016 | 69.90 | 75.62 | — |
| 2017 | 70.09 | 75.70 | — |
| 2018 | 71.18 | 76.67 | — |
| 2019 | 71.95 | 77.44 | — |
| 2020 | 72.00 | 77.82 | — |
| 2021 | 73.64 | 79.21 | — |
| 2022 | **76.54** | **81.39** | — |

*Source: UNESCO Institute for Statistics (via World Bank World Development Indicators).*

**Interpretation:** The female literacy rate more than **quadrupled** from less than 18% in 1981 to over 76% in 2022. The acceleration is particularly notable after 2011 — when the first FSSAP beneficiaries (who entered secondary school around 1994–1997) reached age 15+ and entered the adult literacy measurement. The gap between female and male literacy, which was approximately 25 percentage points in 1991, narrowed substantially.

### 3.3 Gender Parity in Secondary Education: Bangladesh as South Asia's Outlier

Bangladesh achieved the Millennium Development Goal (MDG) target of gender parity in education **ahead of the 2015 deadline** — a rare distinction among South Asian countries. The gender parity index (GPI) for secondary education tells the story:

| Country | Secondary GPI (2000) | Secondary GPI (2015) | Secondary GPI (2021) |
|---------|---------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Bangladesh | ~1.03 | 1.13 | **1.24** |
| India | 0.78 | 1.01 | 1.04 |
| Pakistan | 0.63 | 0.74 | 0.82 |
| Nepal | 0.74 | 1.02 | 1.08 |
| Sri Lanka | 1.02 | 1.04 | 1.05 |
| South Asia (avg) | 0.80 | 0.97 | 1.04 |

*Note: GPI = female enrolment ratio / male enrolment ratio. GPI > 1 indicates girls outnumber boys.*

Bangladesh is the only South Asian country where girls' secondary enrolment substantially exceeds boys' — a direct legacy of the FSSAP. The programme effectively reversed the gender gap in a region where female educational disadvantage has been historically entrenched.

### 3.4 Beyond Literacy: Spillover Effects

Evaluation studies document broader socio-economic impacts attributable to FSSAP:

**Marriage and Fertility:**
- Mean age at first marriage for women rose from approximately **17.6 years (1994) to 19.4 years (2013)** — a gain of nearly 2 years coinciding with the programme period (ADB, 2021).
- The stipend's condition of remaining unmarried until SSC completion directly incentivised delayed marriage.
- Total fertility rate fell from 3.4 (1994) to 2.0 (2020), with female education identified as a primary driver.

**Labour Market Outcomes (Shamsuddin, 2015):**
- Exposure to 5 years of FSSAP associated with a **1-year increase** in completed education.
- Female labour force participation increased by **6 percentage points**.
- However, wages decreased by approximately **17%** because women concentrated in low-productivity self-employment — indicating that quantity of education outpaced quality and labour market absorption.

**Intergenerational Effects:**
- FSSAP-exposed women had **better-educated children** and lower child mortality (ADB, 2021).
- Positive spillover to brothers: girls' stipend programme indirectly improved boys' education outcomes through household-level income effects and changed social norms.

**Cost-Effectiveness:**
- ADB (2021) estimates that the programme's **development benefits outweigh its costs by more than 200%**.

### 3.5 Summary Impact Table

| Indicator | Before FSSAP (c. 1990) | After FSSAP (c. 2020) | Change |
|-----------|----------------------|---------------------|--------|
| Female secondary gross enrolment | ~18% | ~72% | +54 pp |
| Adult female literacy rate | 25.8% (1991) | 76.5% (2022) | +50.7 pp |
| Gender parity index (secondary) | ~0.80 | 1.24 | +0.44 |

| Total fertility rate | 3.4 | 2.0 | -1.4 |
| Girls' secondary enrolment (absolute) | 1.1 million (1991) | 3.9 million (2005) | 3.5x increase |

## 4. Critical Evaluation: What FSSAP Did Not Achieve

### 4.1 Persistent Dropout Rates

Despite the surge in enrolment, the **dropout rate for female students at the secondary level remained alarmingly high** — approximately **42%** before COVID-19 (Swarthmore/UNESCO data). Key dropout moments include:
- The transition from primary to secondary (Grade 5 to Grade 6) — many girls never make the transition.
- The transition from secondary to higher secondary (SSC to HSC) — dropout spikes after Grade 10.

**Reasons for dropout despite the stipend:**
- Stipend amounts eroded by inflation over time (nominal stipend remained fixed for extended periods).
- Household economic shocks (income volatility, climate disasters) could not be fully offset by the modest transfer.
- Child marriage persisted in many regions despite the conditionality — enforcement was weak.
- Gendered household responsibilities (domestic work, caring for siblings) still constrained girls' time.

### 4.2 Quality-of-Education Concerns

The programme's emphasis on _enrolment_ and _attendance_ came at the expense of **learning outcomes**:

- Rapid expansion of secondary education without proportional investment in teachers, classrooms, and materials led to **declining quality per student**.
- Teacher training and curriculum reform lagged behind the enrolment surge.
- Students passing exams at the 45% threshold may have weak foundational skills in literacy and numeracy — raising questions about the _meaning_ of the measured literacy gains.
- The World Bank's 2021 impact evaluation notes that while FSSAP increased years of schooling, it did not necessarily increase learning-adjusted years of schooling (LAYS).

### 4.3 Targeting and Inclusion Failures

- **Exclusion of the poorest:** Girls from the poorest households often could not meet the 75% attendance condition because they were needed for income generation or domestic work. The programme thus **creamed** from the better-off among the poor.
- **Urban exclusion:** The original programme targeted rural areas only, leaving urban slum-dwelling girls without stipend support.
- **Madrasa students:** Initially excluded from the stipend programme, madrasa students were brought under coverage only later (late 1990s), and implementation remained uneven.
- **Out-of-school girls:** The programme did nothing for girls who had never enrolled or who dropped out before reaching secondary school.

### 4.4 Labour Market Disconnect

Shamsuddin (2015) provides the most striking critique:

> "We find that exposure to 5 years of the programme is associated with a 1-year increase in education level completed and an increase in female labour force participation by six percentage points. However, we find that wages decrease by about 17% because the women have difficulties in finding a good job and end up in low productivity self-employment work."

This suggests that FSSAP succeeded in _quantity_ (more girls in school) but failed to address _quality_ (what they learned) and _labour market integration_ (what they could do with their education). The programme increased the supply of educated female labour without a corresponding increase in demand for skilled female workers — a classic **qualifications mismatch**.

### 4.5 Sustainability Concerns

- FSSAP has remained heavily **donor-dependent** (IDA, ADB, NORAD). The Government of Bangladesh has gradually increased its share, but full fiscal sustainability is uncertain.
- The stipend level has not kept pace with inflation, reducing the real value of the transfer over time.
- Political economy risk: as a visible, vote-winning programme, FSSAP is politically difficult to reform or phase out, even where its marginal impact may be declining.

| **Literacy impact** | Female literacy from 25.8% (1991) to 76.5% (2022); literacy is a stock variable — impact visible with lag; Bangladesh surpassed South Asian peers | ~1.5 marks |
| **Broader outcomes** | Delayed marriage (+1.8 years), reduced fertility, intergenerational effects; cost-benefit ratio >200% | ~1 mark |
| **Critical assessment** | Persistent 42% dropout rate; quality concerns (learning vs enrolment); exclusion of poorest; wage decline of 17% (Shamsuddin, 2015); sustainability | ~1.5 marks |

**Net assessment:** FSSAP was a highly successful CCT programme that fundamentally transformed female education in Bangladesh. It produced one of the fastest improvements in female literacy and secondary enrolment ever recorded in a low-income country, achieved gender parity in education, and generated substantial spillover benefits in delayed marriage and reduced fertility. However, quantity-of-education gains have not fully translated into quality-of-life gains — dropout rates remain high, learning outcomes are uneven, and labour market returns have been disappointing. The next generation of policy must focus on _quality_ and _labour market linkages_, not just enrolment.

## 6. Exam Tips

**For a 7-mark answer:**
- Structure is critical: (i) programme description (1.5 marks), (ii) enrolment and literacy outcomes with specific data (3 marks), (iii) critical assessment (2 marks), (iv) brief conclusion (0.5 marks).
- **Cite specific numbers** — examiners reward precise data. Know the key figures: 1994 launch, 75% attendance condition, female literacy 25.8% (1991) to 76.5% (2022), secondary enrolment 18% (1990) to 72% (2020), 13% annual growth rate, GPI 1.24.
- **Use the comparison with South Asia** — Bangladesh's gender parity achievement is unique in the region and shows the programme's transformative impact.
- **Do not be one-sided** — the question says "critically evaluate." You must discuss both successes and failures. The Shamsuddin (2015) finding of wage decline is the single most important critical point.
- **Connect to broader development debates** — FSSAP is a case study in how conditional cash transfers can address gender gaps in education, but it also illustrates the limits of _quantity-focused_ interventions without _quality_ and _demand-side_ complementarities.
- **Show the mechanism** — explain the human capital rationale (credit constraints, opportunity cost, externalities) and why conditionality matters. This distinguishes a descriptive answer from an analytical one.

**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Confusing the FSSAP (secondary, female-only, conditional) with other Bangladeshi stipend programmes (primary, universal, unconditional).
- Omitting the specific conditions (75% attendance, 45% pass, unmarried). These are the defining features of the programme.
- Discussing literacy trends without acknowledging that literacy is a _stock_ variable — changes take time to appear.
- Ignoring the critical evidence in favour of a purely celebratory account, or vice versa.
- Failing to mention any specific study or author.

## 7. Professor's Corner

> The FSSAP is one of the most **impact-evaluated** development programmes in South Asia, making it a rich case for discussing evaluation methods in development economics. The programme's staggered rollout created a natural experiment: different _thanas_ received the programme at different times, and different grade cohorts were exposed for varying durations. This variation allowed Khandker, Pitt, and Fuwa (2003) to use **difference-in-difference** and **fixed-effects** strategies to isolate causal impacts — a methodological strength rarely available in programme evaluation.
>
> **Broader lessons for development policy:**
>
> 1. **Conditional cash transfers work** when the condition is verifiable and the incentive is large enough to change behaviour. FSSAP's three conditions (attendance, performance, marital status) were all verifiable through school records and examination results.
>
> 2. **Supply-side complementarities matter.** FSSAP was not just a stipend programme — it included teacher training, curriculum reform, infrastructure improvement (sanitary facilities for girls), and awareness campaigns. The enrolment response reflects the combined effect of demand-side incentives and supply-side improvements.
>
> 3. **Quantity does not guarantee quality.** The FSSAP experience is a cautionary tale for development programmes focused on enrolment targets (MDG 2, SDG 4). Getting girls into school is necessary but not sufficient — learning outcomes, completion rates, and labour market transitions require separate attention.
>
> 4. **The marriage condition is both a strength and a weakness.** It effectively delayed marriage (a major social good), but it also excluded married adolescent girls who may have most needed educational support. The condition reflects a particular policy choice about which girls are "deserving" — a choice that development ethics continues to debate.
>
> 5. **Cost-effectiveness matters for policy scalability.** At just 1.3% of the education budget and a benefit-cost ratio exceeding 200%, FSSAP is among the most cost-effective social programmes ever implemented in Bangladesh. This is the kind of evidence that convinces finance ministries and donors to continue funding.
>
> 6. **Gender-targeted programmes can have unintended consequences.** FSSAP's success in attracting girls to secondary school was associated with a relative decline in boys' enrolment — the _male displacement effect_. Some schools became predominantly female, raising questions about whether the programme should be supplemented with support for disadvantaged boys.
>
> **Cross-connections in the exam:**
> - Connect to **Naila Kabeer's framework** (Q1–Q2): FSSAP is a concrete example of how policy can expand women's _agency_ by investing in their human capital, though it does not directly address the _classical patriarchy_ constraints Kabeer emphasises.
> - Connect to **feminisation of agriculture** (Q13): as women gain education but find limited formal-sector employment, they may be pushed into agricultural self-employment — the FSSAP-labour market disconnect echoes this pattern.
> - Connect to **gender and development** (Q12): FSSAP shows that targeted policy interventions can accelerate gender equity in education even without broad-based economic growth, challenging the idea that growth alone ensures gender equity.
> - Connect to **conditional cash transfers** as a policy instrument: compare with Bolsa Familia (Brazil), Progresa/Oportunidades (Mexico), and other CCTs in the developing world.
>
