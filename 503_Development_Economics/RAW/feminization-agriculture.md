## A. Why do we think that 'feminisation of agriculture' in Bangladesh has taken place? (08 marks)

> **Marking Guide [8 marks]:** Definition (1) | Four drivers with evidence table (3) | LFS 2022 trend data (2) | Consequences — positive and negative (1) | Kabeer's agency framework applied (1)

### Definition (1 mark)

**Feminisation of agriculture** refers to the rising share of women in the agricultural labour force — both as primary farm managers and as agricultural wage labourers — as men shift to non-agricultural employment. It is a **gendered restructuring of the rural economy**: women are not merely *participating more* in agriculture; they are increasingly becoming *de facto* farm heads and decision-makers in contexts where male household members have migrated or exited the sector.

Three dimensions matter:

| Dimension | Meaning | Bangladesh Context |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Numerical** | Rising share of women in agricultural employment | Women now near parity in agriculture workforce |
| **Functional** | Women taking on roles previously done by men (ploughing, marketing, hiring labour) | Breaking traditional gender division of labour |
| **Decision-making** | Women becoming primary farm decision-makers, not just contributing family workers | Especially in male-out-migration households |

### Four Drivers (3 marks)

| Driver | Causal Mechanism | Bangladesh Evidence |
|--------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **① Male out-migration** | Rural men migrate to cities (RMG, construction, transport, services) or abroad (Middle East, Southeast Asia) for higher wages. Women left behind assume farm management by default. | Over **10 million Bangladeshi migrants** abroad (BMET 2023); massive internal rural→urban migration. Remittances = ~5% of GDP. Women from migrant households operate 30–40% of farms in high-migration areas (Barisal, Chittagong Hill Tracts). |
| **② RMG sector pull** | The RMG sector (4+ million workers, ~60% women) does *not* directly pull women from agriculture — it pulls *men* from agriculture, and pulls some women from rural areas into factories. The net effect: men exit agriculture faster than women. | Female RMG employment: near-zero (1980s) → **2.5+ million** (2023). Male RMG employment also grew (1.5M+), pulling men from farming. The opportunity cost of male farm labour rose — women filled the gap. |
| **③ Land fragmentation** | Population pressure (168M on 147,570 km²) has fragmented holdings below economic viability for male household heads. Men seek non-farm income; women manage the small remaining plots. | Average farm size: **<0.5 hectares** (HIES 2022). Operated holdings: 0.32 ha (mean). Number of holdings: 16.8M (up from 15.1M in 2008). Fragmentation drives men out, leaves women with marginal holdings. |
| **④ Poverty and distress** | Poor households cannot survive on male income alone. Women enter agricultural wage labour — the most precarious category of work — out of **distress-push** rather than opportunity-pull. | **24.3% of female agricultural workers** are casual/day labourers (vs 14.1% of men). Female agricultural wage is **60–70% of male wage** for same tasks. Female-headed households (15.2% of rural HHs) are overrepresented in agricultural labour. |

> **⚠️ Critical distinction:** Drivers ①–③ are *structural* (long-run economic transformation). Driver ④ is *cyclical/distress* (poverty forcing women into the worst work). The exam answer must distinguish these — it is the difference between feminisation-as-transformation and feminisation-as-immiseration.

### Evidence from the Labour Force Survey (LFS 2022) — 2 marks

The LFS provides the definitive quantitative picture of Bangladesh's changing labour market gender composition:

| Indicator | 2010 | 2016 | 2022 | Change (2010–22) |
|-----------|------|------|------|-------------------|
| Total employed | 54.1M | 63.5M | 70.47M | **+30%** |
| Male employed | 37.9M | 42.7M | 45.61M | **+20%** |
| Female employed | 16.2M | 20.8M | 24.86M | **+53%** |
| Female share of employment | 29.9% | 32.8% | 35.3% | **+5.4 pp** |
| Female LFPR | 36.0% | 39.8% | 42.77% | **+6.8 pp** |
| Male LFPR | 82.5% | 81.3% | 80.0% | −2.5 pp |

**Key findings for feminisation of agriculture:**

1. **Agriculture remains the largest employer for women.** Despite growth in RMG and services, **over 60% of employed women** still work in agriculture (vs ~38% of employed men).
2. **The gender composition of agricultural employment has shifted dramatically.** Agriculture was male-dominated in the 1990s; by 2022, women approach parity in the agricultural workforce.
3. **Men are exiting agriculture faster than women are.** Male agricultural employment fell in absolute terms in some districts; female agricultural employment rose everywhere.
4. **"Contributing family worker" status remains a problem.** A large share of women in agriculture are classified as *unpaid contributing family workers* — their labour is statistically invisible as "employment" in national accounts.

> **Exam tip — LFS data punchline:** "Female employment grew 53% while male employment grew only 20% — and **agriculture still absorbs 6 in 10 employed women**. This is the feminisation of agriculture in numbers."

### Positive and Negative Consequences (1 mark)

| Domain | Positive Consequences | Negative Consequences |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| **For women** | Gain agricultural knowledge, skills, and farm management experience | Lack land titles ⇒ cannot access credit, extension, technology |
| **For households** | Maintains food production despite male absence; women manage household food security | Without male labour, women face labour constraints and lower yields per hectare |
| **For the economy** | Mobilises otherwise underutilised female labour; maintains agricultural output | Women's work is classified as "contributing family worker" — **unpaid** and invisible in GDP; perpetuates statistical discrimination |
| **For gender relations** | Women's economic contribution becomes visible; decision-making in farming increases | Extension services and technology remain **male-targeted**; women get second-hand information and obsolete equipment |
| **For children/nutrition** | Evidence that women farmers allocate more income to children's nutrition and education (World Bank 2023) | Triple burden: productive + reproductive + community work leads to time poverty and health costs |

### Kabeer's Framework Applied (1 mark)

Naila Kabeer's agency framework explains why feminisation of agriculture does not automatically translate into women's empowerment:

**Agency within constraint:** Women are not *choosing* agriculture over other sectors — they are responding to male out-migration and RMG demand pulling men elsewhere. This is an example of what Kabeer calls **"adaptive preferences"** — women make the best of limited options, not free choices among equally available alternatives.

**Resources → Agency → Achievements:**

| Kabeer's Dimensions | Application to Feminisation of Agriculture |
|--------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Material resources** | Women gain practical farm knowledge (human capital) but lack **land titles** (the most critical material resource in rural Bangladesh) |
| **Social resources** | Women's agricultural networks expand (neighbour women, NGO groups) but they are excluded from **male-dominated** extension groups, market networks, and input supply chains |
| **Symbolic capital** | Women's farm work remains symbolically devalued — classified as "helping the family" rather than "farming" — so it does not translate into bargaining power within the household |
| **Agency** | Women exercise **practical agency** (day-to-day farm decisions) but not **strategic agency** (land ownership, marketing, technology adoption, credit contracts) |
| **Achievements** | Food production maintained; household survival secured — but gender gaps in yields, income, and asset ownership persist |

> **Kabeer's core insight for this question:** "Feminisation of agriculture expands women's *responsibilities* without necessarily expanding their *rights*." The same process that puts women in charge of farms also exposes them to risk (crop failure, price shocks) without giving them the institutional resources to manage those risks.

## B. What are the institutional barriers faced by female farmers in Bangladesh? (12 marks)

> **Marking Guide [12 marks]:** Land tenure insecurity (2) | Credit constraints (2) | Extension services (2) | Technology gap (1) | Labour constraints (1) | Market access (1) | Social norms (2) | Policy neglect (1)

### Introduction (1 mark)

Female farmers in Bangladesh face a **layered structure of institutional barriers** — formal and informal — that systematically constrain their productivity, income, and well-being. Drawing on Kabeer's framework, these barriers operate across multiple domains: **material resources** (land, credit, technology), **human resources** (extension, training), **social resources** (markets, networks), and **symbolic capital** (social norms, policy recognition). The cumulative effect is a **gender productivity gap** estimated at 20–30% lower yields for women-operated farms compared to male-operated farms in Bangladesh (FAO 2023), not because women are less capable farmers, but because they operate within a structurally unequal institutional environment.

### Institutional Barrier ①: Land Tenure Insecurity (2 marks)

**The problem:** Land is the foundational productive asset in Bangladesh's agrarian economy — it determines access to credit, extension services, irrigation, subsidies, and input markets. Women's land ownership is **extremely limited**.

| Indicator | Value | Source |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| Land owned by women (titles in women's names) | **~7%** | Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics 2022 |
| Agricultural land owned/managed by women | <10% of total operated area | HIES 2022 |
| Female-headed households with land title | 12.3% (vs 67.8% for male-headed HHs) | BDHS 2018 |
| Women who inherited land (despite legal right) | Only 1.8% of all inherited land transfers go to women | Jahan & Sultana 2021 |
| Share of women among agricultural holders | 36% | Agriculture Census 2019 |

**Mechanisms of exclusion:**
- **Legal vs customary dissonance:** Bangladesh's Muslim Family Laws (1961) and Hindu Personal Laws grant women inheritance rights — but customary practice overwhelmingly excludes them. Brothers, uncles, and male relatives exert pressure on women to **relinquish their share** ("for the honour of the family" or "you are married into another family now").
- **Patrilocal exogamy:** Since women move to their husband's village after marriage, parents prefer to keep land with sons who will continue to farm it locally. Daughters receive moveable property (jewellery, cash) instead.
- **Registration bias:** Even when women purchase land or receive it as a gift, it is typically **registered in the husband's or son's name** due to bureaucratic harassment, higher stamp duties for women (recently reformed but culturally embedded), and social expectation.

**Kabeer connection:** Land is the most critical **material resource** in rural Bangladesh. Without it, women cannot convert their agricultural labour into economic independence. The symbolic capital of land ownership (status, authority, identity as a "farmer") remains denied to most women — they are seen as "labourers" or "helpers," never as "farmers" in their own right.

### Institutional Barrier ②: Credit Constraints (2 marks)

**The problem:** Access to institutional credit (banks, microfinance, agricultural loans) is systematically biased against women farmers. The mechanism is simple: **credit requires collateral; collateral requires land titles; women lack land titles.**

| Indicator | Value | Source |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| Women farmers with access to institutional credit | **<5%** of agricultural loans go to women | Bangladesh Bank 2023 |
| Collateral requirement for agricultural loans | Typically **land title** of ≥33 decimals | Bank policy |
| Women reporting "lack of collateral" as barrier | 72% of women who sought a loan were rejected | IFAD 2022 Bangladesh |
| Microfinance to women for agriculture | Only 12% of MFI loans are for agriculture; rest for livestock, petty trade | PKSF 2023 |
| Interest rate gap | Women pay **2–5% higher** effective interest than men in informal lending | BIDS 2022 |

**Three dimensions of the credit constraint:**
1. **Formal banks** require land titles as collateral — directly excluding most women.
2. **Microfinance institutions (MFIs)** reach women but provide **small loans** (avg Tk 15,000–30,000) for **non-agricultural** activities (livestock, poultry, petty trade). MFI loans are too small and short-term for land leasing, irrigation equipment, or mechanisation.
3. **Informal lenders (mohajons)** charge **120–240% annual interest** — women are forced to borrow at these rates for emergency agricultural expenses (irrigation fuel, labour hire at harvest), trapping them in debt cycles.

**Kabeer connection:** Credit access is a **material resource** that enables investment, risk-taking, and productivity growth. Its absence keeps women in low-productivity, subsistence agriculture — what Kabeer would call "poverty as constraint, not choice."

### Institutional Barrier ③: Gendered Extension Services (2 marks)

**The problem:** Bangladesh's agricultural extension system (Department of Agricultural Extension — DAE) was designed by men, for men, and largely **still reaches only men**. Extension is the single most cost-effective intervention for raising farm productivity — its gender bias is therefore one of the most damaging institutional barriers.

| Indicator | Value | Source |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| Female extension agents in DAE | **<5%** of 15,000+ extension officers | DAE 2022 |
| Women farmers reached by extension | **Only 5%** of female farmers have direct contact with extension agents | FAO Bangladesh 2023 |
| Information source for women farmers | 68% rely on **husbands or male relatives** as information intermediaries | IFPRI 2021 |
| Training sessions with women participants | 12% of DAE training programmes include women farmers | DAE Gender Audit 2022 |
| Women satisfied with extension access | 18% (vs 54% for men) | BIDS Extension Survey 2022 |

**Mechanisms of exclusion:**
- **Male agent + male farmer assumption:** Extension officers are overwhelmingly male and default to contacting male farmers. They rarely visit women farmers directly.
- **Second-hand, degraded information:** Women receive agricultural information through their husbands — who may filter, forget, or distort the message. The information is also **delayed** — by the time it reaches women, planting windows may have passed.
- **Timing and location mismatch:** Training sessions and demonstration plots are held at times and places that conflict with women's domestic responsibilities (childcare, cooking, water collection) and mobility constraints (they cannot travel far from the village without male escort).
- **Content bias:** Extension messages focus on HYV rice, cash crops, and commercial agriculture — the domain of male farmers. Women's crops (homestead vegetables, pulses, oilseeds) receive far less extension attention.

**Kabeer connection:** Extension services are a **human resource** (knowledge, skills, information). The gender extension gap means women are systematically denied this resource — and since knowledge is a form of power, its denial reinforces women's subordinate position in agricultural decision-making.

### Institutional Barrier ④: Technology Gap (1 mark)

**The problem:** Women farmers have significantly less access to yield-enhancing agricultural technology — including **HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, irrigation, mechanised equipment, and post-harvest processing technology**.

| Technology | Women's Access vs Men's | Gap |
|-----------|------------------------|-----|
| HYV seeds | 45% of women farmers use HYVs vs 62% of men | −17 pp |
| Chemical fertiliser (recommended dose) | 38% adoption vs 54% | −16 pp |
| Irrigation (power pump access) | 22% vs 44% | −22 pp |
| Mechanical tillage (power tiller/tractor) | 18% own/manage vs 52% | −34 pp |
| Mechanised harvesting | <5% access vs 25% | −20 pp |
| Post-harvest equipment (drying, storage, processing) | 12% vs 30% | −18 pp |

*Source: IFPRI Bangladesh Gender and Agriculture Survey 2021; Agriculture Census 2019*

**Why the gap exists:**
- Technology is marketed, distributed, and demonstrated through **male networks** (input dealers, extension agents, male farmers).
- Machinery is **designed for male users** (power tillers require physical strength; controls are not ergonomically adjusted for women).
- Rental markets for equipment (common in Bangladesh) are controlled by men — women face **higher rental rates** or outright refusal.
- **Risk aversion:** Women, lacking safety nets and credit, are less able to experiment with new technologies — the cost of failure is higher for them.

**Productivity consequence:** The technology gap is a primary driver of the **gender yield gap** — women's rice yields are 22–35% lower per hectare than men's, even controlling for plot size and soil quality (FAO 2023). Closing the technology gap would close most of the yield gap.

### Institutional Barrier ⑤: Labour Constraints (1 mark)

**The problem:** The very process that drives feminisation of agriculture — male out-migration — simultaneously **deprives female-operated farms of male labour** for the most physically demanding agricultural tasks.

| Dimension | Nature of Constraint | Evidence |
|-----------|---------------------|----------|
| Peak-season labour shortage | Women cannot perform or replace male tasks (ploughing, irrigation channel maintenance, heavy lifting) in time-sensitive operations | 40% of female-headed HHs report labour shortage in transplanting/harvest season |
| Labour hire difficulties | Women face **higher wage demands** from hired labourers; male labourers refuse to work for women supervisors | Female farm heads pay 15–20% higher wages in some areas |
| Own-labour constraint | Women's time is already saturated with **reproductive labour** (cooking, childcare, water/fuel collection) — adding farm work creates time poverty | Women work **14–16 hours/day** during peak season vs 8–10 hours for men |
| Childcare penalty | Women with young children cannot work full days in the field; must bring children, reducing productivity | 67% of women farmers report childcare as limiting their farm labour supply |
| Widow-specific | Widowed women lose not only a spouse but **the male labour that made the farm viable** — many are forced to lease out their land and become agricultural labourers | Widows are 3× more likely to sell/lease land than married women |

**Kabeer connection:** Labour constraints reveal the **reproductive bargain** embedded in patriarchy — women's time is allocated first to household reproduction, then to productive work. The feminisation of agriculture adds to women's workload without reallocating reproductive responsibilities (what Kabeer calls the **"triple burden"**).

### Institutional Barrier ⑥: Market Access Discrimination (1 mark)

**The problem:** Women farmers face systematic discrimination in **output markets** (selling produce), **input markets** (buying seeds, fertiliser, pesticides), and **factor markets** (leasing land, hiring labour).

| Market Type | Barrier | Evidence |
|-------------|---------|----------|
| **Output markets** — selling crops | Women receive **lower prices** than men for identical produce; forced to sell at farmgate to male intermediaries | Women get 10–20% lower price for paddy; 15–25% lower for vegetables |
| **Input markets** — buying inputs | Input dealers overcharge women (price discrimination); or refuse to sell on credit (which they extend to male farmers) | 52% of women farmers report being overcharged for fertiliser |
| **Land rental market** | Women pay **higher lease rates** for land; landlords prefer male tenants | Women pay 10–30% higher sharecropping rent |
| **Market information** | Women lack access to price information, market timing intelligence | Only 15% of women farmers have mobile-based market information access |
| **Market spaces** | Physical markets (haat/bazar) are male spaces — women face harassment, lack toilet facilities, cannot stay overnight | 70% of women sell <100m from home; <5% travel to district markets |

**The institution of the bazaar:** In Bangladesh, wholesale agricultural markets (haats, bazaars) are overwhelmingly **male-controlled spaces**. Women cannot participate directly in price negotiation, cannot access wholesale buyers, and are forced to accept prices set by male intermediaries who exploit their immobility. This represents a massive **extraction of surplus from women farmers by male middlemen** — a gender dimension of the market access problem that is rarely discussed in mainstream agricultural economics.

### Institutional Barrier ⑦: Social Norms — Purdah, Mobility, and Decision-Making (2 marks)

**The problem:** Deeply embedded social institutions — what Kabeer calls **"classical patriarchy"** (Kandiyoti 1988) — restrict women's ability to function as autonomous farmers.

**Three dimensions:**

#### Purdah (veiling/seclusion)

- **Institutionalised female seclusion** restricts women's physical mobility and their interaction with male non-kin
- A woman observed in the field, at the market, or negotiating with male labourers may face **social sanction, gossip, loss of family honour, or domestic violence**
- Result: women cannot freely access fields, markets, extension offices, or training centres — they are confined to the homestead and immediate vicinity
- **But purdah is not static** — it is renegotiated daily. A woman may observe purdah with male neighbours but not with extension workers; she may avoid the market but interact freely with NGOs. This is what Kabeer calls **"negotiating patriarchy."**

#### Mobility restrictions

| Restriction | Impact on Farming | Evidence |
|-------------|------------------|----------|
| Cannot travel alone beyond village | Cannot sell produce at best-price market; cannot attend training | 65% of female farmers report mobility as a constraint |
| Cannot interact with male extension officers alone | Misses technical advice; relies on male intermediaries | See Barrier ③ |
| Cannot stay overnight away from home | Cannot access distant markets, processing centres, or agricultural fairs | 89% of women return home daily (vs 42% of men) |
| Requires male escort for official transactions | Cannot independently register land, open bank accounts, access credit | Women's "literacy of paperwork" is lower |

#### Decision-making deficit

| Decision | % of Women Who Participate Jointly or Alone | Source |
|----------|--------------------------------------------|--------|
| What crops to plant | **38%** | BDHS 2018 |
| Whether to lease in/out land | **22%** | IFPRI 2021 |
| Purchase of agricultural inputs | **28%** | IFPRI 2021 |
| Sale of agricultural output | **24%** | IFPRI 2021 |
| Use of agricultural credit | **16%** | BDHS 2018 |
| Use of own income | **45%** (but only 12% have sole authority) | BDHS 2018 |

**Kabeer's "bargaining within constraint":** Women exercise agency within these patriarchal constraints, but their bargaining position is weak because:
- Their contribution is devalued as "helping" not "farming"
- They lack the **exit option** (where would a woman who leaves her marriage go?)
- They operate within **patriarchal bargains** (Kandiyoti 1988) — accepting subordination in exchange for male protection and economic support

### Institutional Barrier ⑧: Policy Neglect — The Invisibility of Women Farmers in Agricultural Policy (1 mark)

**The problem:** Bangladesh's agricultural policies — in design, implementation, and evaluation — have historically **not recognised women as farmers**. The default farmer in policy documents is male.

| Policy/Programme | Gender Gap | Year |
|------------------|------------|------|
| National Agriculture Policy (NAP) | Does not mention women farmers as a distinct category until 2018 revision | 2013/2018 |
| Agricultural subsidy (fertiliser, fuel) | Subsidies channelled through male household heads; women-headed HHs often miss out | Ongoing |
| Agricultural input distribution (govt) | Beneficiary lists prepared by union parishads — overwhelmingly male | Ongoing |
| Crop insurance (recent pilot) | Requires land title to participate — excludes most women | 2022 |
| Procurement policy (government rice purchase) | Women farmers cannot meet procurement volume thresholds; lack transport to procurement centres | Ongoing |
| Disaster response (agriculture inputs) | Post-flood seed/input distribution reaches male farmers first | Recurring |

**The "gender-blind" agriculture budget:** Bangladesh's national budget allocates approximately **Tk 35,000 crore** to agriculture and food security (FY 2023–24). Of this, less than **2%** is specifically directed toward women farmers or has gender-responsive budgeting provisions (Ministry of Finance Gender Budget Report 2023). The majority of the agriculture budget is **gender-blind** — meaning it is de facto male-targeted, because it flows through institutions (DAE, BADC, banks, input channels) that default to male farmers.

**Kabeer connection:** Policy neglect is an institutional expression of women's lack of **symbolic capital** — they are not "seen" as farmers by the state, so they are not "counted" in policy design. This is what Kabeer calls **"institutional exclusion"** — the rules of the game (both formal and informal) systematically disadvantage women because they were designed without women in mind.

### Cumulative Effect: The Institutional Poverty Trap (1 mark — synthesis)

The eight barriers are not independent — they are **mutually reinforcing**. Kabeer's resources framework shows the system:

```
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
         THE INSTITUTIONAL POVERTY TRAP FOR
              WOMEN FARMERS IN BANGLADESH
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────

   LAND INSECURITY ──────────────────────────────┐
        ↓                                        │
   NO COLLATERAL → CREDIT EXCLUDED ──────────────┼──┐
        ↓                                        │  │
   CANNOT BUY INPUTS/TECHNOLOGY                   │  │
        ↓                                        │  │
   LOW PRODUCTIVITY (gender yield gap of 20–35%)  │  │
        ↓                                        │  │
   LOW MARKETABLE SURPLUS                        │  │
        ↓                                        │  │
   LOW INCOME ───────────────────────────────────┘  │
        ↓                                           │
   CANNOT AFFORD TO:                                 │
     • Lease additional land                         │
     • Hire labour                                  │
     • Purchase inputs (cycle starts again) ────────┘
        ↓
   CANNOT INVEST IN DAUGHTERS' EDUCATION
   → Next generation also trapped

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

Each barrier reinforces the others: no land → no credit → no inputs → low yields → low income → cannot escape. Breaking any single barrier (e.g., giving women credit *without* land titles) can help but is insufficient without addressing the **interlocking institutional structure**.

### Policy Recommendations — What Works (for the evaluative conclusion)

| Barrier | Evidence-Based Intervention | Bangladesh Example |
|---------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Land tenure | Joint land titling (husband + wife) by default on all new land registration | **Kerala model** reduced gender gap; BD pilot in 5 upazilas (NSDS 2021) |
| Credit | **Group-based agricultural credit** without collateral (using joint liability) | **BRAC's Agriculture Credit Programme** — reaches 300,000+ women |
| Extension | **Female extension agents** recruited specifically for women farmers | DAE recruited 500+ female Sub-Assistant Agriculture Officers (SAAO) under NAP 2018, still <5% |
| Technology | Women-only **training + rental** programmes for power tillers, harvesters | **CIMMYT** women's mechanisation pilot in Rangpur — 40% yield increase |
| Market access | Women-only **digital market platforms**; mobile-based price information | **e-Grocery/e-Krishok** platforms; **Amar Krishi** app |
| Social norms | **Gender-transformative approaches** that engage men and community leaders, not just women | **HER+ programme** by World Fish/IFPRI — works with couples, not just women |
| Policy | **Gender-responsive budgeting** in agriculture; sex-disaggregated data collection | MOF Gender Budget Report (growing but still <2% of ag budget targeted) |

## Exam Tips

1. **Part A structure:** Define → Four drivers (table) → LFS evidence → Consequences table → Kabeer framework → Judgement (is this empowerment or burden?)
2. **Part B structure:** Introduce (institutions matter) → Eight barriers (2 marks each for first 3, 1 mark each for middle 3, 2 marks for norms, 1 mark for policy) → Synthesis diagram → Policy recommendations
3. **Connect the parts:** The feminisation of agriculture (Part A) does not happen in an institutional vacuum — it happens *despite* the barriers described in Part B. The tension between rising responsibility and persistent institutional exclusion is the heart of the analysis.
4. **Kabeer is the thread:** Use her framework throughout — Part A (agency within constraint) and Part B (resources → agency → achievements; institutional exclusion; symbolic capital).
5. **Data is indispensable:** Without LFS data, Part A loses 2 marks. Without the specific numbers (7% land titles, 5% extension reach, <5% agricultural credit), Part B loses 3–4 marks.
6. **Avoid the "blame game":** Do not present barriers as conspiracies — analyse them as institutional structures with historical roots and functional logic (patriliny, labour markets, bureaucratic path dependence).
7. **End with "what works":** The strongest answers conclude Part B with evidence-based solutions — this shows you do not just diagnose problems but can think about implementation.

## Professor's Corner

> The feminisation of agriculture question is the most applied gender-and-development question on the paper. It tests whether you can move beyond abstract concepts (patriarchy, agency) to concrete institutional analysis. The distinguishing mark of a first-class answer is the **integration of Part A and Part B**: showing how the feminisation *trend* (rising women in agriculture) collides with the institutional *structure* (barriers women face). Most students describe one or the other. The best answers show the **tension and the dynamic**.
>
> On the **Kabeer connection** — the examiners specifically want to see you apply Kabeer's framework, not just name-drop. In Part A, use "agency within constraint" and "adaptive preferences." In Part B, use the resources taxonomy (material, human, social, symbolic capital) to organise the eight barriers — it is a natural fit.
>
> On **data** — use the LFS 2022 for Part A (the +53% figure is essential). For Part B, the 7% land title figure is the single most powerful data point. Commit it to memory.
>
> On **Bangladesh specificity** — this is not a generic "gender in agriculture" answer. The presence of RMG (as both pull factor for men and counter-intuitive push factor for women into agriculture), the specific land fragmentation pattern (0.5 ha average, 16.8M holdings), the DAE extension structure (15,000+ officers, <5% women), and the classical patriarchy belt — all make Bangladesh's case distinctive. Mention these specifics.
>
> **A note on "contributing family worker":** The statistical invisibility of women's agricultural work is itself an institutional barrier — it is woven through barriers ③ (extension ignores them because they are not "real farmers"), ⑤ (their labour is assumed elastic and unpaid), and ⑧ (policy does not count them). A sophisticated answer notes this cross-cutting theme.
