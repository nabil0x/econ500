## Part A: Gender Equality vs Gender Equity (6 marks)

### Definitions

**Gender Equality** refers to the state in which access to rights, opportunities, and outcomes is not determined by sex. It is a **formal/legal concept** rooted in the principle of *sameness of treatment* — that women and men should be treated identically under the law, have equal access to education, employment, political participation, and legal rights. The focus is on **equal outcomes and equal opportunities** as measurable targets.

**Gender Equity**, by contrast, is a **procedural and substantive justice concept**. It recognises that women and men have different needs, historical disadvantages, and structural constraints — and that achieving genuine equality may require **differential treatment** to rectify accumulated injustice. Equity asks not whether treatment is identical, but whether it is **fair and just** given the starting positions of different groups (Kabeer 1999; Sen 1992).

### Key Distinctions

| Dimension | Gender Equality | Gender Equity |
|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| **Core principle** | Sameness of treatment | Fairness and justice |
| **Focus** | Equal rights, opportunities, outcomes | Recognising different needs and historical disadvantage |
| **Legal orientation** | Formal/legal equality (de jure) | Substantive equality (de facto) |
| **Approach** | Sex-blind | Sex-aware; may require affirmative action |
| **Measurement** | Parity ratios (e.g., M:F wage ratio, enrolment ratio) | Process and capability indicators (agency, voice, freedom) |
| **Policy tools** | Anti-discrimination laws, equal pay legislation | Quotas, targeted interventions, care infrastructure, reservations |
| **Critique of the other** | Equity can justify paternalism or special treatment that reinforces stereotypes | Formal equality ignores structural barriers — "equal treatment of unequals perpetuates inequality" (Sen 1992) |
| **Example** | Equal pay for equal work | Providing maternity leave, flexible work, or reserved parliamentary seats |

### Feminist Economics Critique

Feminist economics challenges the conflation of equality with sameness. **Martha Nussbaum** (2000) and **Amartya Sen** (1992) — through the **Capabilities Approach** — argue that well-being is about what people are actually *able to do and be*, not just the formal rights they hold. Gender equality in legal texts does not guarantee a woman can walk safely to work, negotiate her wage, or decide when to marry. The capabilities approach insists on **substantive freedom** — which requires equity measures to address differential starting positions.

**Naila Kabeer** (1999) further distinguishes between:
- **Equality of opportunity** (formal access)
- **Equality of agency** (the capacity to set and pursue one's own goals)
- **Equality of outcomes** (actual achievements)

Equality of agency, she argues, is the most consequential because it captures whether women can *convert* formal rights into real freedoms — a process that requires equity-minded interventions (Kabeer 1999, *Resources, Agency, Achievements*).

### Summary for Exam

| Concept | Mantra | Key Theorist | Policy Implication |
|---------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| Gender Equality | "Same treatment for all" | Liberal feminism | Anti-discrimination laws |
| Gender Equity | "Fair treatment given different starting points" | Sen, Nussbaum, Kabeer | Affirmative action, care policy |
| Capabilities Approach | "What people are actually able to do and be" | Sen, Nussbaum | Multi-dimensional policy beyond income |

## Part B: Does Economic Growth Ensure Gender Equity? (14 marks)

### Thesis

**No. Economic growth does not automatically ensure gender equity.** While growth can create enabling conditions — expanding employment opportunities, raising household incomes, and funding public services — the relationship between growth and gender equity is mediated by **labour market structures, unpaid care work, social norms, and policy choices**. Equity requires deliberate institutional change, not merely aggregate expansion.

### Framework: Why Growth Alone Is Insufficient

| Channel | How Growth Can Help | Why Growth Alone Fails |
|---------|--------------------|----------------------|
| **1. Labour market segregation** | Growth creates new jobs, including in export-oriented manufacturing and services — sectors that have historically drawn women into paid work (e.g., Bangladesh RMG) | Women concentrated in low-productivity, low-wage, informal, insecure work. Occupational segregation by sex persists — women in "female" sectors (garments, domestic work, agriculture) and men in "male" sectors (construction, transport, finance). Growth *per se* does not dismantle segregation; it can even deepen it. |
| **2. Unpaid care work burden** | Higher incomes enable investment in care infrastructure (childcare, elder care, labour-saving technology) that can reduce unpaid work | Women continue to bear disproportionate unpaid care work — 3.2 times more than men globally (ILO 2018). As women enter paid employment, they face a "double burden" of paid + unpaid work, leading to time poverty, overwork, and health costs. Growth does not automatically redistribute unpaid work within households. |
| **3. Persistent social norms** | Urbanisation, education, and media exposure can weaken patriarchal constraints over time | Norms adapt rather than disappear (Kandiyoti 1988 — "classical patriarchy"). Women's employment may be accepted as an economic necessity but not accompanied by changes in household decision-making, mobility, or marriage practices. Social sanctions against women in "non-traditional" roles persist. |

### Cross-Country Evidence: The Feminisation U-Hypothesis

Goldin's (1995) **feminisation U-hypothesis** describes the empirical relationship between economic development (GDP per capita) and female labour force participation (FLFP):

1. **Low-income stage:** FLFP is relatively high — women work in subsistence agriculture, family enterprises, and informal trade. Work is counted as employment but is low-productivity and unpaid.
2. **Middle-income transition:** FLFP *falls* — rising male incomes allow families to withdraw women from the labour force; female domesticity becomes a marker of social status (the "housewife ideal"). This is especially pronounced in societies with strong patriarchal norms (Bangladesh, India, Middle East).
3. **High-income stage:** FLFP rises again — service sector expansion, higher education levels, declining fertility, and labour-saving technology draw women back into paid employment.

**Critical implication:** The U-shape means that in the early-to-middle stages of growth, measured FLFP can actually *decline* — the opposite of what a naive "growth-is-good-for-women" thesis predicts. Equity outcomes are path-dependent and shaped by the *type* of growth, not its rate.

### Bangladesh Evidence

Bangladesh provides a rich case study because it has experienced rapid economic growth (averaging >6% GDP growth through the 2010s) alongside significant shifts in women's economic participation.

| Indicator | Value | Year | Implication |
|-----------|-------|------|-------------|
| Female LFPR | 42.77% | 2022 | Substantial rise from ~26% (2010) — growth-linked increase |
| Male LFPR | 80% | 2022 | Gender gap of ~37 percentage points persists |
| Female unemployment | 3.59% | 2022 | Slightly higher than male (3.50%) despite lower participation |
| Women in informal work | 87% | 2022 | Majority are own-account or unpaid family workers |
| Gender wage gap | 30–40% | Various | Women earn substantially less than men across sectors |
| Women in RMG | ~60% of workforce | 2022 | Single largest employer of women — but low-wage, insecure |
| Women in agriculture | ~55% of agri labour | 2022 | Majority unpaid family workers, not wage workers |

**Interpretation:** Growth increased the *quantity* of women's employment but not necessarily its *quality*. The 87% informal share means most employed women lack job security, social protection, and collective bargaining power. The wage gap suggests that even within formal sectors like RMG, women are concentrated in lower-paying roles while men occupy supervisory and technical positions.

### Kabeer's Two Pathways

Naila Kabeer (drawing on Sen 1990) identifies two channels through which women's employment can enhance empowerment — and both require enabling conditions that growth alone does not guarantee:

| Pathway | Mechanism | Requirements Growth Alone Cannot Provide |
|---------|-----------|-----------------------------------------|
| **Societal pathway** | Visible economic contribution → society revalues women's worth → fairer resource allocation | Social norms that *acknowledge* women's contribution as valuable; media and civil society that amplify women's voices; political representation |
| **Household pathway** | Paid work → financial contribution → stronger bargaining power in household decisions | Women must have control over their earnings (not just hand over wages to male relatives); work must be perceived as a *choice*, not distress-driven; husbands must accept women's role as co-providers |

**Evidence from Bangladesh:** RMG workers have been shown to marry later, have fewer children, and exercise more voice in household spending (Kabeer 2000; Heath & Mobarak 2015). However, these gains are concentrated among formal-sector workers. Rural women working as unpaid family labour or in domestic work experience few of these empowerment benefits. The distinction between *paid work* and *any work* is critical — Kabeer argues that work must involve a wage or income that is visible and under women's control to generate bargaining power.

### The Three Channels in Detail

#### Channel 1: Labour Market Segregation

Growth in Bangladesh has been driven by RMG exports, remittances, and construction — all highly gendered sectors. Women's employment expanded primarily in RMG (60% female workforce) and agriculture (55% female). Meanwhile, men dominate construction, transport, finance, and formal manufacturing. This **horizontal segregation** means women are confined to the lowest-productivity, most precarious segments of the growth process.

Even within RMG, **vertical segregation** operates: men hold supervisory, cutting, and finishing roles while women are concentrated in sewing (the most intensive but lowest-paid task). Promotions are rare; training opportunities are limited. Growth of the sector has not automatically led to women's upward mobility within it.

#### Channel 2: Unpaid Care Work

Bangladeshi women spend an estimated 3–4 times more hours per day on unpaid domestic and care work than men (BBS Time Use Survey 2021). This time burden:
- Reduces women's ability to take on paid employment (or limits them to part-time, informal, home-based work)
- Compresses women's leisure, rest, and education time
- Transmits intergenerational disadvantage — daughters are pulled out of school to help with care

Growth has not reduced this gap because care is treated as a private (household) rather than public (social) responsibility. Public investment in childcare infrastructure in Bangladesh remains negligible. Without policy intervention, growth can *increase* the double burden by drawing women into paid work while leaving unpaid work distribution unchanged.

#### Channel 3: Social Norms

Even as women enter the labour force in greater numbers, patriarchal norms persist. Studies from Bangladesh show:
- Women's mobility remains restricted — many RMG workers live in hostels with curfews and chaperonage
- Marriage remains universal and early (though age at marriage is rising)
- Women's earnings are often controlled by male relatives
- "Izzat" (honour) norms restrict women's work choices — certain occupations (e.g., selling in markets) are considered inappropriate

These norms are "sticky" — they change slowly and unevenly. Growth alone does not eliminate them; in fact, rising incomes can *strengthen* patriarchal norms as families use female seclusion to signal social status (the "U-curve" mechanism described by Goldin).

### Policy Implications

If growth alone cannot ensure gender equity, what is required? Five categories of intervention:

| Policy Area | Specific Measures | Example |
|-------------|------------------|---------|
| **Labour market** | Equal pay enforcement, anti-discrimination laws, skills training for women in non-traditional sectors | Bangladesh's National Women Development Policy 2011 (weakly enforced) |
| **Care infrastructure** | Public investment in childcare, eldercare, maternity/paternity leave | Nordic model — universal childcare enables high FLFP |
| **Social protection** | Cash transfers conditioned on girls' education, health; pension coverage for informal workers | Bangladesh's female stipend programme for secondary education |
| **Legal reform** | Marriage age enforcement, inheritance rights, property rights, workplace sexual harassment laws | Bangladesh amended marriage age (2017) but enforcement remains weak |
| **Norms change** | Community-based programmes engaging men and boys; media campaigns; women's collectives | BRAC's community empowerment groups; Shomobay (cooperative) models |

### Comparative Cases

| Country/Region | Growth Experience | Gender Equity Outcome | What Made the Difference |
|----------------|------------------|----------------------|-------------------------|
| **South Korea** | Rapid export-led growth (1960s–1990s) | FLFP rose with growth, but wage gap narrowed slowly; now among highest FLFP in Asia | State investment in education; later, childcare expansion; strong enforcement of equal pay |
| **Bangladesh** | >6% growth, RMG-led | FLFP rose but informal work dominant, wage gap wide | RMG created formal jobs but social norms and weak enforcement limit equity gains |
| **India** | 6–7% growth (post-1991) | FLFP *declined* from 31% to 23% (2005–2022) — growth coincided with women *leaving* the labour force | Rising household incomes allowed withdrawal; lack of suitable jobs; social stigma against women's work |
| **Nordic countries** | Moderate growth, high social spending | Among the highest gender equity globally — high FLFP, narrow wage gaps, strong political representation | Not growth but *deliberate policy* — universal childcare, parental leave, quota laws |

India's declining FLFP during a period of high growth is the clearest refutation of the "growth ensures equity" thesis. It demonstrates that without structural transformation that creates appropriate jobs for women, and without shifts in social norms, growth can be accompanied by *worsening* gender outcomes.

### Integration with Development Theory

This question reveals a deeper fault line in development economics:

| Framework | Prediction | Outcome in Practice |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Neoclassical growth theory** | Factor accumulation (capital, labour) determines growth; equity follows from growth (trickle-down) | Markets reproduce existing inequalities; women's labour is allocated to low-productivity sectors |
| **Capabilities approach** (Sen, Nussbaum) | Development is expansion of freedoms; equity requires deliberate effort to remove capability deprivations | Gender equity requires targeted action because structural constraints are not market-correcting |
| **Feminist economics** (Kabeer, Agarwal, Elson) | Gender inequality is embedded in economic institutions (markets, households, states); growth can reinforce or challenge these depending on policy | Growth outcomes are contingent on institutional context — "growth is not gender-neutral" (Elson 1995) |
| **Structuralist / Dependency** | Global division of labour assigns women to low-wage, flexible, export-oriented work; growth can intensify exploitation | Bangladesh RMG: growth created jobs but within a global value chain that limits women's bargaining power |

> **Professor's Corner:** The strongest answers to this question make two moves. First, they distinguish between *growth* (quantitative expansion of output) and *development* (qualitative improvement in human capabilities) — a distinction at the heart of the capabilities approach. Second, they recognise that gender equity is not a single dimension but multiple: economic, social, political, bodily. Growth may improve one dimension (e.g., employment) while leaving others unchanged (e.g., household decision-making) or even worsening them (e.g., time poverty). The best evidence for your argument is the feminisation U-hypothesis combined with India's declining FLFP — both show that the relationship between growth and gender outcomes is *non-linear* and *context-dependent*.

## Exam Tips

1. **Part A (6 marks):** Be precise and systematic. The table is your best tool — make it comprehensive. Link to the capabilities approach explicitly (Sen and Nussbaum). Use Kabeer's three types of equality (opportunity, agency, outcomes) to show depth.

2. **Part B (14 marks):** Start with your thesis — "No" — stated directly. Then organise around the three channels (labour markets, unpaid care, norms). Use the feminisation U-hypothesis as your cross-country anchor and Bangladesh data (42.77% LFPR, 87% informal, 30-40% wage gap) as your country-specific evidence. End with policy implications and compare with at least one other country (India's declining FLFP is the strongest contrast). Mention Kabeer's two pathways to show theoretical grounding.

3. **Key names to drop:** Goldin (U-hypothesis), Kabeer (agency framework, three equalities), Sen & Nussbaum (capabilities), Kandiyoti (classical patriarchy), Elson (gender-neutrality of growth).

4. **Data to memorise:** Bangladesh FLFP 42.77% (2022), 87% informal, 30-40% wage gap, India FLFP decline from 31% to 23%.

5. **Common mistakes to avoid:**
   - Conflating equality and equity (Part A tests this directly — do not use them interchangeably)
   - Arguing that growth is irrelevant (it creates conditions but is insufficient — never say it has no effect)
   - Ignoring Bangladesh evidence (the question requires examples — Bangladesh is your best case)
   - Describing problems without policy solutions (end with what should be done)
