## Part A — The Global Gender Gap Index: Concept and Methodology (8 marks)

### Definition

The **Global Gender Gap Index (GGGI)** is a composite index published annually since 2006 by the **World Economic Forum (WEF)**. It measures the relative disparities between women and men across four critical dimensions of socio-economic life. Critically, the index is designed to capture **gaps** in outcomes between genders, not the **absolute levels** of development or wellbeing. A country where both men and women are equally poorly educated may score well on the index despite poor absolute outcomes, because the *gap* between them is small.

### The Four Sub-Indices

The GGGI is composed of four sub-indices, each capturing a distinct dimension of gender equality:

| Sub-Index | Components | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic Participation and Opportunity** | Labour force participation rate (gap ratio); wage equality for similar work (survey-based); estimated earned income (PPP US$); legislators, senior officials & managers; professional & technical workers | 0.25 |
| **Educational Attainment** | Literacy rate; enrolment in primary, secondary, and tertiary education | 0.25 |
| **Health and Survival** | Sex ratio at birth (converted to gap); healthy life expectancy | 0.25 |
| **Political Empowerment** | Women in parliament; women in ministerial positions; years with female head of state (last 50 years) | 0.25 |

### Methodology and Score Calculation

1. **Data collection:** Each component is drawn from internationally comparable sources — ILO, UNESCO, WHO, UNDP, and Inter-Parliamentary Union.

2. **Ratio conversion:** For every component, a female-to-male ratio is computed:
   \[
   \text{Ratio}_i = \frac{\text{Female value}_i}{\text{Male value}_i}
   \]

3. **Truncation and winsorisation:** Ratios are truncated at a **0–1 (or greater) ceiling**. For most components, the equality benchmark is 1 (equal outcomes). For the sex ratio at birth, a special transformation is applied because the natural sex ratio (approximately 1.06 males per female at birth) must be accounted for — deviations toward male bias are penalised.

4. **Sub-index score:** Each sub-index score is the weighted average of its component ratios. For the Health and Survival sub-index, a minimum cap is applied to avoid excessively penalising countries with natural demographic variation.

5. **Final GGGI score:** The overall GGGI score is the **simple (unweighted) average** of the four sub-index scores:
   \[
   \text{GGGI} = \frac{1}{4} \bigl(\text{Economic} + \text{Education} + \text{Health} + \text{Political}\bigr)
   \]

   The score ranges from **0 (complete gender disparity) to 1 (complete gender parity)**.

6. **Ranking:** Countries are ranked by their GGGI score. The index thus provides a **cross-country comparative benchmark** of how well nations are closing gender gaps, but it does **not** rank countries by women's absolute wellbeing.

### Key Conceptual Features

- **Gap-focused, not outcome-focused:** As noted earlier, a low-income country with uniformly poor male and female outcomes can rank higher than a country where women are better off in absolute terms but the gender gap is wider.
- **Relative, not absolute:** Scores reflect women's outcomes *relative* to men's, not against any absolute standard.
- **No penalty for symmetry at the bottom:** If both men and women are equally disadvantaged in a dimension, the gap ratio approaches 1, yielding a high score that may be misleading.

## Part B — Does Bangladesh's GGGI Rank Overstate True Gender Equity? (12 marks)

### Position: AGREE — Bangladesh's rank substantially overstates the country's real advancement towards gender equity.

Bangladesh has consistently ranked among the top-performing South Asian countries in the WEF's Global Gender Gap Index, and in recent years has outperformed several wealthier Asian economies. At first glance this appears encouraging. However, a deeper interrogation of both the index methodology and the structural realities of Bangladeshi women's lives reveals a more sobering picture. I argue that **Bangladesh's relatively high GGGI rank is an artefact of the index's design as much as it is a measure of genuine progress**, and it significantly overstates the substantive achievement of gender equity.

---

### Argument 1: The GGGI Measures Gaps, Not Absolute Outcomes — A Structural Blind Spot

The most fundamental weakness of the GGGI when applied to Bangladesh is that it measures **relative gaps rather than absolute outcomes**. If both men and women in Bangladesh face poor educational quality, low health entitlements, and restricted economic opportunity, the *gap* between them may be narrow even while *both* are disadvantaged in absolute terms. Bangladesh's near-parity in primary education enrolment (a gap ratio close to 1) is an achievement — but it does not capture the reality that learning outcomes for both boys and girls remain low by international standards. As Naila Kabeer (2005) has argued, measuring gender equality requires attention to **the substantive conditions under which women live their lives**, not merely the formal ratios. The GGGI's gap-focus silently equates 'less bad for women relative to men' with 'good for women', which is a deeply problematic substitution.

---

### Argument 2: Political Empowerment — A Dramatic Outlier That Inflates the Aggregate Score

Bangladesh's extraordinarily high score on the **Political Empowerment sub-index** is the single largest driver of its overall rank. The country has had a **female head of state for over 30 years** (Sheikh Hasina and Khaleda Zia alternating since 1991), which contributes maximally to the 'years with female head of state' component. This is a genuine achievement, but it is a **thin, elite-level indicator** that does not reflect mass-level political participation.

Consider the other components of this sub-index:

| Indicator | Bangladesh | Comparable |
|---|---|---|
| Women in parliament (2024 est.) | ~21% | Below global average (~27%) |
| Women in ministerial positions | ~10–12% | Well below global average |
| Years with female head of state (last 50) | ~34 years | Near-maximum score |

The 'years with female head of state' component pulls the sub-index score upwards dramatically, but this metric captures a **single, historically contingent fact** — the emergence of two dominant female political dynasties — rather than a structural transformation of political power. Grassroots political participation by women, representation in local government beyond reserved seats, and influence over policy-making remain severely constrained. The index does not capture that many women in parliament occupy reserved seats with limited substantive power, nor does it reflect the persistence of patriarchal gatekeeping in political parties.

---

### Argument 3: Health and Survival — Biology Masks Deprivation

Bangladesh scores near-parity on the **Health and Survival sub-index**, primarily because the sex ratio at birth (which penalises countries with strong son preference) is only moderately skewed in Bangladesh compared to India or China, and because women's healthy life expectancy exceeds men's (a universal biological pattern). This is **not primarily a policy achievement** — it reflects the well-established demographic fact that women outlive men across virtually all societies. The index's sex-ratio-at-birth component does capture some dimension of gender bias, but the overall health sub-index remains the dimension where Bangladesh's score is most **biologically inflated**.

Furthermore, the index does not capture:
- Maternal mortality (173 per 100,000 live births — among the highest in Asia)
- Child marriage rates (51% of women aged 20–24 married before 18, UNICEF)
- Nutritional deprivation (high rates of anaemia among women of reproductive age)
- Restricted access to reproductive healthcare

A gap-based measure that does not incorporate absolute health outcomes misses these fundamental dimensions of gender-based disadvantage.

---

### Argument 4: Educational Attainment — Progress That Is Real but Incomplete

Bangladesh has made genuine strides in closing the educational gender gap, particularly at the **primary level**, where the female-to-male enrolment ratio exceeds 1 due to targeted stipend programmes. However, three qualifications are necessary:

1. **Drop-off at higher levels:** The gender gap re-emerges at secondary and especially tertiary level, where female enrolment rates lag behind male rates in many disciplines.
2. **Quality of education:** Enrolment parity does not mean quality parity. Girls and boys alike face poor learning outcomes, and the index does not measure **learning-adjusted** gender gaps.
3. **Labour market disconnect:** Most critically, educational gains have **not translated into commensurate economic opportunities** — the very next point.

---

### Argument 5: Economic Participation — The Largest and Most Persistent Gap

This sub-index reveals Bangladesh's deepest gender inequality, and it is precisely the dimension where Bangladesh's overall rank is most deceptive.

| Indicator | Female | Male | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labour Force Participation Rate | 42.77% | ~80% | ~0.53 |
| Informal employment share | ~87% | ~80% | Widening vulnerability |
| Wage gap (estimated) | — | — | 30–40% less than men |
| Legislators, senior officials & managers | ~12% | ~88% | Deeply skewed |
| Professional & technical workers | ~35% | ~65% | Moderate gap |

**Key structural issues the index obscures:**

- **Quality of employment:** 87% of employed women work in the **informal sector** — in domestic work, subsistence agriculture, or the ready-made garment (RMG) sector — where wages are low, job security absent, and labour rights minimal. The index counts these as 'economic participation' without distinguishing between empowering employment and precarious survival work.
- **Unpaid care burden:** Bangladeshi women spend **3–4 times as many hours** on unpaid care and domestic work as men. The GGGI's labour force participation component does not account for the double burden faced by employed women, nor does it recognise that unpaid work constitutes a massive unmeasured economic contribution.
- **Wage discrimination:** Even within the formal sector, women earn 30–40% less than men for comparable work, a gap the index captures only through a single perception-based question ('wage equality for similar work').
- **Occupational segregation:** Women are concentrated in a narrow band of occupations (garments, teaching, NGOs) and remain severely underrepresented in management, STEM, and decision-making roles.

---

### Argument 6: What the Index Leaves Out Entirely

The GGGI, by design, covers only four measurable dimensions of gender inequality. Equally critical dimensions are entirely absent, and their omission systematically overstates Bangladesh's performance:

| Missing Dimension | Bangladesh Reality |
|---|---|
| **Violence against women** | 50%+ of ever-married women report experiencing intimate partner violence (BDHS); no legal prohibition of marital rape |
| **Legal barriers** | Personal laws (Muslim Family Ordinance) discriminate in marriage, divorce, and inheritance; child marriage 'exceptions' in law |
| **Social norms and agency** | Only 28% of women participate in household financial decisions (BDHS 2022); son preference persists despite fertility decline |
| **Asset ownership** | Women own less than 10% of agricultural land; property rights remain deeply gendered |
| **Time poverty** | Women work longer total hours (paid + unpaid) than men, leaving less time for education, leisure, or political participation |

Drawing on **Kabeer's (1999) framework of 'resources, agency, and achievements'**, the GGGI captures only a narrow slice of the 'achievements' dimension — and only those achievements that can be expressed as female-to-male ratios. It says nothing about women's **agency** (the ability to make strategic life choices — about marriage, work, mobility, or political voice) or the **structural resources** (legal status, property rights, freedom from violence) that underpin genuine empowerment.

---

### Counterargument: What the Rank Does Capture Correctly

A balanced assessment must acknowledge that Bangladesh *has* made genuine progress. The closure of the primary education gap, the longevity of women's political representation at the highest level, and the significant (if precarious) expansion of women's formal employment in the RMG sector represent real — if incomplete — advances. Bangladesh ranks **first in South Asia** on the GGGI for good reason, and this is not meaningless. Moreover, the index has been useful in drawing policy attention to gender gaps across multiple dimensions.

---

### Conclusion: Useful but Incomplete Benchmark

**Bangladesh's GGGI rank is a partial and inflated signal of gender equity progress.** The index captures real improvements in specific domains (education enrolment, elite political representation) but systematically overstates progress because:

1. It measures **gaps not absolute outcomes** — a design feature that rewards countries where both genders are equally disadvantaged.
2. The **Political Empowerment sub-index is inflated** by a single outlier indicator (30+ years of female head of state) that does not reflect mass-level political inclusion.
3. The **Health sub-index reflects biological advantage** more than policy success.
4. The **Economic Participation sub-index**, where Bangladesh performs worst, is also the dimension most poorly captured — it ignores the quality, security, and dignity of women's employment, as well as the unpaid care burden.
5. Entire domains of gendered disadvantage — **violence, legal discrimination, social norms, agency, time poverty** — fall entirely outside the index framework.

As Kabeer (2005) reminds us, measuring gender equality requires moving beyond formal ratios to an understanding of whether women have the **capabilities and agency** to live lives they have reason to value. By that standard, Bangladesh's journey toward gender equity remains far more incomplete than the GGGI rank suggests. The index is a useful comparative tool, but it is no substitute for the granular, context-sensitive analysis that genuine policy-making requires.

**Verdict:** Bangladesh's relatively high GGGI rank **substantially overstates** the country's true advancement towards gender equity. The rank reflects a combination of real-but-limited progress, methodological artefacts, and the absence of critical dimensions from the index's framework.
