## 1. Overview: Eight Schools of Thought

Development economics has been shaped by several major schools of thought, each offering different explanations for why some countries grow and others stagnate. Todaro & Smith (Ch. 3) identify four classic approaches: (1) linear-stages-of-growth models, (2) structural-change theories, (3) the international-dependence revolution, and (4) the neoclassical counterrevolution. This answer extends beyond these four to cover the deeper historical roots in classical and Marxian thought, as well as the more recent endogenous growth and new institutional economics frameworks.

The eight schools covered here span over two centuries of economic thinking:

| Period | School | Core Focus |
|--------|--------|------------|
| Late 1700s-1800s | Classical (Smith, Ricardo, Malthus) | Land, labour, capital; diminishing returns; population checks |
| Mid-1800s | Marxian | Mode of production; surplus value; falling rate of profit |
| 1940s-1950s | Harrod-Domar | Savings-investment link; capital-output ratio |
| 1956+ | Solow-Swan Neoclassical | Diminishing returns; exogenous technology; steady-state convergence |
| 1954+ | Lewis Structural Transformation | Two-sector surplus-labour model |
| 1960 | Rostow's Stages | Five sequential stages of growth |
| 1986+ | Endogenous Growth | Technology endogeneity; human capital; R&D spillovers |
| 1990+ | New Institutional Economics | Institutions as deep determinant of growth |

## 2. Classical Growth Theory

### 2.1 Key Authors

- **Adam Smith** (1723-1790) -- *The Wealth of Nations* (1776)
- **David Ricardo** (1772-1823) -- *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817)
- **Thomas Malthus** (1766-1834) -- *Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798)

### 2.2 Core Mechanisms

**Adam Smith's Growth Model:**

Smith identified three factors driving economic growth:

1. **Division of labour** -- Specialisation increases productivity. Smith's pin-factory example demonstrated that dividing a task into specialised operations dramatically raises output per worker.
2. **Extension of markets** -- The division of labour is limited by the extent of the market. Larger markets enable greater specialisation.
3. **Capital accumulation** -- Investment in machinery and infrastructure expands productive capacity.

Smith's growth process was self-reinforcing: market expansion permits greater division of labour, which raises productivity, which generates surplus for capital accumulation, which further expands markets.

**David Ricardo -- Diminishing Returns and Distribution:**

Ricardo's analysis focused on the distribution of output among three social classes (landlords, capitalists, workers) and its implications for growth:

- **Diminishing returns to land** -- As population grows, cultivation extends to less fertile land, raising food costs and rents.
- **Falling rate of profit** -- As rents rise and wages are pushed to subsistence, the profit share declines, reducing the surplus available for capital accumulation.
- **Stationary state** -- Eventually, profits fall to zero, net investment ceases, and the economy reaches a stationary state with zero per capita growth.

**Malthus -- Population Trap:**

Malthus posited that population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically (due to diminishing returns to land). This creates a tendency for per capita incomes to fall to subsistence level -- the **Malthusian population trap**. Only "preventive checks" (birth control, delayed marriage) or "positive checks" (famine, disease, war) could restrain population.

### 2.3 Growth Prediction

| Aspect | Prediction |
|--------|-----------|
| Long-run growth | Zero (stationary state) -- persistent growth is unsustainable |
| Convergence | Not applicable -- all economies tend toward subsistence equilibrium |
| Role of policy | Limited; diminishing returns overwhelm policy efforts |

### 2.4 Key Limitations

- Did not anticipate sustained technological progress that could overcome diminishing returns
- Malthus failed to predict the demographic transition and fertility decline in industrialising countries
- Ricardo's focus on land as the fixed factor became less relevant as industry grew

## 3. Marxian Growth Theory

### 3.1 Key Author

- **Karl Marx** (1818-1883) -- *Das Kapital* (1867, 1885, 1894)

### 3.2 Core Mechanisms

Marx's theory of economic development is rooted in the materialist conception of history, which views economic structure as the foundation upon which political and ideological superstructures are built.

**Mode of Production:**

The "mode of production" combines:
- **Forces of production** -- Technology, tools, machinery, labour power, natural resources
- **Relations of production** -- The social relations (class relations) under which production occurs

History progresses through successive modes: primitive communism -> ancient slavery -> feudalism -> capitalism -> socialism -> communism. Each mode contains internal contradictions that lead to its eventual replacement.

**Surplus Value and Exploitation:**

- Labour power is a commodity whose use-value (ability to create value) exceeds its exchange-value (subsistence wage).
- The **surplus value** is the difference between the value produced by labour and the wages paid to labour.
- Capitalists appropriate surplus value as profit -- this is the basis of exploitation under capitalism.

**Capital Accumulation and the Falling Rate of Profit:**

- Capitalists are compelled by competition to accumulate capital and invest in labour-saving machinery.
- As the organic composition of capital rises (more machinery relative to labour), the rate of profit tends to fall.
- The falling rate of profit leads to periodic crises, rising unemployment (the "reserve army of labour"), and increasing immiseration of the working class.
- Eventually, the contradictions of capitalism lead to its revolutionary overthrow and replacement by socialism.

### 3.3 Growth Prediction

| Aspect | Prediction |
|--------|-----------|
| Long-run growth | Growth under capitalism is inherently unstable, crisis-prone |
| Convergence | No; capitalism generates increasing inequality between capitalists and workers |
| Endpoint | Revolution and transition to socialism |

### 3.4 Relevance and Criticism

- Influenced dependency theory and world-systems analysis (see Todaro & Smith SS3.4)
- The prediction of falling profit rates and increasing immiseration has not been empirically validated for developed economies
- However, Marx's analysis of capital accumulation, technological change, and crisis remains influential
- The emphasis on class conflict and institutional change prefigured later work on power and institutions in development

### 3.5 Comparison Table: Classical vs Marxian

| Feature | Classical (Smith/Ricardo/Malthus) | Marxian |
|---------|----------------------------------|---------|
| Driving force | Self-interest, market expansion | Class struggle, capital accumulation |
| Key constraint | Diminishing returns (land) | Falling rate of profit |
| Distribution | Functional (rent, wage, profit) | Exploitation (surplus value extraction) |
| Endpoint | Stationary state | Revolution -> socialism |
| View of capitalism | Progress (if markets free) | Inherently contradictory |

## 4. Harrod-Domar Growth Model (Keynesian Tradition)

### 4.1 Key Authors

- **Roy Harrod** (1900-1978) -- "An Essay in Dynamic Theory" (1939)
- **Evsey Domar** (1914-1997) -- "Capital Expansion, Rate of Growth, and Employment" (1946)

### 4.2 Core Equation and Logic

The Harrod-Domar model is the foundational **linear-stages-of-growth** model. It posits a simple relationship between growth, savings, and the capital-output ratio.

**Fundamental equation:**

$$g = \frac{s}{c}$$

where:
- $g$ = rate of growth of GDP ($\Delta Y / Y$)
- $s$ = net national savings ratio ($S/Y$)
- $c$ = capital-output ratio ($K/Y$, assumed constant)

**Derivation:**

1. Net saving: $S = sY$
2. Net investment: $I = \Delta K = c\Delta Y$  (because $K/Y = c$, so $\Delta K/\Delta Y = c$)
3. Equilibrium: $S = I$
4. Therefore: $sY = c\Delta Y$, so $\Delta Y/Y = s/c$

**Economic logic:**

- To grow, economies must save and invest a proportion of GDP.
- The growth rate equals the savings rate multiplied by the productivity of capital ($1/c$).
- Higher savings $\rightarrow$ faster growth. Lower capital-output ratio $\rightarrow$ faster growth.

### 4.3 "Knife-Edge" Instability Problem

Harrod identified a fundamental instability problem:

- **Warranted growth rate** ($g_w = s/c$): the rate that satisfies savers and investors, keeping the economy on a steady growth path.
- **Natural growth rate** ($g_n = n + \lambda$): the maximum growth rate the labour force and technology permit.
- **Actual growth rate** ($g$): what actually occurs.

If $g \neq g_w$, the economy diverges rather than converges:
- If $g > g_w$: actual growth exceeds the warranted rate, investment is too high, leading to further acceleration (boom)
- If $g < g_w$: actual growth falls short, investment is insufficient, leading to further deceleration (bust)

This is the **"knife-edge" problem**: any deviation from the warranted path is self-reinforcing. The model lacks self-correcting mechanisms.

### 4.4 Policy Implications

The model was enormously influential in the 1950s-1960s development policy:

| Policy tool | Mechanism | Target |
|-------------|-----------|--------|
| Raise savings rate ($s$) | Increase $s$ in $g = s/c$ | Higher growth for same capital-output ratio |
| Lower capital-output ratio ($c$) | Improve capital productivity | More output per unit of investment |
| Fill savings gap | Foreign aid/borrowing | Supplement domestic savings to reach target $g$ |
| Technological progress | Reduces $c$ over time | Efficiency gains |

**Rostow's takeoff threshold:** Countries that can save 15-20% of GDP can achieve self-sustaining growth.

### 4.5 Limitations and Criticism (Todaro & Smith, pp. 123-124)

| Criticism | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| Capital fundamentalism | Overemphasis on capital accumulation as sufficient condition for growth |
| Ignoring structural conditions | European Marshall Plan worked because recipient countries had institutions, skilled labour, transport, etc. -- absent in many developing countries |
| Fixed capital-output ratio | In reality, $c$ is not constant; substitutability between capital and labour exists |
| Abundant labour assumption | Labour is assumed available on demand -- but skill shortages may bind |
| Neglect of capacity utilisation | In practice, much installed capacity in developing countries is underutilised |

> Todaro & Smith: "The mechanisms of development embodied in the theory of stages of growth did not always work... not because more saving and investment isn't a necessary condition... but because it is not a sufficient condition." (p. 123)

## 5. Solow-Swan Neoclassical Growth Model (1956)

### 5.1 Key Authors

- **Robert Solow** (1924-2023) -- "A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth" (1956) -- Nobel Prize 1987
- **Trevor Swan** (1918-1989) -- "Economic Growth and Capital Accumulation" (1956)

### 5.2 Core Production Function

The Solow model departs from Harrod-Domar by introducing **diminishing returns to capital** and **factor substitutability**:

$$Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}$$

where:
- $Y$ = output (GDP)
- $K$ = capital stock (physical + human)
- $L$ = labour
- $A$ = labour-augmenting technology (grows exogenously at rate $\lambda$)
- $\alpha$ = elasticity of output with respect to capital ($0 < \alpha < 1$)

Key assumptions:
- Diminishing returns to capital ($\alpha < 1$)
- Constant returns to scale
- Technology is **exogenous** (determined outside the model)
- Savings rate is exogenous and constant

### 5.3 Steady State

The **steady state** is the point where capital per worker ($k$) is constant and output per worker ($y$) is constant. It occurs when:

$$sf(k^*) = (n + \delta + \lambda)k^*$$

where:
- $s$ = savings rate
- $n$ = population growth rate
- $\delta$ = depreciation rate
- $\lambda$ = technology growth rate
- $k^*$ = steady-state capital per effective worker

**Key insight:** In the steady state, per capita income grows **only** at the rate of technological progress ($\lambda$). The savings rate affects the **level** of income, not its **long-run growth rate** -- a critical difference from Harrod-Domar.

### 5.4 Convergence Prediction

The Solow model generates the famous **convergence hypothesis**:

| Type | Definition | Assumption | 
|------|-----------|------------|
| **Absolute (unconditional) convergence** | Poor countries grow faster than rich ones, regardless of their characteristics | All countries have identical steady-state determinants ($s$, $n$, $\delta$, $\lambda$, $A$) |
| **Conditional convergence** | A country grows faster the further it is below its *own* steady state | Steady-state determinants vary across countries |

**Mechanism:** Diminishing returns to capital means a poor country (low $k$) has a high marginal product of capital, hence high growth. As $k$ rises, growth slows. All economies converge to their respective steady states, with transitional growth fastest when farthest below steady state.

### 5.5 Policy Implications

- Savings rates affect income *levels* but not long-run growth rates (level effects, not growth effects -- Debraj Ray, pp. 2245-2256).
- Long-run growth is driven entirely by **exogenous technological progress** (the "Solow residual").
- Open economies converge faster because capital flows from rich (low MPK) to poor (high MPK) countries.
- No policy implications for raising long-run growth -- technology is exogenous.

### 5.6 Empirical Evidence (MRW 1992)

Mankiw, Romer, and Weil (1992) tested the Solow model with human capital augmentation:

| Finding | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Absolute convergence | Rejected -- no significant negative relationship between initial income and growth across 98 countries |
| Conditional convergence | Supported -- ~2% per year convergence to own steady state |
| Human capital significance | Highly significant; roughly equal to physical capital |
| Model fit | Augmented Solow explains ~80% of cross-country income variation |

### 5.7 Limitations

| Limitation | Explanation |
|------------|-------------|
| Technology is exogenous | The model cannot explain *why* technological progress occurs or why it differs across countries |
| Convergence failure | Poor countries as a group have not caught up -- contradicts unconditional convergence |
| Savings endogeneity | Savings rates likely depend on income, not an independent parameter |
| No microfoundations for technical change | Technical progress is treated as "manna from heaven" |

## 6. Lewis Structural Transformation (1954)

### 6.1 Key Author

- **W. Arthur Lewis** (1915-1991) -- "Economic Development with Unlimited Supplies of Labour" (1954) -- Nobel Prize 1979

### 6.2 Core Two-Sector Model

The Lewis model is a **structural-change theory** that explains how underdeveloped economies transform from subsistence agriculture to modern industrial production.

**Two sectors:**

| Feature | Traditional (Agriculture) | Modern (Industrial) |
|---------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Labour productivity | Low (marginal product = 0) | High |
| Wage determination | Average product (shared output) | Marginal product |
| Labour supply | Unlimited (surplus labour) | Demanded at fixed wage |
| Capital | Fixed, traditional | Accumulates via reinvested profits |
| Technology | Traditional, unchanging | Modern, improving |

**Mechanism of labour transfer (Todaro & Smith, pp. 124-129):**

1. Surplus labour exists in agriculture ($MPL_A = 0$) -- workers can be withdrawn without reducing output.
2. Modern-sector wage ($W_M$) is set at a premium above average rural income ($W_A$), attracting labour.
3. At $W_M$, labour supply to the modern sector is perfectly elastic (horizontal supply curve).
4. Capitalists in the modern sector reinvest all profits, expanding capital stock ($K_{M1} \rightarrow K_{M2} \rightarrow K_{M3}$).
5. This shifts the labour demand curve outward ($D_1 \rightarrow D_2 \rightarrow D_3$), increasing modern-sector employment.
6. The process continues until all surplus rural labour is absorbed -- the **Lewis turning point**.

### 6.3 The Lewis Turning Point

At the Lewis turning point:
- The agricultural sector's marginal product becomes positive.
- Further labour withdrawals reduce agricultural output, raising food prices.
- The labour supply curve to the modern sector becomes positively sloped.
- Wages begin to rise.

After the turning point, the economy has completed its structural transformation -- the balance of economic activity shifts from traditional agriculture to modern industry.

### 6.4 Criticisms (Todaro & Smith, pp. 127-129)

| Criticism | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| Laboursaving bias of technology | Modern-sector investment may be in capital-intensive equipment that creates few jobs (see Figure 3.2) |
| Rural surplus labour may not exist | Empirical evidence suggests $MPL_A > 0$ in most developing countries |
| Urban wages rise prematurely | Institutional factors (unions, civil service scales, MNC practices) push urban wages up even with high unemployment |
| Capital flight | Profits may be repatriated abroad rather than reinvested locally |
| Increasing returns in industry | Lewis assumed diminishing returns, but much industrial production exhibits increasing returns |

Despite these limitations, the Lewis model remains influential for understanding the growth experiences of China and other East Asian economies where labour transfer from agriculture to manufacturing has been central (Todaro & Smith, p. 129).

## 7. Rostow's Stages of Growth (1960)

### 7.1 Key Author

- **Walt W. Rostow** (1916-2003) -- *The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-Communist Manifesto* (1960)

### 7.2 The Five Stages

Rostow's linear-stages theory posits that all countries pass through five sequential stages of development:

| Stage | Description | Key Characteristics |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|
| **1. Traditional Society** | Pre-Newtonian science and technology; subsistence agriculture; limited social mobility | High share of agriculture; hierarchical social structure; low productivity |
| **2. Preconditions for Take-off** | Transitional period; new ideas emerge; education spreads; financial institutions develop | Commercial agriculture; infrastructure investment; centralised state |
| **3. Take-off** | The "great watershed" -- rapid, self-sustaining growth begins | Investment rises to 10%+ of GDP; leading sector drives growth; institutional framework modernises |
| **4. Drive to Maturity** | Economy extends modern technology across all sectors; sustained growth for ~60 years | New leading sectors emerge; economy becomes more diversified; labour force shifts to industry |
| **5. Age of High Mass Consumption** | Economy shifts toward consumer durables and services; welfare state develops | High per capita income; mass production of consumer goods; service sector dominance |

### 7.3 Key Mechanisms

**Investment threshold:** Rostow defined take-off as the period when the investment rate rises from 5% to 10% or more of national income. At this threshold, growth becomes self-sustaining (citing the Harrod-Domar logic: $g = s/c$ with $s$ rising to 15-20%).

**Leading sectors:** Each stage is driven by leading sectors that stimulate growth through backward and forward linkages (e.g., railroads in the US, textiles in Britain).

**Savings mobilisation:** The key policy implication is that raising the savings rate is the central challenge of development.

### 7.4 Criticisms

| Criticism | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| Historically descriptive, not analytical | The sequences describe Western development but do not explain why some countries get stuck or skip stages |
| Not universally applicable | Developing countries today face different conditions (colonial legacy, technology gaps, global markets) than those faced by early industrialisers |
| Capital fundamentalism | Overemphasis on savings rate; ignores institutional, social, and political prerequisites |
| Eurocentric bias | Assumes all development follows the Western path |
| Take-off not empirically verified | Many countries have achieved high savings rates without sustained growth; others grew without the predicted preconditions |

> Todaro & Smith: "The Marshall Plan worked for Europe because the European countries receiving aid possessed the necessary structural, institutional, and attitudinal conditions... Yet, in many cases, they are lacking." (p. 123)

## 8. Endogenous Growth Theory (Romer 1986, Lucas 1988)

### 8.1 Key Authors

- **Paul Romer** (b. 1955) -- "Increasing Returns and Long-Run Growth" (1986) -- Nobel Prize 2018
- **Robert Lucas** (b. 1937) -- "On the Mechanics of Economic Development" (1988) -- Nobel Prize 1995
- **Robert Barro** (b. 1944) -- "Government Spending in a Simple Model of Endogenous Growth" (1990)

### 8.2 Core Mechanism: Why Technology Is Endogenous

Endogenous growth theory was developed to address the central weakness of the Solow model: the **exogeneity of technological progress**. If technology determines long-run growth, and technology is exogenous, then economic policy cannot affect long-run growth -- an unsatisfactory conclusion.

**Key innovations:**

1. **Non-diminishing returns to capital** -- By including human capital and knowledge, the effective "capital" stock may not face diminishing returns.
2. **Knowledge spillovers** -- One firm's investment in knowledge benefits other firms (positive externalities), offsetting diminishing returns.
3. **Increasing returns to scale** -- In aggregate, the production function may exhibit increasing returns because knowledge is a non-rival, partially excludable good.

**The AK formulation:**

$$Y = AK$$

where:
- $K$ includes **both** physical and human capital
- $A$ captures technology level and productivity
- There are **no diminishing returns** -- a radical departure from Solow

### 8.3 The Romer Model (Todaro & Smith, Appendix 3.3)

Romer's model assumes:

$$Y_i = AK_i^{\alpha}L_i^{1-\alpha}\bar{K}^{\beta}$$

where $\bar{K}$ is the economy-wide capital stock that generates knowledge spillovers to individual firms. With symmetry across industries:

$$Y = AK^{\alpha+\beta}L^{1-\alpha}$$

Without spillovers ($\beta = 0$), per capita growth is zero (Solow case). With positive spillovers ($\beta > 0$), per capita growth is positive and **endogenous** -- driven by investment decisions.

**Growth rate:**

$$g - n = \frac{\beta n}{1 - \alpha - \beta}$$

### 8.4 Growth Predictions

| Aspect | Endogenous Growth | Solow Model |
|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| Long-run growth source | Endogenous (investment in knowledge, R&D, human capital) | Exogenous (technology) |
| Returns to capital | Constant or increasing | Diminishing |
| Convergence | No; divergence possible | Yes (conditional) |
| Savings effect on growth | Growth effect (permanent) | Level effect only |
| Role of policy | Active -- can affect growth via R&D subsidies, education spending | Limited |

### 8.5 Policy Implications

| Policy | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|
| R&D subsidies | Increase knowledge creation, raise growth |
| Education investment | Builds human capital, raises productivity of physical capital |
| IPR protection | Incentivises innovation (but may also inhibit technology diffusion) |
| Industrial policy | Target knowledge-intensive industries with spillover potential |
| Infrastructure | Complementary investment raises returns to private capital |

### 8.6 Criticisms

- Remains dependent on neoclassical assumptions (single sector, symmetrical production) inappropriate for developing countries (Todaro & Smith, pp. 162-163)
- Ignores structural change -- the crucial reallocation of labour during development
- Overlooks institutional inefficiencies (poor infrastructure, imperfect markets) that impede growth
- Limited empirical support -- predictions are difficult to test
- Pack (1994): "Endogenous growth theory: Intellectual appeal and empirical shortcomings"

## 9. New Institutional Economics (North 1990, Acemoglu 2001)

### 9.1 Key Authors

- **Douglass North** (1920-2015) -- *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990) -- Nobel Prize 1993
- **Daron Acemoglu** (b. 1967) -- "The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development" (2001); *Why Nations Fail* (2012)
- **Simon Johnson** (b. 1963) and **James Robinson** (b. 1960) -- Co-authors with Acemoglu

### 9.2 Core Argument

Institutions are the **"rules of the game"** in society -- the formal rules (laws, constitutions, property rights) and informal constraints (norms, customs, traditions) that shape human interaction. The central proposition of new institutional economics is:

> **Institutions shape economic incentives, and therefore determine long-run economic performance.**

**North's framework:**

- Well-functioning institutions reduce **transaction costs** and **uncertainty**.
- Secure **property rights** encourage investment and innovation.
- **Contract enforcement** enables specialisation and exchange.
- **Inclusive institutions** (broad participation, rule of law) promote sustained growth.
- **Extractive institutions** (elite control, weak property rights) generate stagnation.

### 9.3 Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2001): Colonial Origins

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001) provided influential empirical evidence by studying the long-run effects of colonial institutions:

| Institutional type | Settler mortality (low) | Settler mortality (high) |
|-------------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Institutions created | Inclusive (property rights, rule of law) | Extractive (forced labour, expropriation) |
| Long-run outcome | High growth (e.g., USA, Australia, Canada) | Low growth (e.g., Congo, Peru, Bolivia) |
| Key mechanism | Settlers stayed and built institutions for themselves | Small elite extracted resources, leaving weak institutions |

**Identification strategy:** Used **settler mortality rates** as an instrument for institutional quality. Higher settler mortality led to extractive institutions, which persist to the present day, explaining current income differences.

**The "reversal of fortune":** Among former colonies, those that were richest in 1500 (e.g., Mexico, Peru, India) are now relatively poor, while those that were poorest (e.g., USA, Canada, Australia) are now rich. The cause: colonial powers set up extractive institutions in wealthy, densely populated areas (where exploitation was profitable) and inclusive institutions in poor, sparsely populated areas (where settlers had to build from scratch).

### 9.4 Growth Predictions

| Aspect | Prediction |
|--------|-----------|
| Source of growth | Good institutions (property rights, rule of law, contract enforcement) |
| Convergence | Not automatic; countries with poor institutions remain poor |
| Divergence | Can persist indefinitely if extractive institutions are locked in |
| Policy implication | Institutional reform is the fundamental prerequisite for sustained growth |

### 9.5 Relationship to Other Theories

Institutional economics does not replace growth theory but provides a **deeper determinant** explanation:

Solow/Endogenous growth: $Y = f(K, L, H, A)$ -- these are **proximate** causes of growth.

Institutional economics: Institutions determine the rates of capital accumulation, human capital investment, and productivity growth -- institutions are **fundamental** causes.

### 9.6 Criticisms

- Institutions are endogenous -- they emerge from economic development, not just cause it
- Reverse causality: richer countries may afford better institutions
- Measurement challenges: how to quantify "institutional quality"
- Neglects geography and other deep determinants
- Colonial-origins instrument remains debated

## 10. Comparative Summary Tables

### 10.1 Master Comparison: All Eight Schools

| School | Key Author(s) | Core Mechanism | Growth Engine | Diminishing Returns? | Convergence? | Role of Policy |
|--------|--------------|----------------|---------------|---------------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Classical** | Smith, Ricardo, Malthus | Division of labour; population checks | Capital accumulation, market expansion | Yes (land) | No (stationary state) | Limited |
| **Marxian** | Marx | Surplus value, capital accumulation | Capitalist competition, exploitation | Yes (falling profit rate) | No (increasing inequality) | Revolution |
| **Harrod-Domar** | Harrod, Domar | $g = s/c$ | Capital accumulation | No (constant $K/Y$) | No ("knife-edge") | Raise $s$, lower $c$ |
| **Solow-Swan** | Solow, Swan | $Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}$ | Exogenous technology | Yes (capital) | Conditional | Level effects only |
| **Lewis** | Lewis | Labour transfer agriculture $\rightarrow$ industry | Capital accumulation in modern sector | No (unlimited labour supply) | Structural transformation | Promote modern sector |

| **New Institutional** | North, Acemoglu | Institutions shape incentives | Good institutions | Context-dependent | No (institutional persistence) | Institutional reform |

### 10.2 Growth Predictions: Convergence vs Divergence

| School | Predicts Convergence? | Condition | Empirical Status |
|--------|----------------------|-----------|------------------|
| Classical | Implicitly (all to stationary state) | -- | Not observed |
| Marxian | No -- predicts polarisation | -- | Ambiguous |
| Harrod-Domar | Not a core prediction | -- | Not testable in simple form |
| Solow-Swan | Yes -- **conditional** | Identical $s, n, \delta, \lambda$ | Supported (MRW 1992) |
| Solow-Swan | Yes -- **absolute** | All parameters identical | Rejected |
| Lewis | Implicitly (structural transformation) | Unlimited labour supply | Supported for China, East Asia |
| Rostow | Yes (all countries pass same stages) | -- | Not supported |
| Endogenous | No -- divergence possible | Differences in R&D, human capital | Supported (limited) |
| New Institutional | No -- institutional persistence | Colonial/extractive legacy | Strong empirical support |

### 10.3 Policy Implications Summary

| School | Key Policy Prescription |
|--------|------------------------|
| Classical | Remove barriers to trade and specialisation |
| Marxian | Overthrow capitalism; establish socialist planning |
| Harrod-Domar | Increase domestic savings; fill savings gap with foreign aid |
| Solow-Swan | Promote free movement of capital; education (level effects) |
| Lewis | Promote modern-sector investment; manage urban-rural terms of trade |
| Rostow | Mobilise 10%+ savings rate; target leading sectors |
| Endogenous | Subsidise R&D; invest in education; protect IPR |
| New Institutional | Secure property rights; enforce contracts; build inclusive institutions |

### 10.4 Key Equations Comparison

| Model | Core Equation | Meaning |
|-------|--------------|---------|
| Harrod-Domar | $g = s/c$ | Growth = savings rate / capital-output ratio |
| Solow-Swan | $Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}$ | Cobb-Douglas production with labour-augmenting technology |
| Solow steady state | $sf(k^*) = (n + \delta + \lambda)k^*$ | Investment = break-even investment at steady state |
| MRW augmented Solow | $Y = K^{\alpha}H^{\beta}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta}$ | Three-factor production with human capital |

| Romer spillover | $Y = AK^{\alpha+\beta}L^{1-\alpha}$ | Capital externality generates increasing returns |
| Lewis labour transfer | $W_M = W_A + \text{premium}$ | Urban wage = rural average product + premium |

## 12. Professor's Corner

### Why So Many Theories?

The existence of multiple growth theories is not a sign of failure in economics but a reflection of the **complexity of development**. No single model captures all dimensions of the growth process. Each theory illuminates different aspects:

- **Harrod-Domar** correctly highlighted that capital matters, even if it is not sufficient.
- **Solow** correctly highlighted diminishing returns, even if technology is not truly exogenous.
- **Lewis** correctly highlighted structural transformation, even if the labour-surplus assumption does not fit all countries.
- **Endogenous growth** correctly highlighted knowledge spillovers, even if it underplays institutional constraints.
- **New institutional economics** correctly highlighted that incentives matter, even if institutions are hard to measure.

### The Big Debate: Proximate vs Fundamental Causes

A useful way to organise these theories is the distinction between **proximate** and **fundamental** causes of growth:

| Level | What It Explains | Which Theories |
|-------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Proximate** | How much output from given inputs | Solow, Harrod-Domar, AK models |
| **Fundamental** | Why some countries accumulate more inputs and use them more productively | Institutions, geography, culture |

Acemoglu (2009): "Differences in income per capita across countries are ultimately due to differences in institutions." This places new institutional economics as the deepest layer of explanation.

### The Convergence Debate: What We Know

Three decades of empirical research have established:

1. **Absolute convergence does NOT hold** across the full sample of world countries.
2. **Conditional convergence DOES hold** -- controlling for savings, population growth, human capital, and institutions, poorer countries grow faster.
3. **Convergence clubs** exist -- groups of countries with similar characteristics converge among themselves but not across groups.
4. **Speed of convergence** is approximately 2% per year (MRW), implying a half-life of ~35 years.
5. **Divergence** is possible and observed when fundamental determinants (institutions, geography) differ sufficiently.
