## (A) What is 'IMF Conditionality'? — 5 Marks

### 1. Definition

IMF conditionality refers to the **policy conditions** that the International Monetary Fund attaches to its financial assistance (loans) to member countries. These conditions require the borrowing country to implement specific economic policies and reforms as a prerequisite for accessing loan disbursements. The formal justification is that conditionality:

1. **Ensures repayment capacity** — reforms restore macroeconomic stability so the country can service its debt
2. **Reduces moral hazard** — prevents countries from relying indefinitely on IMF resources without adjusting underlying imbalances
3. **Provides a policy anchor** — external commitment device helps governments overcome domestic political resistance to needed reforms

The legal basis for conditionality is found in the IMF's **Articles of Agreement** (Article I(v), Article V, Section 3) and has been elaborated through successive guidelines (1979, 2002, 2009 — the most recent framework emphasising "country ownership" and streamlined conditions).

### 2. Types of Conditionality Conditions

IMF programmes employ a layered framework of conditions, each with different force and monitoring mechanisms:

| Condition Type | Binding Force | Description | Example |
|----------------|---------------|-------------|---------|
| **Prior Actions** | Most binding — must be completed *before* Board approval or disbursement | Non-negotiable steps taken before the loan is approved | Pass a revised budget law; devalue the currency; establish a revenue authority |
| **Quantitative Performance Criteria (QPCs)** | Binding — non-observance triggers automatic loan suspension | Specific numeric targets for key macroeconomic variables, assessed quarterly | Floor on net international reserves (NIR) ≥ $X; ceiling on fiscal deficit ≤ Y% of GDP; ceiling on central bank credit to government |
| **Structural Benchmarks** | Binding but with more flexibility | Policy actions that signal reform progress (legislative steps, institutional changes) | Submit a financial sector reform bill to parliament; implement a new VAT regime; adopt a unified exchange rate |
| **Indicative Targets** | Non-binding — tracked for monitoring | Targets for variables influenced indirectly or subject to exogenous shocks | Inflation target; social spending floor; poverty reduction spending target |
| **Programme Reviews** | Gatekeeping mechanism | Quarterly/semi-annual comprehensive assessments combine QPCs and benchmarks; if conditions are met, the next tranche is released | July 2023 review of Bangladesh ECF/EFF performance |
| **Consultation Clause** | Procedural | Required when policies deviate from programme parameters; may trigger renegotiation | Waiver requests for missed QPCs (as in Bangladesh, 2025, for exchange restrictions) |

### 3. Historical Evolution of Conditionality

| Period | Dominant Approach | Key Features |
|--------|-------------------|--------------|
| **1950s–1970s** | Light conditionality | Stand-By Arrangements (SBAs) with few conditions; focused on monetary/fiscal aggregates |
| **1980s** | Structural Adjustment (Washington Consensus) | Proliferation of conditions — up to 140 per programme — covering trade liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, financial sector reform |
| **1990s** | Poverty focus post-Asian Crisis | Introduction of Poverty Reduction and Growth Facility (PRGF); Poverty Reduction Strategy Papers (PRSPs) required |
| **2000s** | Streamlining initiative | Post-2002 guidelines reduce condition count; emphasis on "country ownership"; fewer structural conditions |
| **2010s–present** | Flexible, tailored approach | New facilities: Rapid Financing Instrument (RFI), Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF) for climate; more focus on social protection floors; differentiated conditions for LICs vs EMs |

> **Theoretical rationale (1 mark worth):** Conditionality addresses a fundamental **principal-agent problem** — the IMF (principal) lends resources belonging to its membership and must ensure the borrower (agent) undertakes corrective policies rather than using the loan to postpone adjustment. Without conditionality, countries would face a classic moral hazard: IMF lending would bail out unsustainable policies, encouraging worse outcomes.

## (B) Impact of IMF Conditionalities on Bangladesh Economy (2023 Loan Package) — 9 Marks

### 1. Context: Bangladesh's $4.7 Billion IMF Package (January 2023)

In January 2023, the IMF Executive Board approved a **42-month blended financing package** for Bangladesh comprising three facilities:

| Facility | Amount (SDR) | Amount (≈USD) | Purpose |
|----------|-------------|---------------|---------|
| **Extended Credit Facility (ECF)** | SDR 1.25 billion | ~$1.65 billion | Concessional lending for LICs; 0% interest, 10-year maturity |
| **Extended Fund Facility (EFF)** | SDR 1.25 billion | ~$1.65 billion | Non-concessional balance of payments and structural reform support |
| **Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF)** | SDR 1.0 billion | ~$1.4 billion | Climate-resilient investment; first Asian country to access RSF |
| **Total** | **SDR 3.5 billion** | **~$4.7 billion** | |

> As of July 2026, the programme had been augmented to ~$5.5 billion with an additional $800 million top-up approved in June 2025.

**Triggers for the programme:** (i) Post-pandemic import surge and sharp widening of current account deficit (from 0.9% of GDP in FY21 to 4.1% in FY22); (ii) Fuel/commodity price shocks from Russia-Ukraine war; (iii) Depletion of foreign reserves (from $48bn gross in 2021 to $21.8bn by end-2022); (iv) Taka depreciation pressures; (v) Inflation reaching a decade-high of 9.9% in August 2023.

### 2. Key Conditionalities Imposed on Bangladesh

The table below summarises the major conditions, their specific requirements, and compliance status as of 2026:

| Condition Area | Specific Requirements | Status (as of 2026) | Remarks |
|----------------|----------------------|---------------------|---------|
| **Revenue Mobilisation** | Increase tax-to-GDP ratio (from ~7.5%, among world's lowest); implement 15% unified VAT; reduce tax exemptions; establish independent Revenue Authority | **Partially met** — Revenue target missed repeatedly (Tk 378,000cr vs target Tk 443,530cr for FY25); Revenue Commission formed (2024) but tax policy/administration separation pending; tax-to-GDP fell to 6.9% in FY25 | Most persistent shortfall area |
| **Exchange Rate Unification** | Transition to market-clearing exchange rate; eliminate multiple exchange rate practices; crawl from crawling peg to flexible regime | **Largely met** — Crawling peg introduced May 2023; widened to ±1%; by mid-2024 BB allowed greater flexibility; IMF still flags FX intervention practices needing adjustment (2025 Article IV) | Major structural shift; significant achievement |
| **Monetary/Interest Rate Liberalisation** | Move from administered lending rates to market-based rates; adopt forward-looking monetary policy framework (inflation targeting roadmap); strengthen BB independence | **Partially met** — Lending rates liberalised; but monetary transmission still weak; tight monetary stance maintained (repo rate raised progressively); inflation target yet to be adopted as anchor | Prolonged tight policy has slowed growth |
| **Financial Sector Reform** | Address NPL crisis (NPLs >10%); strengthen Bangladesh Bank regulatory/supervisory powers; resolve/recapitalise weak banks; enact Bank Resolution Act; reduce directed lending | **Partially met — slow progress** — 10-12 banks identified as most distressed; Bank Resolution Act passed (2025) but provision on merged banks disputed; full restructuring strategy not yet adopted | Most problematic area; IMF's 2025 review flagged as major concern |
| **Subsidy Reduction (Energy)** | Phase out untargeted energy subsidies; adopt automatic fuel pricing formula; adjust electricity tariffs to cost-recovery levels; reduce SOE arrears | **Partially met** — Automatic fuel pricing largely maintained; but electricity tariff hike delayed (government refused in 2024 due to high inflation); 5 largest SOEs accounted for ~70% of total SOE losses in FY24 | Political sensitivity; social cost concerns |
| **SOE Reform** | Reduce quasi-fiscal operations; contain losses in energy SOEs (BPC, BPDB); limit government guarantees; privatisation roadmap | **Weak progress** — SOE losses continue; subsidy bill ~1% of GDP in FY25; arrears in energy/fertiliser sector reduced (IMF condition met) but structural reform minimal | Governance challenges |
| **Social Protection** | Maintain/expand social safety net; consolidate programmes (150→95); target vulnerable groups; introduce social spending floors | **Partially met** — Consolidation achieved; but coverage of poor still low; capital/social spending cut in FY25 to meet fiscal targets | Trade-off between fiscal consolidation and social spending |
| **Climate Resilience (RSF)** | Integrate climate into public investment management; improve climate spending efficiency; develop green finance framework | **Broadly on track** — RSF reviews completed; fiscal space created for climate priorities; first Asian RSF recipient | New facility; less contentious |
| **Reserve Targets (QPC)** | Maintain Net International Reserves (NIR) floor (e.g., $17.4bn for June 2025) | **Met consistently since interim government** — NIR stood at $20.73bn in June 2025 vs $17.4bn target; driven by remittance boom ($30.33bn record in FY25) and import compression | Success post-August 2024; previously repeatedly missed |

#### Source: IMF Country Reports (2023/66, 2023/410, 2026/024); Daily Star; TBS; Reuters; IMF Press Releases (PR 23/25, PR 23/440, PR 25/369)

### 3. Assessment of Impact

#### 3.1 Positive Assessments

| Dimension | Impact | Evidence |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| **External stability** | Foreign exchange reserves stabilised and rebuilt ($17.4bn → $20.73bn NIR); current account deficit narrowed (4.1% of GDP in FY22 → ~0.3% projected FY26) | IMF programme provided credible framework; remittances surged post-2024 political transition; import compression through tighter policy |
| **Exchange rate correction** | Crawling-peg transition removed overvaluation; Taka realignment improved export competitiveness; eliminated parallel market premium | Before programme, large gap between official and market rates; by 2025, rates unified; black market premium eliminated |
| **Fiscal discipline** | Primary deficit held at 2.3% of GDP (FY23); overall deficit at 3.7% (FY24); subsidy arrears reduced | Programme targets imposed fiscal discipline that might not have occurred otherwise; moderation in expenditure growth |
| **Catalytic effect on other financing** | IMF approval signalled reform credibility, unlocking ADB and World Bank co-financing; additional $750m from IMF + $800m augmentation | Bangladesh accessed additional $1.55bn beyond initial programme; development partners aligned with IMF conditionality framework |
| **Monetary policy tightening** | Inflation beginning to moderate from 9.9% peak (Aug 2023) to 8.2% (Oct 2025), though still elevated | Tight monetary stance (policy rate increases) supported by programme conditionalities; BB independence enhanced |
| **Financial sector attention** | Problem of NPLs and weak banks now on the policy agenda with a structured reform matrix | Before programme, banking sector weaknesses were largely unaddressed; IMF conditionality forced legislative action (Bank Resolution Act) and diagnostic exercises |

#### 3.2 Negative Assessments

| Dimension | Impact | Evidence |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| **Inflation burden** | Inflation remained elevated (8–10% throughout 2023–2026), imposing a regressive tax on the poor; monetary tightening contributed to demand compression | CPI inflation at 9.42% (May 2026); food inflation at 9.06%; tight monetary policy raised borrowing costs; policy rate increases transmitted to lending rates |
| **Social spending compression** | Fiscal consolidation targets forced cuts in capital and social spending; primary deficit target met by cutting spending, not raising revenue | IMF's own 2025 Article IV notes: "the primary deficit target was met through significant cuts in capital and social spending"; coverage of poor by social safety net remains low |
| **Subsidy reform regressivity** | Energy price adjustments + fertiliser subsidy rationalisation hit poor and small farmers disproportionately; electricity tariff freeze delayed but pressures remain | Fertiliser subsidies ~0.8% of GDP; energy subsidies ~1% of GDP; price increases without fully compensating social protection expansion |
| **Growth slowdown** | GDP growth decelerated from 7–8% pre-pandemic average to 6% (FY23), 4.2% (FY24), 3.7% (FY25) — far below potential; tight policy mix contributed | IMF lowered growth forecast repeatedly: from 6.5% to 5.4% for FY26; structural reforms slow to deliver growth dividends |
| **Policy sovereignty concerns** | Critics argue IMF conditions constrained Bangladesh's policy space, particularly regarding subsidy policy, exchange rate management, and interest rate setting | Energy adviser publicly stated government "would not increase electricity prices due to high inflation" — direct tension with IMF conditionality |
| **Tax burden without institutional reform** | Revenue conditions (15% VAT, reduced exemptions) risk placing heavy tax burden on formal sector and poor, without corresponding improvements in public service delivery | Tax-to-GDP *fell* from 7.5% to 6.9% (FY25), indicating reform struggled; compliance weak; administration assessed by IMF/World Bank as *weaker* than in 2017 |
| **Financial sector moral hazard** | IMF conditions requiring bank resolution may force costly taxpayer-funded bailouts; slow progress suggests political economy constraints preventing genuine reform | 10-12 banks identified as distressed; recapitalisation costs estimated significant (2025 Article IV); Bank Resolution Act faced political opposition |
| **Pro-cyclical fiscal stance** | Fiscal consolidation during an economic slowdown is contractionary — classic IMF pro-cyclicality critique | Primary surplus targets during growth deceleration; capital expenditure compressed even as economy needed stimulus |

### 4. Synthesis: Net Assessment

```
                    BALANCE SHEET OF IMF CONDITIONALITY IN BANGLADESH (2023–2026)

       POSITIVE                                          NEGATIVE
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐  ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  ✓ Reserves stabilised & rebuilt    │  │  ✗ Inflation persistently >8%      │
  │  ✓ Exchange rate unified            │  │  ✗ Growth slowed sharply (7→3.7%)  │
  │  ✓ Current account deficit narrowed │  │  ✗ Social spending compressed      │
  │  ✓ Catalytic financing unlocked     │  │  ✗ Subsidy reforms hit poor hardest │
  │  ✓ Fiscal deficit contained         │  │  ✗ Tax revenue targets missed       │
  │  ✓ Banking reform on policy agenda  │  │  ✗ Bank resolution painfully slow   │
  │  ✓ Climate resilience framework     │  │  ✗ Sovereignty constraints          │
  └─────────────────────────────────────┘  └─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Overall assessment (examiner's balanced view):**

The IMF programme achieved its **primary macroeconomic stabilisation objectives** — reserves were rebuilt, the exchange rate was unified, the external deficit narrowed, and crisis pressures eased. The catalytic effect on other financing and the structural attention to banking reform and climate resilience are genuine positive externalities.

However, the programme's **social and growth costs have been substantial**. Inflation remained above target throughout the programme period, growth decelerated sharply (to 3.7% in FY25 — the slowest in decades), and fiscal consolidation came partly at the expense of capital and social spending. Revenue mobilisation — the most fundamental condition — **underperformed persistently**, suggesting ownership and implementation capacity constraints that the programme's design did not adequately address.

The net outcome is **context-dependent**: for balance-of-payments stability and external creditor confidence, the programme succeeded; for inclusive growth, poverty reduction, and policy sovereignty, the record is mixed at best. This mirrors the broader international evidence on IMF conditionality (see Answer 15) — programmes tend to stabilise external accounts but at short-term output and social costs, and the distribution of these costs falls disproportionately on poorer households.

- Present the **table of condition types** (prior actions, QPCs, structural benchmarks, indicative targets, reviews) — this is the core content.
- Mention the historical evolution briefly (Washington Consensus → streamlining → country ownership) to show breadth.
- Do NOT spend time on Bangladesh in part (a) — that's exclusively for part (b).
- ⏱ Spend ~8 minutes on this part.

**For the 9-mark part (b) — Bangladesh assessment:**
- **Context first** (2 marks): Describe the $4.7bn package (ECF/EFF/RSF), triggers (Ukraine war, reserve depletion, inflation), and programme duration (42 months to July 2026). Use the table of conditions.
- **Balance is essential** (3+3 marks): Dedicate roughly equal space to positive and negative assessments. Use tables for clarity and to economise on words.
- **End with synthesis** (1 mark): A net assessment table (balance sheet) is an effective way to conclude.
- **Use evidence**: Cite specific numbers (NIR targets, inflation rates, growth rates, revenue shortfalls). Examiners reward empirical grounding.
- **Connect to theory** (bonus marks): Referencing Washington Consensus, structuralist critiques, or the IEO 2011 evaluation shows you understand the broader debate. The theoretical framework table above is exam-ready.

**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- ❌ Only discussing IMF conditionality generally without applying it to Bangladesh (part b requires a country-specific answer)
- ❌ One-sided polemic — either uncritical praise or total condemnation (examiners want balanced assessment)
- ❌ Failing to distinguish between different types of conditions (QPCs vs structural benchmarks)
- ❌ Neglecting the RSF/climate dimension, which is novel to Bangladesh's programme
- ❌ Not mentioning the 2024 political transition and its impact on programme performance

## Professor's Corner

> **The Bangladesh case is a fascinating contemporary test of IMF conditionality's effectiveness because it occupies a middle ground.** Unlike the deep crisis contexts of Sri Lanka (sovereign default, 2022) or Pakistan (chronic BOP crises), Bangladesh approached the IMF from a position of relative strength — sustained high growth, low poverty reduction trajectory, and a diversified export base. The programme was therefore more about *precautionary adjustment* than *crisis management*.
>
> **Three analytical points worth emphasising in an exam answer:**
>
> 1. **Revenue mobilisation is the Achilles' heel.** Bangladesh's tax-to-GDP ratio (~7.5%) is among the world's lowest. IMF conditions targeting this are clearly justified in principle, but implementation has been the weakest aspect of the programme. This raises a broader question: can conditionality force fiscal capacity-building in states with weak extractive institutions? The evidence from Bangladesh suggests **no** — at least not in the short-to-medium term. This is consistent with the broader literature on "state capacity" (Besley & Persson 2010), which argues that tax collection capacity is a deeply structural phenomenon that external pressure alone cannot transform.
>
> 2. **The political transition effect.** The change of government in August 2024 following the popular uprising had a dramatic effect on programme performance — reserve targets that were repeatedly missed under the previous government were consistently met afterwards. This is *not* because IMF conditions changed, but because the new interim government had stronger political legitimacy and less embedded resistance from vested interests (particularly in the banking sector and SOEs). This demonstrates that **country ownership is not just a design feature but a political condition** — reform implementation depends fundamentally on the domestic political settlement.
>
> 3. **The RSF is a novel institutional experiment.** Bangladesh being the first Asian country to access the RSF is significant. This facility represents a shift in IMF thinking — from post-crisis stabilisation to *ex ante* resilience building, including climate adaptation. If successful, the Bangladesh RSF could become a model for other climate-vulnerable LDCs. However, the RSF's effectiveness depends on the quality of climate public investment management, which is as yet unproven in Bangladesh.
>
> **The broader lesson** (one examiners at the 503 level often look for): The Bangladesh experience confirms the **conditional convergence** thesis in the literature on IMF programmes — they work reasonably well for restoring external balance and reserve adequacy, but their record on promoting growth, reducing poverty, and building institutional capacity is far weaker. The net assessment is a function of initial conditions, programme design, domestic politics, and the global economic environment — not an inherent property of conditionality itself.
