## 1. Iran's Place in the Development Syllabus

Iran is the **second oil-dependent case study** in 503, paired with Venezuela to demonstrate:

- **Same structural vulnerability** (oil export dependence) can produce **different crisis trajectories** depending on the trigger and institutional context.
- **Sanctions** act as an external shock analogous to a price crash but with distinct transmission mechanisms.
- The **political economy of oil** is not just about domestic governance — geopolitics determines outcomes.

| Dimension | Shared with Venezuela | Distinctive to Iran |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Oil export dependence | Yes (60–80% of exports) | Lower concentration than Venezuela (95%) |
| Rentier state dynamics | Yes | Regime more ideologically driven |
| Crisis trigger | Oil price crash | **Sanctions** (US-led) |
| Regime response | Money printing, expropriation | Subsidy reforms, managed currency, "resistance economy" |
| Outcome | Total collapse | Severe contraction but regime survival |

## 2. Historical Phases of Iran's Oil Strategy

### Phase 1: 1950s–1970s — Oil-Led Modernisation

| Period | Strategy | Key Features |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| **1950s** | Nationalisation | Mossadegh nationalised oil (1951); CIA-MI6 coup (1953) restored Shah |
| **1960s–70s** | White Revolution | Shah's modernisation programme — oil revenues funded industrialisation, land reform, military build-up |
| **1973** | Oil price quadrupling | Iranian oil revenues surged — rapid state-led industrialisation accelerated |

By the 1970s, Iran was the world's **second-largest oil exporter**. Oil revenues financed: large-scale industrial projects, military expansion (Iran as "Gendarm of the Gulf"), education and health infrastructure, and rapid urbanisation.

### Phase 2: 1979–1988 — Revolution and War

| Event | Economic Impact |
|-------|----------------|
| **Islamic Revolution (1979)** | Oil production disrupted; foreign firms expelled; Western sanctions began |
| **Iran-Iraq War (1980–88)** | Oil infrastructure destroyed; ~$500 billion in estimated damage; oil revenues diverted to war effort |

The revolution fundamentally altered Iran's political economy: the new regime rejected Western economic influence, nationalised industries, and instituted Islamic economic policies. Oil revenues, which had been the engine of modernisation, were now financing war and ideological consolidation.

### Phase 3: 1990s–2000s — Reconstruction and Subsidisation

- Post-war reconstruction raised oil output back to ~3.5 million barrels per day (mbpd).
- The government implemented **massive energy subsidisation** — making Iran one of the world's cheapest energy producers.
- By the 2000s, energy subsidies consumed **15–20% of GDP** — one of the highest subsidy ratios in the world.
- Limited economic diversification; the non-oil economy remained dependent on oil-financed government spending.

### Phase 4: 2010s–Present — Sanctions and Resistance

| Year | Sanctions Event | Oil Export Impact |
|------|----------------|-------------------|
| **2012** | EU/US nuclear sanctions | Oil exports fell from 2.5 mbpd to ~1.0 mbpd |
| **2015** | JCPOA (Nuclear Deal) | Sanctions lifted; exports recovered to ~2.5 mbpd |
| **2018** | US withdrew from JCPOA | "Maximum pressure" campaign; exports fell to ~0.5 mbpd |
| **2020+** | Continued sanctions | Exports stabilised at 0.5–0.7 mbpd (through smuggling and grey markets) |

The regime responded by promoting the **"Resistance Economy"** (Eqtesad-e Moghavemati) — an attempt at forced import-substituting industrialisation to reduce dependence on oil revenues and foreign trade.

## 3. The Subsidy Regime — Iran's Distinctive Social Contract

### What Iran Did

Iran maintained **among the world's lowest domestic energy prices**:

| Energy Product | Iran Price | Global Average | Subsidy Rate |
|---------------|-----------|----------------|--------------|
| Petrol (gasoline) | ~$0.10/L | ~$1.20/L | >90% |
| Natural gas | ~$0.05/m³ | ~$0.30/m³ | >80% |
| Electricity | ~$0.02/kWh | ~$0.12/kWh | >80% |

### Why Iran Did It

The subsidy regime functioned as a **form of social welfare**:

- Compensated for low wages and high unemployment.
- Maintained regime legitimacy — cheap fuel was a tacit social contract.
- Supported energy-intensive industries (petrochemicals, steel, cement).
- Prevented social unrest (until the 2019 price hike).

### The Costs

| Cost | Magnitude |
|------|-----------|
| **Fiscal burden** | Subsidies consumed 15–20% of GDP at peak |
| **Extreme energy intensity** | Energy use per unit of GDP was 3–4× the global average |
| **Smuggling** | Subsidised fuel smuggled to Turkey, Pakistan, Afghanistan |
| **Environmental damage** | Worst air pollution in the world (Ahvaz, Isfahan, Tehran) |
| **Structural inefficiency** | No incentive for conservation or investment in renewables |

## 4. Sanctions as the Crisis Trigger

### How Sanctions Transmit to the Economy

```
Sanctions imposed
        ↓
Oil exports blocked (2.5 mbpd → 0.5 mbpd)
        ↓
Government revenue collapses (40–60% of budget was oil-funded)
        ↓
Foreign exchange earnings ↓ → Rial depreciates >90%
        ↓
Import costs explode → Inflation >40%
        ↓
Real wages collapse → Poverty rises
        ↓
Social contract breaks down → 2019 protests
```

### Three Primary Channels of Crisis Transmission

| Channel | Mechanism | Outcome |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **1. Oil export blockade** | Oil exports fell from 2.5 mbpd to ~0.5 mbpd | Government revenue collapsed; budget deficit ballooned |
| **2. Currency crisis** | Sanctions blocked SWIFT access and foreign exchange markets | Rial depreciated >90% (2017–2021); imported inflation |
| **3. Inflation** | Money printing to cover deficits; import costs rose | Inflation exceeded 40% annually; savings and wages destroyed |

---

## 5. Transmission Mechanisms — From Sanctions to Collapse

### Mechanism 1: Revenue Collapse

- Oil revenues had funded ~40–60% of government budget.
- When sanctions cut oil exports by ~80%, the government lost its primary revenue source.
- Unlike Venezuela (which printed money freely), Iran attempted some fiscal adjustment — cutting subsidies and raising taxes — but the gap was too large.

### Mechanism 2: Currency Collapse

- The rial depreciated from ~30,000/USD (2015) to ~400,000+/USD (2021) — a >90% loss.
- Multiple exchange rates created: official rate (for essentials), NIMA rate (for exporters), and free market rate (for everything else).
- The gap between official and market rates reached 100%+, creating arbitrage and corruption.

### Mechanism 3: Imported Inflation

- Iran imports ~60% of its essential goods (wheat, edible oil, medicine, animal feed, industrial inputs).
- Currency collapse made imports prohibitively expensive → food prices, medicine prices, and input costs soared.
- Inflation exceeded 40% annually; food inflation was even higher.

## 6. Economic Outcomes

| Indicator | Pre-Sanctions (2017) | Post-Sanctions (2020–23) |
|-----------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| GDP growth | 3.8% | –6% (2018), –7% (2019) |
| Oil exports | 2.5 mbpd | ~0.5 mbpd |
| Inflation | ~10% | >40% |
| Rial/USD exchange rate | ~38,000 | >400,000 |
| Unemployment (official) | ~12% | >15% (higher among youth 15–29) |
| Poverty rate | ~20% | >30% |
| GDP per capita | ~$5,600 | ~$2,500 (PPP) |

**Structural damage:**
- Capital stock depletion — oil fields, refineries, and industrial plants deteriorated from underinvestment.
- Human capital flight (brain drain) — educated youth emigrated in large numbers.
- Informal economy expanded — estimated at 20–30% of total economic activity.
- Investment collapsed — both domestic and foreign.

---

## 7. Social Outcomes

| Indicator | Pre-Sanctions | Post-Sanctions | Trend |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|-------|
| Food insecurity | Low | Rising | Essential goods became unaffordable |
| Medicine access | Universal | Strained | Sanctions restricted medical imports |
| Infant mortality | Declining | Stagnated | Reversal of decades of progress |
| Life expectancy | 76 years | Plateaued | Progress halted |
| Higher education | Expanding | Brain drain crisis | Skilled emigration accelerated |
| Social unrest | Controlled | Major (2019 protests) | Largest since 1979 revolution |

### The 2019 Protests

- Trigger: 200% increase in gasoline prices (subsidy reform).
- Scale: Protests in 100+ cities; hundreds killed; largest civil unrest since the 1979 revolution.
- Significance: The oil-funded social contract had broken down. Cheap energy had been the regime's implicit promise to citizens — when that promise was broken, the regime's legitimacy was directly challenged.

## 8. Comparison with Venezuela

| Dimension | Venezuela | Iran |
|-----------|-----------|------|
| Oil export share | ~95% of exports | 60–80% (pre-sanctions) |
| Oil production | 3.2M → <500k bpd | 3.5M → ~2M bpd (including condensates) |
| Crisis trigger | **Oil price crash (2014)** | **Sanctions (2012, 2018)** |
| Inflation | Hyperinflation (>1,000,000%) | High but controlled (40–50%) |
| Currency | Bolívar virtually worthless | Rial severely depreciated but usable |
| Regime response | Money printing, expropriation | Subsidy reforms, managed float, resistance economy |
| Production collapse | Yes (PDVSA disinvestment) | Partial (some adaptation through grey markets) |
| Migration | 7M+ refugees (~20% of population) | Brain drain (~1.5M educated emigrants) |
| Diversification | None | Some (agriculture, petrochemicals, auto, pharma) |
| Total collapse? | Yes | Severe crisis but regime survived |

### Why Iran Avoided Total Collapse

| Factor | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Partial diversification** | Agriculture, petrochemicals, auto manufacturing, and pharmaceuticals provided some non-oil economic activity |
| **Regime coercive capacity** | The Islamic Republic retained stronger state capacity than Venezuela's regime |
| **Sanctions-forced adaptation** | Import substitution created some domestic production capacity (though inefficient) |
| **Geopolitical support** | China and Russia provided trade and investment alternatives (unlike Venezuela's isolation after 2015) |
| **Lower population dependence on oil** | Iran's more diversified (though still oil-dependent) economy meant the non-oil sector was relatively larger |

## 9. Theoretical Framework — Rentier State Meets Geopolitics

### The Rentier State (Beblawi & Luciani, 1987)

Iran is a classic rentier state — the government derives the bulk of its revenue from **external oil rents** rather than domestic taxation:

- **Breakdown of the fiscal contract**: Citizens are not taxed → government is not accountable.
- **Patronage distribution**: Oil revenue funds subsidies, public employment, and regime loyalists.
- **Economic inefficiency**: No pressure for productivity or innovation when revenue comes from natural resource extraction.

### Geopolitical Amplification

Iran's case adds a distinct dimension: **geopolitical exclusion** as an amplifier of rentier state vulnerabilities.

| Factor | Without Geopolitical Pressure | With Sanctions |
|--------|------------------------------|----------------|
| Oil revenue volatility | Cyclical (price-driven) | Structural (politically driven) |
| Policy space | Full policy autonomy | Severely constrained |
| Trade integration | Normal global integration | Blocked from SWIFT, shipping, insurance |
| Technology access | Open market | Restricted (dual-use sanctions) |
| Investment | FDI available | Almost zero |

### The Resistance Economy (Eqtesad-e Moghavemati)

Iran's response to sanctions — a forced import-substituting industrialisation strategy. This echoes the **Latin American ISI strategies of the 1950s–60s** (Prebisch, Singer). The strategy has:

- **Partial successes**: Domestic production of previously imported goods (some industrial machinery, pharmaceuticals, petrochemical derivatives).
- **Structural weaknesses**: Low productivity due to lack of competition; capital stock depletion; inefficiency; inflation.

## 10. Lessons for Other Developing Countries

### For Countries at Risk of Sanctions

| Lesson | Implication |
|--------|-------------|
| **Diversify exports** | Oil dependency makes sanctions devastating — multiple export markets reduce vulnerability |
| **Build foreign exchange reserves** | Iran had insufficient reserves to buffer the sanctions shock |
| **Maintain diplomatic relationships** | Isolated countries have fewer options when sanctions hit |
| **Develop domestic productive capacity** | Iran's partial industrial base softened the blow |

### For Bangladesh Specifically

| Iran Warning | Bangladesh Application |
|--------------|-----------------------|
| Oil dependency → sanctions vulnerability | RMG concentration creates similar vulnerability — not to sanctions but to demand or preference shocks |
| Subsidies as a social contract | Energy and food subsidies in Bangladesh are politically sensitive — reform must be carefully managed |
| Currency collapse → imported inflation | Bangladesh Bank's reserve management is critical — reserves have fallen from $48B to $20B |
| Brain drain as a long-term cost | Bangladesh loses skilled workers to emigration — turn this into a benefit through remittances and diaspora engagement |

## 11. Exam Tips

### Structure for (10 + 10) Mark Split

**Part A — Iran's Oil Strategy (10 marks):**
- Structure by historical phases (4 phases — 1950s–70s, 1979–88, 1990s–2000s, 2010s–present).
- Emphasise the **subsidy regime** as Iran's distinctive strategy — this is what differentiates Iran from other oil-dependent countries.
- Reference the "resistance economy" as the current phase.

**Part B — Economic and Social Outcomes (10 marks):**
- Start with the **three transmission channels** (revenue collapse, currency crisis, inflation).
- Present economic data: GDP contraction, inflation >40%, unemployment, poverty.
- Present social data: food insecurity, health stagnation, brain drain, protests.
- End with the **comparison to Venezuela** — examiners love this.

### Key Data Points to Memorise

| Data Point | Value |
|------------|-------|
| Oil exports pre-sanctions | 2.5 mbpd |
| Oil exports post-sanctions | ~0.5 mbpd |
| Inflation rate | >40% |
| rial depreciation | >90% (2017–2021) |
| Energy subsidies as % of GDP | 15–20% (peak) |
| Poverty rate increase | 20% → 30%+ |
| GDP contraction | –6% (2018), –7% (2019) |

### Common Mistakes

- Treating Iran and Venezuela as the same case — highlight the differences.
- Ignoring the subsidy regime — this is Iran's distinctive feature.
- Blaming everything on sanctions — Iran's own policy choices (subsidies, limited diversification) magnified the shock.
- Forgetting the 2019 protests — a key piece of evidence for the social contract breakdown.
- Not making the comparison table explicit — the exam question is designed for this.

## 12. Professor's Corner

> Iran's case is analytically important because it **separates two explanations** that are often conflated in development discussions of oil-dependent economies:
>
> *Hypothesis A: Oil dependence itself causes poor development outcomes (the resource curse).*
> *Hypothesis B: External political factors (sanctions, geopolitical exclusion) cause poor outcomes in oil-dependent economies.*
>
> Iran is a rare case where **both hypotheses can be tested simultaneously**. The pre-2012 period tested Hypothesis A (Iran showed classic rentier state symptoms — low productivity, inefficiency, limited diversification). The post-2012 period tested Hypothesis B (sanctions dramatically amplified the resource curse effects).
>
> **The conclusion:** Resource curse + geopolitical vulnerability = disaster. Countries that combine oil wealth with geopolitical risk face a double burden that even good domestic policy cannot fully overcome.
>
> **The Bangladesh connection:**
>
> Bangladesh is not Iran, but the exercise of comparing the two is valuable for the exam. Ask yourself: *What is Bangladesh's geopolitical vulnerability?* The answer may be different (climate vulnerability, LDC graduation, RMG buyer concentration), but the analytical framework is the same — identifying the external shock that could turn a structural vulnerability into a crisis.
