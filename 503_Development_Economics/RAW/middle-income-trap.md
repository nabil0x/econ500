## 1. Definition and Origin of the Middle-Income Trap

### 1.1 Core Definition

The **middle-income trap (MIT)** is a situation in development economics where an economy that has grown rapidly from low-income to middle-income status subsequently fails to sustain the momentum needed to transition to high-income status. The country becomes "trapped" at middle-income levels, unable to compete internationally on either of two fronts:

- **With low-wage economies:** Rising domestic wages erode competitiveness in mature, labour-intensive manufacturing industries.
- **With high-innovation economies:** The country lacks the technological capacity, human capital, and institutional sophistication to compete in high-value-added, innovation-driven sectors.

Formally, the trap is defined as a prolonged stagnation in which GNI per capita remains within the middle-income band (approximately $1,000--$12,000 at constant 2011 prices, per the World Bank's original framing) for decades without crossing into high-income territory.

### 1.2 Origin: Gill and Kharas (2007)

The term was **coined by Indermit Gill and Homi Kharas** in their 2007 World Bank report *"An East Asian Renaissance: Ideas for Economic Growth"*. They observed that:

> *"Rapid growth from low-income to middle-income levels -- fuelled by cheap labour, basic technology catch-up, and the reallocation of labour and capital from low-productivity sectors like traditional agriculture to export-driven, high-productivity manufacturing -- is often followed by lower growth. Unless new sources of economic growth are found, a country may find itself unable to compete with either low-wage countries that dominate mature industries or high-income countries that dominate innovative, high-technology industries."*

The concept was not entirely new -- it echoes earlier work on **growth slowdowns** and **development traps** -- but Gill and Kharas gave it a clear label and a policy-focused framework that resonated with policymakers across the developing world.

### 1.3 Later Refinements

- **Gill and Kharas (2015):** "The Middle-Income Trap Turns Ten" -- reflecting on the decade since coining the term, they acknowledged that the trap concept was as much about the *absence of a satisfactory growth theory for middle-income countries* as about a specific empirical phenomenon.
- **World Development Report 2024:** The World Bank devoted its entire 2024 WDR to the middle-income trap, identifying **108 countries** currently stuck in it, and proposing a "3i strategy" (Investment, Infusion, Innovation) to escape it.
- **Aiyar et al. (2013, IMF):** Found that growth slowdowns are more likely at middle-income levels than at low- or high-income levels, lending empirical support to the concept.
- **Eichengreen, Park, and Shin (2014):** Identified two specific income thresholds at which slowdowns tend to occur: approximately $10,000--$11,000 and $15,000--$16,000 (2005 PPP-adjusted dollars).

## 2. Key Characteristics of the Trap

| Characteristic | Description |
|----------------|-------------|
| **Decelerating growth** | After a period of rapid catch-up growth (often 6--10% per year), the growth rate falls sharply and stabilises at a lower level (2--4%). |
| **Inability to compete on wages** | Rising labour costs make the country's traditional export industries uncompetitive against lower-wage neighbours (e.g., Bangladesh vs Ethiopia in garments). |
| **Inability to compete on innovation** | The country cannot shift into high-technology, high-value-added production because it lacks R&D capacity, skilled labour, and institutional frameworks for innovation. |
| **Premature deindustrialisation** | Manufacturing's share of GDP and employment peaks at a lower income level than it did in today's high-income countries, and begins declining before the country has reached high-income status. |
| **Low total factor productivity (TFP) growth** | Growth becomes driven by factor accumulation (more capital, more labour) rather than productivity improvements. TFP growth stalls. |
| **Persistent middle-income status** | GNI per capita remains in the middle-income band ($1,136--$13,845 in 2024 terms) for decades, with no trajectory toward the high-income threshold ($13,845+). |
| **Weak structural transformation** | Labour moves out of agriculture but into low-productivity services (informal sector) rather than into high-productivity manufacturing. |

### 2.1 The "Nutcracker" Analogy

A common metaphor: middle-income countries are squeezed like a nut between two jaws:
- **Lower jaw:** Low-income, low-wage countries that undercut them in basic manufacturing.
- **Upper jaw:** High-income, high-innovation countries that dominate advanced technology and knowledge-intensive services.

To escape, the country must transition from an **investment-driven** growth model to an **innovation-driven** growth model -- a far more difficult institutional and technological transformation than the initial shift from agriculture to manufacturing.

## 3. The Two Thresholds

The transition from low-income to high-income involves crossing two critical thresholds:

### Threshold 1: Low-Income to Lower-Middle-Income

- Characterised by: surplus labour in agriculture, basic industrialisation, labour-intensive manufacturing exports.
- Growth drivers: factor accumulation, technology adoption (catch-up), structural change (labour reallocation).
- Risk: relatively low -- this transition is well-understood and has been achieved by many countries.
- **Bangladesh crossed this threshold in 2015.**

### Threshold 2: Lower-Middle-Income to Upper-Middle-Income

- Characterised by: rising wages, diminishing returns to capital accumulation, need for productivity-driven growth.
- Growth drivers: human capital deepening, technological upgrading, industrial diversification.
- Risk: **High** -- this is where most countries get stuck.
- Bangladesh's current position: lower-middle-income, approaching the upper-middle-income threshold (~$4,496 GNI per capita in 2024 terms; Bangladesh at ~$2,820).

### Threshold 3: Upper-Middle-Income to High-Income

- Characterised by: need for frontier innovation, sophisticated institutions, high-value services, global brand presence.
- Growth drivers: R&D, innovation ecosystems, institutional quality, global competitiveness.
- Risk: **Extreme** -- very few countries have crossed this threshold (only 13 of 101 middle-income countries in 1960 reached high-income by 2008, per World Bank).

### World Bank Income Thresholds (FY2025, based on 2023 GNI per capita, Atlas method)

| Income Group | GNI per Capita Range (USD) |
|--------------|---------------------------|
| Low income | $1,145 or less |
| Lower-middle income | $1,146 -- $4,515 |
| Upper-middle income | $4,516 -- $14,005 |
| High income | $14,006 or more |

*Source: World Bank Country and Lending Groups, July 2025.*

## 4. Causes of the Middle-Income Trap

### 4.1 Weak Institutions

- Poor rule of law, corruption, weak property rights, and inefficient bureaucracy discourage long-term investment.
- Institutional quality matters more at middle-income than at low-income levels because growth must shift from simple factor accumulation to complex, innovation-driven activities that require contract enforcement, intellectual property protection, and predictable regulation.
- **Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001):** Institutions are the deep determinant of income differences.

### 4.2 Insufficient R&D and Innovation

- Middle-income countries typically spend far less on R&D as a share of GDP than high-income countries. (South Korea: 4.8% of GDP; Bangladesh: ~0.4% of GDP.)
- Without domestic innovation capacity, countries cannot move up the value chain.
- Technology transfer from abroad diminishes as a growth driver once the country reaches the global technology frontier in its existing industries.

### 4.3 Middle-Income Skills Gap

- The education system produces workers with basic literacy and numeracy but insufficient higher-order skills (critical thinking, problem-solving, technical expertise).
- There is a mismatch between the skills the labour force possesses and the skills demanded by higher-value-added industries.
- The "middle-income skills gap" is one of the most commonly cited causes of the trap in the literature (World Bank WDR 2024).

### 4.4 Premature Deindustrialisation

- Manufacturing employment and output shares peak at lower income levels than they did in today's advanced economies (Rodrik, 2016).
- Countries begin deindustrialising before they have accumulated enough capital or technology to transition to high-productivity services.
- Causes: globalisation (competition from China and other manufacturing powerhouses), labour-saving technological change, and natural resource dependence.

### 4.5 Resource Misallocation

- Capital and labour remain trapped in low-productivity sectors (subsistence agriculture, informal services) due to policy distortions, credit market failures, and regulatory barriers.
- Industrial policy often favours politically connected firms rather than competitive, dynamic sectors.
- State-owned enterprises and protected industries absorb resources that could otherwise flow to more productive uses.

### 4.6 The "Political Trap" (Babones and Elsenhans)

- The middle-income trap is fundamentally a **political trap**: the economic methods to escape it exist, but powerful elites who benefited from the previous growth model block reforms.
- Wealthy elites favour a strong currency (which enables luxury imports) and resist minimum wage increases, mass consumption, and the structural reforms that would unlock new growth.

### 4.7 Demographic Headwinds

- Countries that took too long at middle-income levels begin to age before becoming rich -- "growing old before growing rich."
- The demographic dividend (a large working-age population relative to dependents) is a one-time window that must be exploited before the population ages.

## 5. Country Examples: Trapped vs Escaped

Only a handful of countries have successfully transitioned from middle-income to high-income status in the last 60 years. Of 101 middle-income countries in 1960, only **13** had become high-income by 2008 (World Bank).

### 5.3 Key Lessons from the Comparison

| Factor | Escaped Countries | Trapped Countries |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **R&D spending** | >3% of GDP (Korea 4.8%) | <1.5% of GDP (Brazil 1.2%, Malaysia 1.0%) |
| **Education quality** | Top-quartile PISA scores | Lower-quartile PISA scores |
| **Export sophistication** | High-tech (electronics, semiconductors, pharma, automobiles) | Mid-tech assembly, natural resources, commodities |
| **Institutional quality** | Strong rule of law, low corruption (top 30 in CPI) | Moderate-to-weak institutions (top 60--100 in CPI) |
| **Industrial policy** | Strategic, performance-based, time-bound | Patronage-based, open-ended, protectionist |
| **FDI strategy** | Targeted FDI with technology-transfer requirements | Passive FDI or FDI restricted |
| **Demographic transition** | Exploited demographic dividend effectively | Ageing before becoming rich (some cases) |

### 6.3 LDC Graduation Status

Bangladesh met all three criteria for LDC graduation (GNI per capita, Human Assets Index, Economic Vulnerability Index) in 2018 and received final UN approval in 2021. The country is scheduled to formally **graduate from LDC status in November 2026** (transition period originally 2024 extended to 2026 due to COVID-19).

Post-graduation challenges include:
- Loss of duty-free, quota-free access to EU and other markets.
- Loss of preferential access to concessional development finance (IDA-only eligibility ends).
- Need to comply with WTO TRIPS agreement for pharmaceutical exports.
- Higher scrutiny on compliance and standards in export markets.

## 7. Analysis: Is Bangladesh Facing the Challenges of the Middle-Income Trap?

### 7.1 The Argument That Bangladesh Is at Risk (Yes)

There are strong reasons to believe Bangladesh is facing the early-stage challenges of the middle-income trap:

**1. Slowing Growth Trajectory**
- GDP growth averaged 6.5%+ from 2010--2019, peaking at 7.9% in 2019.
- Post-COVID growth has decelerated sharply: 5.8% in 2023, 4.2% in 2024.
- This growth deceleration is precisely the pattern the MIT literature identifies: rapid catch-up followed by significant slowdown as the economy reaches middle-income levels (Aiyar et al., 2013; Eichengreen et al., 2014).

**2. Extreme Export Concentration**
- Ready-made garments (RMG) account for **~84% of total export earnings** -- one of the highest export concentration ratios in the world.
- This is a textbook vulnerability: a single sector faces rising wages, global competition from lower-cost producers (Ethiopia, Vietnam, Cambodia), and technological disruption (automation, 3D knitting).
- Historical comparison: Brazil's coffee dependence, Malaysia's rubber/tin dependence, Nigeria's oil dependence -- all examples of countries that failed to diversify and got trapped.

**3. Minimal Export Diversification**
- Non-RMG exports (leather, jute, pharmaceuticals, IT services) collectively account for ~16% of exports.
- In contrast, Vietnam has diversified into electronics (Samsung accounts for ~20% of exports but is part of a diversified basket including phones, machinery, textiles, footwear, and agriculture).

**4. Extremely Low FDI**
- FDI at **0.33% of GDP** (2024) is among the lowest in developing Asia. Vietnam attracts 4.3% of GDP.
- FDI is a key channel for technology transfer, managerial know-how, and global value chain integration. Bangladesh's failure to attract FDI limits its ability to upgrade technologically.

**5. Middle-Income Skills Gap**
- Bangladesh ranks low on human capital indices relative to its growth peers. The World Bank's Human Capital Index: Bangladesh 0.48 (a child born in Bangladesh will be only 48% as productive as she could be with full health and education).
- The education system produces graduates with poor employability: ~40% of university graduates are unemployed or underemployed.
- Shortage of engineers, technicians, and managers with the skills needed for higher-value industries.

**6. Low R&D Investment**
- R&D spending: ~0.4% of GDP (compared to Korea 4.8%, China 2.4%, Vietnam 0.5%).
- Bangladesh has no globally competitive high-technology export sector.

**7. Financial Sector Weakness**
- Non-performing loans (NPLs) in the banking sector: ~27% (one of the highest ratios in the world).
- The banking sector has been plagued by loan scams, political interference, and weak governance.
- This constrains credit to the private sector and limits investment in new productive capacity.

**8. Infrastructure Gaps**
- Power supply reliability, port efficiency, road and rail connectivity, and digital infrastructure all lag behind competitor countries.
- The Logistics Performance Index (World Bank): Bangladesh ranks 100th out of 160 countries, far behind Vietnam (39th), Thailand (34th), and Malaysia (41st).

**9. Premature Deindustrialisation Risks**
- Manufacturing value-added as a share of GDP peaked at ~19% (2011) and has declined to ~17% (2024).
- While this is not as dramatic as in Latin America, the trend is concerning because manufacturing typically needs to reach 25--30% of GDP before a country can transition to high-income status.

### 7.2 The Counterargument: Not Yet Trapped (Optimistic View)

**1. Growth Still Positive**
- Even at 4.2%, Bangladesh's growth rate is above the developing-country average and comparable to many countries that eventually escaped the trap.
- The deceleration may be cyclical (post-COVID adjustment, global inflation, Ukraine war) rather than structural.

**2. Demographic Dividend Remains**
- Median age 27.6 years: the working-age population is still growing. The dependency ratio is still declining, providing a tailwind for growth that will last at least another 15--20 years.
- This is a window of opportunity that many trapped countries (Brazil, Thailand) have already lost.

**3. Emerging Dynamic Sectors**
- Pharmaceuticals: Bangladesh meets 97% of domestic demand and exports to over 150 countries.
- IT/outsourcing: a growing IT sector with exports of ~$1.5 billion and potential to scale rapidly.
- Leather and footwear: exports of ~$1.2 billion with potential to reach $5 billion by 2030.
- Shipbuilding: a nascent but potentially competitive industry.

**4. RMG Is Not Stagnating**
- The RMG sector is moving up the value chain: from basic t-shirts to high-value items (synthetic jackets, denim, technical textiles).
- Bangladesh has the largest number of LEED-certified green garment factories in the world (over 200).
- RMG exports reached a record ~$47 billion in 2023--24 and crossed $50 billion in 2025--26.

**5. Digital Transformation**
- The government's "Digital Bangladesh" agenda has expanded internet access from 5% of the population (2008) to over 60% (2024).
- Mobile financial services (bKash, Nagad) have revolutionised financial inclusion.
- The IT sector is growing at 30--40% annually and could become a significant export earner.

**6. Macroeconomic Resilience**
- Despite the recent reserve drawdown, Bangladesh has a history of macroeconomic stability.
- The country has never defaulted on its external debt.
- Remittances ($24 billion in 2024) provide a stable income floor for the economy.

### 7.3 Balanced Assessment

**The most accurate assessment is that Bangladesh is at a critical inflection point:**

- It has not yet fallen into the trap -- the growth rate is still positive, key structural indicators are still moving in the right direction, and the demographic dividend remains intact.
- However, **many of the early warning signs of the trap are visible**: extreme export concentration, declining growth, low FDI, a skills gap, and weak R&D.
- The next 5--10 years will be decisive. If Bangladesh uses its remaining demographic window to diversify exports, upgrade skills, attract FDI, and invest in R&D, it can escape. If it persists with the current growth model, it risks joining the list of countries trapped at middle-income for decades (like Malaysia, Brazil, and Thailand).

## 8. Challenges Specific to Bangladesh

### 8.1 Structural Challenges

| Challenge | Description | Risk Level |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| **RMG dependency** | 84% of exports in one sector; post-LDC graduation will remove trade preferences | Critical |
| **Low FDI** | 0.33% of GDP -- limits technology transfer and GVC integration | Critical |
| **Skills gap** | Low HCI (0.48); weak technical and vocational education | High |
| **Low R&D** | 0.4% of GDP -- insufficient for innovation-driven growth | High |
| **Infrastructure deficits** | Poor logistics, unreliable power, congested ports | High |
| **Financial sector fragility** | 27% NPLs, governance weaknesses, constrained credit | Critical |
| **Governance and corruption** | Bangladesh ranks 149th out of 180 in Transparency International's CPI | High |
| **Political uncertainty** | Post-hasina transition, upcoming elections, polarisation | Medium |
| **Climate vulnerability** | One of the most climate-vulnerable countries; adaptation costs are high | Medium |
| **Land constraints** | Dense population; limited room for industrial expansion | Medium |

### 8.2 Post-LDC Graduation Challenges

1. **Market access erosion:** Loss of duty-free, quota-free access to the EU (under Everything But Arms) and other markets. Bangladesh's RMG exports could face tariffs of 9--12% in the EU, reducing competitiveness.
2. **Development finance graduation:** Bangladesh will lose access to IDA (concessional World Bank loans) and will need to borrow from IBRD at higher interest rates.
3. **WTO TRIPS compliance:** As a graduating LDC, Bangladesh will lose its transition period exemption from TRIPS for pharmaceutical products -- a key concern for the growing pharma export sector.
4. **Competition from lower-cost LDCs:** Ethiopia, Cambodia, and Myanmar have lower wages and may attract RMG buyers seeking cheaper sourcing.

### 8.3 Regional Competition

- **Vietnam** has the most competitive manufacturing sector in the region (FDI-to-GDP 4.3%, EU-Vietnam FTA, diversified export basket).
- **India** offers a large domestic market and is investing heavily in infrastructure and manufacturing (PLI schemes).
- **Indonesia** has abundant natural resources and a large domestic market; moving up the GVC in nickel processing and EV batteries.
- **Sri Lanka** (despite its crisis) has better human capital indicators and a more diversified export base.

## 10. Policy Recommendations to Avoid the Trap

### 10.1 The "3i Strategy" (World Bank WDR 2024)

The World Bank's recommended framework for middle-income countries:

| Phase | Focus | Key Actions |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| **1st i: Investment** | Low-income countries should focus on increasing investment in physical and human capital | Infrastructure; basic education; health; FDI attraction |
| **2nd i: Infusion** | Lower-middle-income countries should shift to absorbing foreign technology and integrating into global value chains | Technology licensing; FDI with technology-transfer requirements; GVC participation |
| **3rd i: Innovation** | Upper-middle-income countries must become frontier innovators | R&D investment; patent system; university-industry links; venture capital |

Bangladesh is currently at the **transition between Investment and Infusion** -- it needs to maintain investment momentum while aggressively pursuing technology infusion.

### 10.2 Specific Policy Recommendations for Bangladesh

#### A. Export Diversification
- Implement a **National Export Diversification Strategy** with clear targets for non-RMG sectors (pharma: $1B; IT: $5B; leather: $5B; light engineering: $3B by 2030).
- Establish sector-specific **Special Economic Zones** for priority industries (e.g., a Leather SEZ in Savar, a Pharma SEZ in Munshiganj).
- Use **performance-linked incentives** (not blanket cash subsidies) to reward new product introduction, new market entry, and R&D.
- Negotiate **Free Trade Agreements** with emerging markets (ASEAN, African Continental Free Trade Area) to offset post-LDC preference erosion.

#### B. FDI Attraction and Technology Transfer
- Target FDI-to-GDP ratio to rise from 0.33% to at least 2% within 5 years (Vietnam's level is 4.3%).
- Create a **single-window clearance system** for FDI projects (currently fragmented across BIDA, BEZA, NBR).
- Offer **targeted incentives** for FDI in priority sectors with mandatory technology transfer and local content requirements.
- Improve **ease of doing business**: simplify business registration, tax administration, and customs procedures.

#### C. Human Capital Development
- Increase public education spending from ~2% to 4% of GDP (South Asian average: 3.2%).
- Reform the **TVET (Technical and Vocational Education and Training)** system to produce industry-relevant skills.
- Establish **industry-led skills councils** in each priority sector to align training with employer needs.
- Expand **STEM education** at the tertiary level: double engineering and IT graduate output within 5 years.

#### D. R&D and Innovation
- Raise R&D spending from 0.4% to 1.5% of GDP, with a mix of public and private investment.
- Create a **National Innovation Fund** to support applied research in priority sectors.
- Establish **university-industry research partnerships** (model: South Korea's KAIST, Taiwan's ITRI).
- Strengthen the **patent and intellectual property framework** to encourage innovation.

#### E. Infrastructure Development
- Complete the **Padma Bridge rail link** and other major transport projects to reduce logistics costs.
- Expand and modernise **Chittagong Port** and develop **Matarbari deep-sea port**.
- Address **energy sector inefficiencies**: reduce transmission losses, diversify energy sources (renewables, LNG, nuclear), and reform pricing.
- Expand **digital infrastructure**: 5G rollout, data centres, and subsea cable capacity.

#### F. Financial Sector Reform
- Resolve the **NPL crisis** through a combination of debt restructuring, asset recovery, and strengthened supervision.
- Reduce **crowding out** of private credit by government borrowing.
- Strengthen **Bangladesh Bank independence** and banking sector governance.
- Develop **capital markets** for long-term project financing.

#### G. Governance and Institutional Reform
- Strengthen the **rule of law** and address corruption systematically (Competition: Bangladesh ranks 149th in CPI).
- Implement a comprehensive **tax reform**: increase tax-to-GDP ratio from ~8.5% (one of the lowest in the world) to at least 15%.
- Improve **public procurement transparency** and reduce political interference in state-owned enterprises.
- Strengthen the **independence and capacity of anti-corruption institutions**.

### 10.3 International Experience to Draw On

| Country | Lesson for Bangladesh |
|---------|----------------------|
| **South Korea** | State-led industrial policy with performance benchmarks; massive R&D investment; education reform. The chaebol system shows the value of creating national champions -- but Bangladesh needs to avoid the cronyism trap. |
| **Singapore** | FDI attraction as a deliberate strategy for technology transfer; world-class infrastructure; strategic pivot from manufacturing to high-value services. |
| **Vietnam** | FTA-driven integration; FDI as the engine of export diversification; competitive labour costs combined with improving skills. Vietnam's shift from textiles to electronics is the model Bangladesh should study most closely. |
| **Malaysia (cautionary)** | Despite decades of growth, failed to escape due to ethnic-based affirmative action (NEP), brain drain, and inability to move beyond mid-value assembly. Bangladesh must avoid similar identity-based distortions. |
| **Brazil (cautionary)** | Premature deindustrialisation; commodity dependence; fiscal profligacy; inequality. Bangladesh must industrialise further and deeper (not deindustrialise) while the window is still open. |

## 11. Exam Tips

### For the Definition Part (8 marks)

- **Start with the Gill and Kharas (2007)** origin story -- this shows the examiner you know the literature.
- **Define the trap precisely:** the country cannot compete with low-wage economies OR high-innovation economies.
- **Use the "nutcracker" analogy** -- it is memorable and shows conceptual clarity.
- **Distinguish between the three income thresholds** and note that Bangladesh crossed the first in 2015.
- **Cite at least one key empirical reference** (Aiyar et al., 2013; Eichengreen et al., 2014; or WDR 2024).
- **Mention the debate:** some economists (Bulman, Eden, and Nguyen, 2014) argue there is no systematic trap -- countries that grow slowly do so at all income levels, not specifically at middle income. This evaluative nuance scores well.

### For the Bangladesh Analysis Part (12 marks)

- **Structure your answer as a debate:** "On one hand..." and "On the other hand..." then give your reasoned conclusion.
- **Cite specific data:** GNI per capita ($2,820 in 2024), RMG share of exports (84%), FDI (0.33% of GDP), NPLs (27%), R&D spending (0.4% of GDP).
- **Use the trapped vs escaped comparison table** to position Bangladesh relative to comparators.
- **Mention the post-LDC graduation challenge specifically** -- it is Bangladesh-specific and shows applied knowledge.
- **Show you understand the demographic dividend** and its finite window.
- **End with policy recommendations** -- the 3i framework is a safe anchor, but tailor it to Bangladesh with specific numbers and sectors.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Giving a definition of the trap without linking it to the Bangladesh analysis -- the two parts must connect.
- Quoting outdated GNI data (pre-2020).
- Ignoring the post-LDC graduation challenge entirely.
- Presenting one-sided arguments (only optimistic OR only pessimistic).
- Discussing RMG without mentioning the efforts at upgrading (LEED certification, value addition).
- Not distinguishing between lower-middle and upper-middle-income thresholds.
- Failing to mention the gender dimension (women in RMG, feminisation of the labour force, and how the trap affects women differently).

## 12. Professor's Corner

> The middle-income trap question is one of the most policy-relevant topics in contemporary development economics. It forces students to integrate growth theory, trade theory, institutional economics, and country-specific knowledge into a single analytical framework.
>
> **Key intellectual architecture:**
>
> 1. The trap concept is a bridge between **neoclassical growth theory** (Solow-Swan predicts conditional convergence, but the speed is too slow for many countries) and **endogenous growth theory** (Romer, Lucas -- innovation and human capital are the drivers of sustained growth). The trap exists because countries fail to make the transition from the first to the second.
>
> 2. The trap is **not inevitable** -- 13 countries escaped between 1960 and 2008. The question is why some succeed and others fail. This connects to the **institutions vs geography vs policy debate** (Acemoglu, Rodrik, Hausmann).
>
> 3. For Bangladesh specifically, this is the **central strategic question of the next decade**. The country has achieved remarkable progress but faces a structural transition that historically only a minority of countries have managed. The LDC graduation in 2026 is both the culmination of past success and the beginning of a new, more difficult phase.
>
> 4. The trap is ultimately a **governance story** (Babones and Elsenhans). The technical solutions (R&D, education, FDI) are well known. The binding constraint is political: the ability to implement reforms that threaten existing interests. Bangladesh's political economy -- the role of the RMG lobby, the banking sector elite, the political party system, and the civil-military bureaucracy -- will determine whether the country escapes or stagnates.
>
> 5. **Gender dimension (important for exam context):** The RMG sector employs ~4 million workers, 60% of whom are women. Any transition away from RMG dominance must manage the impact on female employment. The middle-income trap is not gender-neutral -- women are disproportionately affected by deindustrialisation and informalisation.
>
> **Must-read references for deeper understanding:**
> - Gill, I. & Kharas, H. (2007). *An East Asian Renaissance: Ideas for Economic Growth.* World Bank, Washington DC. (The origin of the term.)
> - Gill, I. & Kharas, H. (2015). "The Middle-Income Trap Turns Ten." World Bank Policy Research Working Paper No. 7403. (The retrospective assessment.)
> - World Bank (2024). *World Development Report 2024: The Middle-Income Trap.* Washington DC. (The definitive contemporary treatment, including the 3i strategy.)
> - Aiyar, S. et al. (2013). "Growth Slowdowns and the Middle-Income Trap." IMF Working Paper 13/71. (Empirical evidence that slowdowns are more likely at middle income.)
> - Eichengreen, B., Park, D. & Shin, K. (2014). "Growth Slowdowns Redux." *Japan and the World Economy*, 32, 65--84. (Identifies specific income thresholds for slowdowns.)
> - Rodrik, D. (2016). "Premature Deindustrialisation." *Journal of Economic Growth*, 21(1), 1--33. (The structural argument for why middle-income countries struggle.)
> - Babones, S. & Elsenhans, H. (2014). "The Middle-Income Trap as a Political Trap." (The political economy interpretation.)
> - Cherif, R. & Hasanov, F. (2015). "The Leap of the Tiger: How Malaysia Can Escape the Middle-Income Trap." IMF Working Paper. (A detailed case study of a country stuck at the threshold.)
> - Bulman, D., Eden, M. & Nguyen, H. (2014). "Transitioning from Low-Income Growth to High-Income Growth: Is There a Middle-Income Trap?" World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 7104. (The sceptical view: no systematic trap exists.)
