## Part A: Basic Principles of the Paris Agreement (2015) — ~10 marks

The Paris Agreement, adopted on **12 December 2015** under the UNFCCC and entering into force on **4 November 2016**, represents the first universal, legally binding global climate accord. It fundamentally restructured international climate governance from a top-down model (Kyoto Protocol) to a bottom-up architecture built around nationally determined contributions. Below are the ten core principles:

| # | Principle | Article | Explanation |
|---|-----------|---------|-------------|
| **1** | **Temperature Goal** | Art. 2.1(a) | Hold global average temperature increase to **well below 2°C** above pre-industrial levels and **pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C**, recognising that this significantly reduces climate risks and impacts. This is the Agreement's central mitigation anchor. |
| **2** | **Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)** | Art. 4.2–4.3 | Each Party must **prepare, communicate and maintain successive NDCs** representing its highest possible ambition. This is the **bottom-up** architecture: countries set their own targets rather than having them imposed internationally. Each successive NDC must represent a **progression** (ratchet mechanism). |
| **3** | **Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC)** | Art. 2.2 | The Agreement "will be implemented to reflect equity and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in the light of different national circumstances." This is the **cornerstone principle**: developed countries, having contributed most to historical emissions, bear primary responsibility for mitigation and must provide finance and technology to developing countries. |
| **4** | **Adaptation Goal** | Art. 7.1 | Establishes a **global goal on adaptation**: enhancing adaptive capacity, strengthening resilience, and reducing vulnerability to climate change. Adaptation is elevated to equal footing with mitigation — a major advance over the Kyoto Protocol. |
| **5** | **Loss and Damage** | Art. 8 | Recognises the importance of **averting, minimising and addressing loss and damage** associated with climate change impacts, including extreme weather and slow-onset events. The **Warsaw International Mechanism** is placed under the Agreement's authority. Critically, Art. 8 does **not** create a liability or compensation mechanism (a red line for developed countries). |
| **6** | **Climate Finance** | Art. 9 | Developed countries **shall** (legally binding obligation) provide financial resources to assist developing countries with mitigation and adaptation. Art. 9.3 references the **$100 billion per year goal** (originally set for 2020, extended to 2025). Art. 9.4 prioritises particularly vulnerable countries (LDCs, SIDS). Art. 9.9 calls for **simplified approval procedures** for LDCs. |
| **7** | **Technology Development & Transfer** | Art. 10 | Establishes a **technology framework** to guide the Technology Mechanism in promoting and facilitating enhanced action on technology development and transfer. Art. 10.5 emphasises accelerating innovation and facilitating access to technology for developing countries, particularly at early stages of the technology cycle. |
| **8** | **Capacity-Building** | Art. 11 | Mandates capacity-building to enhance the ability of developing country Parties — **"in particular countries with the least capacity, such as the least developed countries"** (Art. 11.1) — to take effective climate action. Capacity-building must be **country-driven** and responsive to national needs. |
| **9** | **Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF)** | Art. 13 | Establishes an enhanced transparency framework for action and support with **built-in flexibility** (Art. 13.1–13.2) that takes into account Parties' different capacities. Art. 13.3 explicitly recognises **the special circumstances of LDCs and SIDS**. All Parties must submit biennial transparency reports (BTRs) but LDCs get flexibility in scope and timing. |
| **10** | **Global Stocktake & Ratchet Mechanism** | Art. 14; Art. 4.3, 4.9 | A **global stocktake** every five years (first in 2023) to assess collective progress. Stocktake outcomes **inform** Parties in updating their NDCs. Each successive NDC must be **more ambitious** than the previous one (Art. 4.3) — creating a **ratchet mechanism** that prevents backsliding. |

## Part B: Bangladesh's Position as an LDC Within the Paris Principles — ~10 marks

Bangladesh occupies a **paradoxical position** within the Paris Agreement framework: it is simultaneously one of the **most climate-vulnerable countries on earth** and one of the **lowest historical emitters** (<0.5% of global cumulative emissions). As an LDC with a population of 170+ million living in a low-lying delta, its position within each Paris principle reveals both the **strengths and limitations** of the Agreement for the world's most vulnerable nations.

### (i) CBDR-RC Principle (Art. 2.2) — Bangladesh's Strongest Shield

| Dimension | Bangladesh's Position |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Historical emissions** | <0.5% of global cumulative CO₂ emissions (vs. ~25% for US, ~23% for EU, ~13% for China) |
| **Per capita emissions** | ~0.6 tCO₂/year (global average: ~4.8 tCO₂; US: ~15 tCO₂) |
| **Vulnerability rank** | 7th most climate-vulnerable country (Global Climate Risk Index); 1st in damage-to-GDP ratio |
| **CBDR-RC implication** | No obligation for absolute emission reductions — only encouraged to enhance mitigation efforts "in light of different national circumstances" (Art. 4.4) |

**Analysis:** The CBDR-RC principle, rooted in Art. 2.2 and operationalised in Art. 4, provides Bangladesh with the **moral and legal foundation** to prioritise development and adaptation over mitigation. As Art. 4.1 explicitly states, "peaking [of emissions] will take longer for developing country Parties." Bangladesh can legitimately argue that its right to development — including increased energy consumption for industrialisation — takes precedence over emission reduction obligations.

> **Text Reference:** *"This Agreement will be implemented to reflect equity and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in the light of different national circumstances."* — Art. 2.2

### (ii) NDCs — Special Provisions for LDCs (Art. 4.6)

Art. 4.6 of the Paris Agreement creates a **special carve-out** for LDCs:

> *"The least developed countries and small island developing States may prepare and communicate strategies, plans and actions for low greenhouse gas emissions development reflecting their special circumstances."*

This means LDCs **may** (not shall) communicate NDCs; the obligation is **voluntary** rather than mandatory. Bangladesh has nonetheless submitted two ambitious NDCs:

| NDC | Year | Mitigation Target | Conditionality |
|-----|------|-------------------|----------------|
| **NDC-1** | 2015 | 5% unconditional, 15% conditional reduction from BAU by 2030 | Conditional on $40bn international support |
| **NDC-2 (Updated)** | 2021 | 6.7% unconditional, 21.8% conditional reduction from BAU by 2030 | Conditional on $56bn international support |

**Paradox:** Bangladesh submitted increasingly ambitious NDCs despite being under **no legal obligation** to do so as an LDC. This demonstrates **voluntary climate leadership** but also raises concerns about implementation feasibility given the high conditionality (both NDCs are substantially conditional on external finance).

### (iii) Climate Finance (Art. 9) — Access & Shortfall

| Provision | Relevance to Bangladesh |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Art. 9.1** — Developed countries "shall provide financial resources" | Legally binding obligation, but $100bn/year pledge (2009 Copenhagen) remains unmet — only ~$83bn mobilised by 2020 |
| **Art. 9.4** — Prioritise "least developed countries and small island developing States" | Explicit LDC preference in resource allocation |
| **Art. 9.9** — "Simplified approval procedures" for LDCs | Green Climate Fund (GCF) and other mechanisms have established simplified approval processes |
| **Adaptation finance** — Art. 9.4 calls for balance between mitigation and adaptation | BD needs ~$5–10 billion/year for adaptation alone (World Bank estimate) |

**The Finance Gap:**
| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Bangladesh's adaptation costs (annual) | $5–10 billion |
| Bangladesh's climate finance received (annual) | ~$1–2 billion (GCF + bilateral) |
| Global $100bn pledge fulfilment | ~$83bn (2020, OECD estimate) — shortfall persists |
| New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG, post-2025) | Under negotiation — developing countries demand $1 trillion+/year |

**Assessment:** While Art. 9 provides a **favourable legal framework** for Bangladesh — prioritised access, simplified procedures, adaptation balance — the **implementation gap** between pledged and delivered finance remains the single largest barrier to Bangladesh's climate resilience.

### (iv) Adaptation (Art. 7) — Bangladesh's Priority Arena

Adaptation is **Bangladesh's primary climate focus** — unlike developed countries that prioritise mitigation. Art. 7 provides several tools:

| Tool | Bangladesh's Engagement |
|------|------------------------|
| **National Adaptation Plans (NAPs)** — Art. 7.9(b) | Bangladesh has submitted NAP (2022) with $230 billion investment plan to 2050 |
| **Adaptation communications** — Art. 7.10–7.11 | Integrated into NDC submissions and national communications |
| **Recognition of adaptation efforts** — Art. 7.3 | Bangladesh's extensive adaptation programme (coastal embankments, cyclone shelters, drought-resistant crops) is internationally recognised |
| **Country-driven approach** — Art. 7.5 | BD's adaptation planning is integrated into national development planning (8th Five Year Plan, Delta Plan 2100) |

**Bangladesh's Adaptation Infrastructure at Scale:**
- 139 coastal polders protecting ~8 million people
- ~12,000 km of embankments
- ~4,000 cyclone shelters with capacity for ~3 million people
- Over 700,000 hectares of land brought under climate-resilient crop varieties
- Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 — 100-year strategic plan for water and land management ($37 billion investment)

### (v) Loss and Damage (Art. 8) — Critical but Insufficient

Art. 8 is **existentially important** for Bangladesh. As a country facing **irreversible loss and damage** — submergence of land, salinisation of aquifers, permanent displacement — the Loss and Damage mechanism is Bangladesh's most crucial provision.

| Art. 8 Element | Bangladesh Reality |
|----------------|-------------------|
| **Extreme weather events** (Art. 8.4(a)) | Cyclones (Sidr 2007, Amphan 2020), floods (2022 — 7.2 million affected) |
| **Slow onset events** (Art. 8.4(c)) | Sea-level rise (projected 0.5–1m by 2100), salinity intrusion, groundwater depletion |
| **Irreversible and permanent loss** (Art. 8.4(d)) | Land loss: 20–30% of land area could be inundated with 1m SLR; ~15 million climate migrants projected by 2050 |
| **Risk insurance facilities** (Art. 8.4(f)) | BD is part of the **V20** (Vulnerable Twenty Group) and supports the **Global Shield against Climate Risks** |
| **Non-economic losses** (Art. 8.4(g)) | Loss of cultural heritage, biodiversity (Sundarbans mangrove), community displacement |

**Critical Limitation:** Art. 8 explicitly operates on a **"cooperative and facilitative basis"** (Art. 8.3). The Decision accompanying the Paris Agreement (1/CP.21, para. 51) states that Art. 8 "does not involve or provide a basis for any liability or compensation." This means **Bangladesh cannot claim compensation** for climate-induced loss and damage — a deliberate exclusion insisted upon by developed countries.

> **COP28 Breakthrough (2023):** The **Loss and Damage Fund** was operationalised at COP28 — a major win for Bangladesh and the V20. However, initial pledges (~$700 million) are a fraction of estimated needs ($100–400 billion/year for developing countries).

### (vi) Technology Transfer (Art. 10) — Potential vs. Reality

Art. 10 promises technology development and transfer to developing countries. For Bangladesh:
- **Needs:** Solar energy technology, efficient irrigation, climate-resilient crop varieties, early warning systems, salinity-resistant construction materials
- **Reality:** IPR restrictions, limited technology transfer under the Technology Mechanism, lack of capacity to absorb advanced technologies
- **Art. 10.5** emphasises "facilitating access to technology, in particular for early stages of the technology cycle, to developing country Parties" — but implementation has been slow

### (vii) Capacity-Building (Art. 11) — Explicit LDC Focus

Art. 11.1 explicitly targets LDCs: *"Capacity-building under this Agreement should enhance the capacity and ability of developing country Parties, in particular countries with the least capacity, such as the least developed countries."*

For Bangladesh, capacity-building needs include:
- Technical expertise for greenhouse gas inventory preparation
- Institutional capacity for NDC implementation and monitoring
- Access to and management of climate finance
- Transparency reporting under Art. 13

### (viii) Transparency Framework (Art. 13) — Built-in Flexibility for LDCs

The Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) includes **built-in flexibility** (Art. 13.2):
- LDCs have **longer transition periods** and **reduced reporting requirements**
- Art. 13.3 recognises "special circumstances of the least developed countries and small island developing States"
- Technical expert review (Art. 13.11–13.12) includes assistance in identifying capacity-building needs for LDCs

**Challenge:** Despite flexibility, Bangladesh faces capacity constraints in preparing biennial transparency reports (BTRs), conducting national greenhouse gas inventories, and tracking NDC progress — particularly given the cross-ministerial coordination required.

## Overall Assessment: Bangladesh's Position in the Paris Architecture

### Strengths (Favourable Provisions)

| Principle | BD Advantage | Key Article |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| CBDR-RC | No emission reduction obligations; right to development prioritised | Art. 2.2, 4.4 |
| NDC flexibility | LDCs may (not shall) communicate mitigation strategies | Art. 4.6 |
| Finance priority | LDCs and SIDS prioritised for resource allocation | Art. 9.4, 9.9 |
| Adaptation focus | Global adaptation goal aligns with BD's primary need | Art. 7 |
| Loss & Damage | Formal recognition of L&D — existential for BD | Art. 8 |
| Capacity-building | Explicit LDC targeting | Art. 11.1 |
| Transparency flexibility | Reduced reporting burden for LDCs | Art. 13.2, 13.3 |

### Challenges (Implementation Gaps)

| Challenge | Mechanism | Impact on Bangladesh |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Finance gap** | $100bn/yr pledge unmet; adaptation finance shortfall | BD receives ~$1–2bn/yr against ~$5–10bn/yr need |
| **No liability for L&D** | Art. 8 is facilitative, not compensatory | BD cannot seek compensation for irreversible losses |
| **NDC implementation** | High conditionality (21.8% reduction requires $56bn external finance) | Risk of non-implementation if finance not delivered |
| **Just transition** | Art. 2 preamble + Art. 4.15 recognise just transition but BD's coal-dependent industrialisation may conflict | Potential tension between development pathway and mitigation expectations |
| **Transparency burden** | Despite flexibility, BTR preparation requires technical capacity BD may lack | Risk of non-compliance if capacity-building is inadequate |
| **Technology access** | Limited actual transfer despite Art. 10 provisions | BD remains dependent on imported technology |
| **Ratchet mechanism** | Each NDC must be more ambitious (Art. 4.3) | As BD develops, progressive NDC targets may become more demanding |

### Bangladesh's Negotiating Position: What BD Demands

As an LDC and current Chair of the **Climate Vulnerable Forum (CVF)** / **V20**, Bangladesh has articulated clear demands:

1. **Finance:** Full delivery of $100bn pledge; new collective quantified goal (NCQG) of $1 trillion+/year from 2025; 50:50 balance between adaptation and mitigation finance
2. **Loss and Damage:** Full operationalisation of the Loss and Damage Fund with adequate capitalisation; inclusion of non-economic losses
3. **Technology:** Removal of IPR barriers to climate technology transfer; concessional licensing for LDCs
4. **Capacity-building:** Sustained technical support for transparency reporting and NDC implementation
5. **Adaptation:** Doubling of adaptation finance; simplified access procedures for LDCs

## Exam Tips

1. **Structure your answer in two clear parts:** (A) 10 principles with Article references — 10 marks; (B) Bangladesh's position — 10 marks. The examiner expects equal treatment of both parts.

2. **Memorise key Article numbers.** Citing specific articles (Art. 2.2 for CBDR-RC, Art. 4.6 for LDC NDCs, Art. 9.4 for LDC finance priority, Art. 13.3 for LDC transparency flexibility) distinguishes a high-scoring answer.

3. **Use the CBDR-RC principle as your analytical framework.** It is the single most important principle for understanding Bangladesh's position and connects all other elements.

4. **Quantify where possible.** Exact figures carry weight:
   - BD's historical emissions: <0.5% global
   - BD's per capita emissions: ~0.6 tCO₂ vs global ~4.8 tCO₂
   - Adaptation cost: $5–10 billion/year
   - Finance received: ~$1–2 billion/year
   - $100bn pledge: ~$83bn delivered (shortfall)

5. **Show both sides.** The question asks for Bangladesh's "position" — demonstrate awareness of both favourable provisions and implementation challenges. Balance strengthens your argument.

6. **Connect to development economics.** The Paris Agreement is not just environmental policy — it's about the right to development, the development-climate nexus, and climate justice. Link to Todaro & Smith's development themes.

7. **Reference recent developments.** Mention COP28 (Loss and Damage Fund operationalisation, 2023) and the first Global Stocktake (2023) to demonstrate currency.

> **Professor's Corner:**
>
> The Paris Agreement represents a fundamental break from the Kyoto Protocol's top-down, legally binding emission targets. Its bottom-up architecture — where countries set their own NDCs — was the only politically feasible design that could secure universal participation (195 Parties signed, 194 ratified). But this very flexibility creates an **ambition problem**: collective NDCs are currently insufficient to meet the 1.5°C goal (current trajectory: ~2.5–2.9°C warming by 2100).
>
> For development economists, the Paris Agreement is best understood as an **institutional response to a global collective action problem** — climate change being the ultimate public bad (non-rival, non-excludable, global in scope). The CBDR-RC principle embeds **distributive justice** into climate governance, recognising that those who contributed least to the problem face the gravest consequences — a Rawlsian difference principle applied globally.
>
> Bangladesh's position illustrates the **climate-development paradox**: the very industrialisation pathway that lifted Bangladesh from low-income to lower-middle-income status (RMG exports, coal power, urbanisation) increases emissions. The Paris Agreement provides Bangladesh with legitimate "policy space" through CBDR-RC and LDC provisions, but this space is narrowing — as Bangladesh graduates from LDC status (scheduled 2026), it will face progressively stronger expectations to contribute to global mitigation. The just transition framework (preamble) is Bangladesh's key rhetorical tool for navigating this transition without sacrificing development gains.
>
> The critical unanswered question is whether the Paris Agreement's **facilitative, non-punitive compliance mechanism** (Art. 15) has sufficient teeth to ensure developed countries meet their finance obligations. Without enforceable finance commitments, the entire architecture — which depends on developed country support for developing country action — rests on voluntary cooperation.
>
> > **Key Theoretical Lens:** Climate change as a **global commons problem** (Hardin 1968, Ostrom 1990) — the atmosphere is a global commons, each country has incentive to free-ride, and the Paris Agreement's transparency + stocktake + ratchet mechanism represents a **polycentric governance** approach (Ostrom) rather than a centralised authority.
