# Part A — Types of Preston Curves with Health Technology Availability (*10 marks*)

> **Marking Guide [10 marks]:** Definition and concave shape of Preston Curve (2) | Four types of health technology availability — base case parallel shift, rich-biased, poor-biased, nonparametric hinge (6) | Preston's key finding — 75–90% from upward shifts (2)

## 1. The Preston Curve: Definition and Empirical Foundation

The **Preston Curve** is an empirical cross-sectional relationship between **life expectancy at birth** and **real per capita income** (GDP per capita, log scale). First documented by **Samuel H. Preston (1975)** in his seminal paper *"The Changing Relation between Mortality and Level of Economic Development"* (Population Studies, 29(2)), the curve illustrates a robust positive but **concave** (diminishing) relationship: as income rises, life expectancy increases, but the rate of improvement declines sharply at higher income levels.

Preston studied the relationship across three decades — the **1900s, 1930s, and 1960s** — finding that the same concave pattern held for each period. More recent work by **Spence & Lewis (2009)**, **Becker, Philipson & Soares (2005)**, and others has updated and extended the analysis.

**Diagram description:** A downward-sloping (from left to right), rapidly rising curve that flattens at higher income levels. The x-axis is log GDP per capita (rising left to right); the y-axis is life expectancy at birth (typically 40–85 years). At very low income levels ($500–$2,000), small increases in income are associated with large gains in life expectancy (steep slope). Above approximately $10,000–$15,000 per capita, the curve flattens substantially — further income increases produce minimal life expectancy gains.

### The Concave Shape: Diminishing Returns to Income

The concave shape reflects a fundamental principle in health economics:

| Income Level | Slope of Preston Curve | Interpretation |
|--------------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Low income** ($500–$2,000) | **Steep** — large gains in LE per $1 increase in income | Basic health needs (nutrition, clean water, sanitation, basic vaccinations) are highly income-elastic at low levels |
| **Middle income** ($2,000–$10,000) | **Moderate** | Epidemiological transition underway; health investments shift from infectious to chronic disease control |
| **High income** ($15,000+) | **Flat** — minimal additional gains from income growth | Health outcomes determined more by lifestyle, behaviour, and health system quality than by material deprivation |

**Key insight:** The diminishing returns mean that a **transfer of income from rich to poor** would increase average global health — assuming the relationship is causal (Preston's own caveat). However, this policy conclusion depends on whether the correlation reflects genuine causation from income to health.

---

## 2. Preston's Central Finding: Upward Shifts vs Income Growth

Preston's most significant finding was that the **entire curve shifted upward** over the course of the 20th century. Life expectancy increased in virtually all countries **independently of changes in income**. Using decomposition analysis, Preston estimated:

- **75–90% of the increase in global life expectancy** was due to **exogenous upward shifts in the curve** — improvements in health technology, education, public health infrastructure, and nutrition that benefited all countries regardless of income.
- **Only 10–25%** was attributable to **income growth itself** (movement *along* the curve).

This finding fundamentally reshaped development economics: it showed that **health improvements can and do occur without income growth** — and conversely, that income growth alone cannot explain the dramatic improvements in global health.

**What caused the upward shift?** Preston credited:
- **Education** — especially female education, which improves health-seeking behaviour and child care practices
- **Better medical technology** — vaccines, antibiotics, oral rehydration therapy (ORT)
- **Improved public health services** — clean water, sanitation, disease surveillance
- **Better nutrition** — calorie intake improvements, micronutrient supplementation

---

## 3. Four Types of Preston Curves by Health Technology Availability

The critical variable determining the *shape* and *shift* of the Preston Curve is the **availability and distribution of health technologies**. Three distinct scenarios (plus a non-parametric variant) capture how differential access to health technology alters the income–health relationship:

---

### Type (i): Base Case — New Technology Equally Applicable in All Countries (*Parallel Upward Shift*)

**Scenario:** A new health technology (e.g., a vaccine, ORT, antibiotics) becomes available and is **equally accessible** in all countries regardless of income level.

**Effect on the Preston Curve:** The entire curve shifts **upward in parallel** — life expectancy increases by approximately the same absolute amount at every income level.

**Historical examples:**
- The **global smallpox eradication campaign** (1967–1980) — a technology (vaccine) applied universally, benefiting rich and poor countries alike
- **Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)** for diarrhoeal disease — a low-cost technology (salt + sugar + water solution) that dramatically reduced child mortality in all developing countries
- **Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI)** — WHO's global vaccination programme raising coverage universally

**Key implication:** This was the dominant pattern in Preston's original data. The exogenous upward shifts accounted for the majority (75–90%) of life expectancy gains, and the parallel nature of the shift suggests that the most important health technologies were those that **did not require high income** to be effective.

```
Life Expectancy
    ↑
    |                                  ─── Post-technology
    |                                 ─── Pre-technology
    |                            ╱
    |                        ╱           (Parallel upward shift
    |                    ╱                at all income levels)
    |                ╱
    |            ╱
    |        ╱
    |    ╱
    |╱
    └─────────────────────────────────→ log GDP per capita
```

---

### Type (ii): Technology Benefits Rich More — Widening Gap (*Rich-Biased Shift*)

**Scenario:** A new health technology is disproportionately available or effective in **high-income countries**. This could be because the technology is expensive, requires advanced medical infrastructure, depends on skilled personnel, or is protected by patents and intellectual property regimes.

**Effect on the Preston Curve:** The curve shifts upward **more at high-income levels** than at low-income levels — widening the health gap between rich and poor countries.

**Historical examples:**
- **Antiretroviral therapy (ART) for HIV/AIDS** — when first introduced in the 1990s, ART cost $10,000–$15,000 per patient per year, placing it out of reach for sub-Saharan African countries where the epidemic was most severe. The result: a **divergence** in the Preston Curve, with African countries (South Africa, Zimbabwe, Botswana) falling below the curve while rich countries maintained their position.
- **Advanced cancer treatments** (immunotherapy, targeted therapy, proton beam therapy) — these are concentrated in high-income countries and contribute to the flattening of the curve at the top end (the rich get marginally healthier, but the poor do not benefit).
- **Robotic surgery, MRI diagnostics, intensive care units** — all require capital investments that poor countries cannot afford.

**Data:** Preston himself noted that while the upward shift was greatest in poor countries *for basic technologies*, a good portion of the potential gains from more advanced medical technology had **not been realised** in developing countries.

```
Life Expectancy
    ↑
    |                                     ─── Post-technology
    |                                    ─── Pre-technology
    |                               ╱
    |                          ╱    
    |                     ╱              (Rich-biased shift:
    |                ╱                    gap widens at high
    |            ╱                       income levels)
    |        ╱
    |    ╱
    |╱
    └─────────────────────────────────→ log GDP per capita
```

---

### Type (iii): Technology Benefits Poor More — Catching Up (*Poor-Biased Shift*)

**Scenario:** A new health technology is **disproportionately beneficial for low-income countries**. This typically occurs when the technology is: (a) inexpensive and simple to deliver, (b) targets diseases prevalent in poor countries, or (c) is subsidised or donated by international organisations.

**Effect on the Preston Curve:** The curve shifts upward **more at low-income levels** than at high-income levels — causing **convergence** in life expectancy as poor countries catch up.

**Historical examples:**
- **Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)** — costing only a few cents per treatment, ORT dramatically reduced diarrhoeal mortality in poor countries (saving an estimated 50+ million lives since the 1970s). Rich countries already had intravenous rehydration; the technology had little additional effect on them.
- **Insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs) for malaria** — distributed free or subsidised through global health initiatives (Global Fund, PMI, WHO), ITNs disproportionately benefit poor, malaria-endemic countries.
- **Measles vaccination** — the GAVI Alliance subsidised vaccine delivery in the poorest countries, reducing measles deaths by 78% globally (2000–2012), with the largest absolute reductions in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
- **Directly Observed Therapy Short-Course (DOTS) for tuberculosis** — a standardised, low-cost treatment protocol that disproportionately benefited poor countries with high TB burdens.

**Key implication:** Poor-biased technologies are the engine of **health convergence** — they narrow the gap in life expectancy between rich and poor countries and explain why health indicators have converged even as incomes have diverged (Becker et al. 2005).

```
Life Expectancy
    ↑
    |                                ─── Post-technology
    |                               ╱── Pre-technology
    |                          ╱
    |                     ╱
    |                ╱                (Poor-biased shift:
    |            ╱                    life expectancy gap
    |        ╱                       narrows as poor
    |    ╱                           countries catch up)
    |╱
    └─────────────────────────────────→ log GDP per capita
```

---

### Type (iv): Non-parametric "Hinge" — The Epidemiological Transition

**Scenario:** When the Preston Curve is estimated using **non-parametric regression** (rather than assuming a parametric log-linear form), it reveals a distinctive **"hinge"** — a kink where the slope changes sharply at a specific income threshold.

**The turning point:** Approximately **$2,045 per capita** (in year 2000 purchasing power parity dollars). This is roughly the per capita income level of India at that time.

**What the hinge represents:** The **epidemiological transition** — a fundamental shift in the disease burden and mortality pattern of a society:

| Below the Hinge (< $2,045) | Above the Hinge (> $2,045) |
|----------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Infectious diseases** dominate | **Chronic diseases** dominate |
| **High infant and child mortality** | Old-age mortality predominates |
| **Maternal mortality** significant | Declining maternal mortality |
| **Malaria, diarrhoea, pneumonia** primary causes | **Heart disease, cancer, diabetes** primary causes |
| Health interventions are **highly cost-effective** (vaccines, ORT, bed nets) | Health interventions have **lower marginal returns** |
| **Steep slope** — income matters a lot for health | **Flat slope** — income matters less; lifestyle and health systems matter more |

**Why this matters for development policy:**

The hinge implies that crossing the $2,045 threshold fundamentally changes the relationship between income and health. Development strategies must adapt:

1. **Below the hinge:** The priority is **cost-effective public health interventions** — vaccines, ORT, bed nets, sanitation, clean water. These have enormous marginal returns in life expectancy per dollar spent.
2. **Above the hinge:** The priority shifts to **health system strengthening** — chronic disease management, health insurance, primary care networks. Income growth alone will not deliver health improvements; institutional and behavioural factors dominate.

## 4. Summary Table: Four Types of Preston Curves

| Type | Description | Effect on Curve | Historical Example | Distribution of Benefit |
|------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **(i) Base case — parallel shift** | Technology equally applicable in all countries | Uniform upward shift at all income levels | Smallpox eradication, EPI, ORT | **Equal** — all countries gain proportionally |
| **(ii) Rich-biased shift** | Technology disproportionately benefits high-income countries | Curve shifts up more at high incomes; gap widens | Early ART for HIV/AIDS, advanced cancer treatments | **Regressive** — rich gain more |
| **(iii) Poor-biased shift** | Technology disproportionately benefits low-income countries | Curve shifts up more at low incomes; gap narrows | ORT, ITNs, measles vaccination, DOTS for TB | **Progressive** — poor gain more |
| **(iv) Non-parametric hinge** | Structural kink at ~$2,045/capita marking epidemiological transition | Slope changes sharply at the income threshold | Transition from infectious to chronic disease burden | **Phase change** — the nature of the income-health relationship itself changes |

## 5. Updating Preston: Contemporary Evidence

### Spence & Lewis (2009) — Stronger Fit

Analysis of more recent data by **Michael Spence and Maureen Lewis** suggests that the Preston Curve's "fit" (explanatory power) has actually **strengthened** in the decades since Preston's original study. The relationship between income and life expectancy has become tighter, with less variance around the curve.

However, they note a critical nuance: **the source of income growth matters**. Countries whose growth is driven by the **mining sector** (Ryan Edwards' finding) tend to lie below the curve — i.e., mineral wealth does not translate into health improvements as effectively as growth from other sectors (manufacturing, services). This connects to the broader **resource curse** literature.

### Becker, Philipson & Soares (2005) — Health Convergence Despite Income Divergence

Becker et al. found a striking paradox:
- **Income divergence:** Cross-country incomes have **diverged** significantly since 1960 (rich countries grew faster or maintained their advantage).
- **Health convergence:** Life expectancy and other health indicators have **converged** globally (poor countries narrowed the gap with rich countries).

This is precisely what the **poor-biased shift** (Type iii) predicts: health technologies that disproportionately benefit the poor have been powerful enough to overcome the divergenceary effect of income inequality.

### Critique: Lack of Longitudinal Evidence

A limitation of Preston's original analysis is that the curve is a **cross-sectional** relationship (snapshot across countries at one point in time). Some research suggests that the same relationship does **not** hold within countries over time — changes in a single country's income may not produce the same health gains that cross-sectional comparisons predict. This is known as the **ecological fallacy** problem.

---

## 6. Reverse Causality — Does Health Cause Income?

A fundamental challenge to interpreting the Preston Curve is **reverse causality**: perhaps better health (higher life expectancy) causes higher incomes, rather than the other way around.

| Direction of Causality | Mechanism | Evidence |
|------------------------|-----------|----------|
| **Income → Health** | Higher income enables better nutrition, housing, health care, sanitation | Preston Curve correlation; Pritchett & Summers (1996) — growth prevented 500K+ child deaths |
| **Health → Income** | Healthier workers are more productive; healthier children learn better; healthier individuals save more | Sachs (2001) — malaria burden explains tropical poverty; Bloom & Canning (2000) — 1-year increase in LE → 4% increase in output |
| **Bidirectional** | Both directions operate simultaneously; OLS estimates of income on health are biased upward | Instrumental variables studies suggest income matters more in poor countries, health matters more in rich countries |

**Implication:** Any estimates of the impact of income on life expectancy from simple regressions may **overestimate** the importance of income. This reinforces Preston's core finding that the upward shifts (technology, education, public health) mattered more than income growth.

## 1. The Central Puzzle

If the Preston Curve predicts average life expectancy at a given income level, individual countries can, and frequently do, lie **above or below** the curve. Countries with nearly identical GDP per capita often have vastly different life expectancies. This variance is not random — it reveals systematic differences in how societies translate economic resources into health outcomes.

**Key cases of divergence:**

|---------|--------------------------|-----------------|-----------------------------------|
| United States | ~$76,000 | 77–79 yrs | **Below** the curve |
| Japan | ~$42,000 | 84 yrs | **Above** the curve |
| Cuba | ~$9,500 | 79 yrs | **Far above** the curve |
| Sri Lanka | ~$3,800 | 77 yrs | **Far above** the curve |
| Bangladesh | ~$2,800 | 70–73 yrs | **Above** the curve |

## 2. Five Factors Explaining the Divergence

The literature identifies five interconnected determinants of life expectancy that operate independently of income:

---

### Factor 1: Health System Quality — Coverage, Accessibility, and Primary Care

| Variable | High-Life-Expectancy Country | Low-Life-Expectancy Country |
|----------|------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Health financing** | Universal coverage, tax-funded or social insurance | Out-of-pocket spending dominant |
| **Primary care emphasis** | Strong primary care gatekeeping, preventive focus | Hospital-centric, curative focus |
| **Workforce** | High doctor-to-population ratio, community health workers | Shortage of trained personnel |
| **Access equity** | Low financial barriers to access | User fees, informal payments |

**The Cuba paradox:** Cuba achieves a life expectancy of **79 years** — comparable to the United States (77–79 years) — despite an income level that is less than **one-tenth** of the US level. Explanations include:

1. **Universal, free health care** as a constitutional right — no financial barrier to access
2. **Primary care emphasis** — the *médico de la familia* (family doctor) programme assigns every neighbourhood a physician-nurse team responsible for prevention, monitoring, and treatment
3. **Prevention over cure** — massive investment in vaccination, maternal health, and chronic disease management at the primary care level
4. **High doctor density** — Cuba has one of the highest doctor-to-population ratios globally
5. **Community embeddedness** — health campaigns delivered through mass organisations (CDRs, FMC) reaching every household

However, Cuba's experience also demonstrates a vulnerability: during the **"Special Period"** (post-Soviet economic collapse in the 1990s), health indicators temporarily deteriorated, showing that health systems need economic foundations even if income alone does not determine outcomes.

**The US anomaly:** The United States spends **~17% of GDP** on health — by far the highest globally — yet has lower life expectancy than all other high-income countries. Reasons include:
- Fragmented, fee-for-service insurance system leaving millions uninsured or underinsured
- High inequality in access and quality
- High rates of obesity, drug overdoses, and gun violence (epidemiological exceptionalism)
- Weak primary care relative to specialist care

---

### Factor 2: Public Goods Provision — Water, Sanitation, and Disease Control

Health outcomes depend critically on **public goods** that markets underprovide. Countries that invested early in water supply, sanitation, vaccination, and vector control achieved dramatic health gains at relatively low cost.

| Public Good | Health Mechanism | Impact on Life Expectancy |
|-------------|------------------|---------------------------|
| **Clean water** | Reduces waterborne diseases (cholera, typhoid, diarrhoea) | Responsible for ~50% of mortality decline in 19th/20th centuries (Cutler & Miller 2005) |
| **Sanitation** | Reduces faecal-oral transmission, improves nutrition | WHO: 2.5 billion people still lack improved sanitation |
| **Vaccination** | Prevents infectious disease mortality | 2–3 million deaths prevented annually (WHO) |
| **Vector control** | Reduces malaria, dengue, sleeping sickness | ITNs reduced malaria mortality by 60% in sub-Saharan Africa |
| **Health education** | Improves hygiene, nutrition, health-seeking behaviour | Particularly important for maternal and child health |

**Sri Lanka's success:** Sri Lanka achieved a life expectancy of **77 years** despite a per capita income of only ~$3,800. The explanation lies in early, sustained investment in public goods:
- Universal free education from 1945
- Free health care from 1951
- Extensive malaria eradication programme (1940s–1960s)
- High female literacy (>92%)

Sri Lanka's health achievements **predated** its economic growth — the classic case of "growth-following-health" rather than "health-following-growth" (Caldwell 1986).

---

### Factor 3: Inequality — Income, Gender, and Health Inequality

More equal societies tend to have better average health outcomes, independent of average income (Wilkinson & Pickett 2009, *The Spirit Level*).

| Dimension of Inequality | Mechanism | Example |
|------------------------|-----------|---------|
| **Income inequality** | More unequal societies underinvest in public goods; the poor cannot afford private health care; social stress affects health | US (Gini ~0.41) vs Japan (Gini ~0.33) — Japan's life expectancy 84 vs US 77–79 |
| **Gender inequality** | Women's education and agency directly improve child survival and family health | Bangladesh's female education investment improved child health outcomes dramatically |
| **Geographic inequality** | Rural-urban health gaps; regional disparities within countries | Kerala (India) life expectancy 75 yrs vs Uttar Pradesh 64 yrs — within the same country |
| **Racial/ethnic inequality** | Discrimination affects access to health services and quality of care | US Black life expectancy (72 yrs) vs White (79 yrs) — a 7-year gap |

**The Japan–US contrast:** Japan achieves the world's highest life expectancy (84 years) with a per capita income of ~$42,000, while the United States achieves only 77–79 years at ~$76,000. The gap is primarily explained by:
- **Lower inequality** in Japan (Gini 0.33 vs US 0.41)
- **Universal health insurance** (Japan's system covers all citizens since 1961)
- **Diet and lifestyle** (lower obesity rates: 4% in Japan vs 36% in US)
- **Social cohesion** (stronger community networks, less social isolation)

---

### Factor 4: Institutional Quality — Governance, Corruption, and Policy Effectiveness

The ability of a state to design and implement health policy depends on institutional quality. Poor governance — corruption, weak bureaucracy, political instability — undermines health outcomes even when income is adequate.

| Institutional Dimension | Health Impact | Country Evidence |
|------------------------|---------------|------------------|
| **Corruption** | Diverts health budget from services to private pockets; fake medicines; ghost workers on payroll | Nigeria: 70% of health budget lost to corruption (Transparency International) |
| **Rule of law** | Enforces building codes (earthquake resilience), drug quality standards, environmental regulations | Chile's building codes → much lower earthquake mortality than Haiti (2010) |
| **Government effectiveness** | Capacity to deliver vaccination programmes, disease surveillance, emergency response | Thailand's universal coverage scheme achieved via strong bureaucratic capacity |
| **Political stability** | Avoids disruption of health services; enables long-term planning | Botswana's democratic stability enabled effective ART programme despite high HIV prevalence |

**Botswana vs South Africa:** At similar income levels (~$6,000–$7,000 per capita) and both facing severe HIV/AIDS epidemics, the two countries had divergent health trajectories:

| Indicator | Botswana | South Africa |
|-----------|----------|--------------|
| **Life expectancy (1990)** | ~65 yrs | ~62 yrs |
| **Life expectancy (2005 — peak HIV)** | ~50 yrs | ~53 yrs |
| **Life expectancy (2020)** | ~70 yrs | ~62 yrs |
| **HIV prevalence (adults)** | ~20% | ~19% |
| **ART coverage** | Rapid scale-up from 2002; 85%+ coverage | Slower political response; denialism under Mbeki (1999–2008) delayed ART rollout |

The divergence is largely explained by **governance**: Botswana's stable democratic institutions and low corruption enabled a faster, more effective HIV/AIDS response, while South Africa's government under President Mbeki infamously denied the link between HIV and AIDS, delaying treatment access for nearly a decade.

---

### Factor 5: Cultural and Behavioural Factors — Diet, Lifestyle, Social Norms

Beyond income, health systems, and institutions, cultural and behavioural factors independently shape health outcomes:

| Factor | Health Impact | Country Evidence |
|--------|---------------|------------------|
| **Diet** | Heart disease, diabetes, cancer rates | Japan (low heart disease — fish, vegetables, low saturated fat); US (high obesity — processed food, sugar-sweetened beverages) |
| **Smoking** | Lung cancer, COPD, heart disease | Russia (one of highest smoking rates → male LE 68 yrs); Australia (strict tobacco control → male LE 81 yrs) |
| **Alcohol consumption** | Liver disease, accidents, violence | Eastern Europe (high binge drinking → excess male mortality) |
| **Exercise and physical activity** | Obesity, cardiovascular health, diabetes | Netherlands (cycling culture → lower obesity 13%) vs US (car-dependent → obesity 36%) |
| **Social cohesion** | Stress reduction, mental health, health information sharing | Mediterranean dietary culture + strong family networks → Italy's longevity despite limited health spending |
| **Gender norms** | Health-seeking behaviour, maternal mortality, child nutrition | Bangladesh's community health workers reach women in purdah through home visits |

**The Mediterranean paradox:** Greece, Italy, and Spain achieve life expectancies above 82 years with health spending below the OECD average — reflecting the protective effects of the Mediterranean diet, strong family support networks, and lower social stress.

## 3. The South Asian Comparison: Bangladesh vs India vs Pakistan

The most striking example of life expectancy divergence at similar income levels comes from South Asia:

| Indicator | Bangladesh | India | Pakistan |
|-----------|------------|-------|----------|
| **GDP per capita (approx.)** | ~$2,800 | ~$2,500 | ~$1,600 |
| **Life expectancy** | **70–73 yrs** | **67 yrs** | **66 yrs** |
| **Female LE advantage over male** | ~3 yrs | ~3 yrs | ~2 yrs |
| **Infant mortality (per 1,000)** | ~31 | ~47 | ~59 |
| **Under-5 mortality (per 1,000)** | ~40 | ~57 | ~72 |
| **Maternal mortality (per 100,000)** | ~173 | ~97 | ~186 |
| **Female literacy** | ~73% | ~66% | ~46% |

Bangladesh — with income levels **similar to or lower than India** — outperforms both India and Pakistan on life expectancy and child survival. This is the **Bangladesh paradox** extended from social indicators to health outcomes.

### Kabeer's Explanation: Agency, Women's Education, and Community Health

**Naila Kabeer** explains Bangladesh's superior health performance through three interconnected channels:

1. **Women's agency and renegotiation of patriarchy:** Bangladesh's development model — built on women's paid employment (especially RMG), microfinance, and NGO-led health programmes — gave women resources (income, knowledge, mobility) that they used to improve health-seeking behaviour for themselves and their children. This is distinct from India and Pakistan, where women's labour force participation is lower and patriarchal constraints remain more binding.

2. **Investment in girls' education:** Bangladesh achieved near-gender parity in primary and secondary education through targeted programs (stipends for girls, food-for-education). Female education is the single strongest predictor of child survival (WHO): each additional year of maternal education reduces child mortality by 7–9%.

3. **Community health workers and NGO programmes:** BRAC's community health volunteer programme (Shasthya Shebika) — 100,000+ female health workers — delivered doorstep health services to women who could not otherwise access care due to purdah restrictions. No comparable programme exists at scale in India or Pakistan.

| Factor | Bangladesh | India | Pakistan |
|--------|------------|-------|----------|
| **Female LFPR** | ~36–42% | ~20–25% | ~22% |
| **Girls' secondary enrolment** | ~75% | ~72% | ~44% |
| **Community health workers** | 100,000+ (BRAC) | ASHA workers (1M but lower coverage density) | Lady Health Workers (~100K, lower population coverage) |
| **NGO health programmes** | Extensive (BRAC, Grameen, ICDDR,B) | Moderate | Limited |
| **Family planning access** | High — contraceptive prevalence ~62% | Moderate — ~56% | Low — ~34% |
| **Childhood vaccination** | ~97% (DPT3) | ~91% | ~84% |

## 4. The Capabilities Approach: Beyond Income

The life expectancy divergence at similar income levels is a direct empirical illustration of **Amartya Sen's capabilities approach** — the most important theoretical framework underpinning this question.

### The Core Distinction

| Approach | Focus | Policy Implication |
|----------|-------|-------------------|
| **Utility/income approach** (standard neoclassical) | Maximising income and consumption | Growth first — health improvements will follow automatically |
| **Capabilities approach** (Sen, Nussbaum) | What people are able to do and be (functionings) | Health, education, and social arrangements matter *directly* for wellbeing; income is only a *means*, not an *end* |

### Sen's Argument Applied to Health

Sen (1999, *Development as Freedom*) argues that:
- Income is **instrumental** to wellbeing, not constitutive of it
- What matters is the **conversion of income into capabilities** — which depends on personal characteristics (age, health status), social arrangements (public services, gender norms), and environmental conditions (epidemiological environment, climate)
- Two people with identical incomes can have vastly different capabilities if one faces discrimination, lives in a region with weak public services, or lacks education
- This is exactly what we observe in life expectancy comparisons: Cuba converts a relatively low income into exceptional health outcomes because of its social arrangements; the US converts a very high income into only average health outcomes because of inequality and fragmented health care

### Health as a Central Capability

Martha Nussbaum identifies **bodily health** as one of ten central human capabilities. Its absence — premature mortality, preventable morbidity — is a fundamental deprivation that constrains all other capabilities. This is why life expectancy is not merely one development indicator among many but a **primary measure of development success**.

## 5. Reverse Causality: When Health Drives Income

Part A discussed reverse causality (health → income) as a statistical challenge for interpreting the Preston Curve. In Part B, this same mechanism becomes a **substantive explanation** for divergence:

Countries that successfully improve health outcomes may **accelerate economic growth**, creating a virtuous cycle that explains their position above the Preston Curve:

| Health Improvement → | Economic Effect |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| Reduced child mortality | Lower fertility → demographic dividend (fewer dependents per worker) |
| Improved nutrition | Higher labour productivity, especially in manual occupations |
| Better disease control | Reduced absenteeism, higher FDI attraction |
| Higher life expectancy | More incentive to invest in education (longer payoff period) |
| Healthier students | More learning per year of schooling — better human capital quality |

**Bloom, Canning & Sevilla (2004)** estimate that a **1-year increase in life expectancy** raises GDP per capita by **4%** — a substantial effect that operates through productivity, investment, and education.

This means that the countries that invest early in health (Sri Lanka, Costa Rica, Kerala) may be **building the foundation for future growth**, not just consuming the fruits of past growth.

---

## 6. Broader Implications for Development Policy

### What This Means for Policy

| Policy Implication | Evidence Base | Example |
|--------------------|---------------|---------|
| **Growth is not enough** — health requires deliberate investment in health systems, public goods, and social arrangements | Preston's 75–90% from upward shifts; variance at similar income levels | Cuba, Sri Lanka |
| **Primary care and prevention** are more cost-effective than curative hospital care for improving population health | Cuba's *médico de la familia* model; Costa Rica's primary-care-led system | Costa Rica achieves life expectancy 80 yrs at $12,000 per capita |
| **Women's education and agency** are among the most powerful health interventions | Kabeer (Bangladesh); Subbarao & Raney (1995) | Bangladesh's girls' education stipend programme |
| **Inequality reduction** improves average health | Wilkinson & Pickett (2009); Preston Curve logic of diminishing returns | Japan's low inequality + universal coverage |
| **Institutional quality** enables effective health spending | Botswana vs South Africa HIV response | Chile's health system reforms (1980s–2000s) |
| **International health technology diffusion** can accelerate convergence | Global Fund, GAVI, WHO programmes have compressed the epidemiological transition | ORT, ITNs, ART scale-up |

### The Health Transition Framework

Caldwell (1986) and Riley (2001) argue that health transition — the shift from high to low mortality — depends on a **"health social system"** comprising:

1. Health services (access, quality, coverage)
2. Education (especially female)
3. Nutrition (calorie sufficiency, micronutrients)
4. Household behaviour (hygiene, health-seeking, breastfeeding)
5. Social organisation (community health networks, women's status)

Countries that invest in **all five components simultaneously** achieve health outcomes decades ahead of their income level. This is the explanation for Sri Lanka, Costa Rica, Kerala, and Cuba — and the explanation for why Bangladesh outperforms India and Pakistan.

---

## 7. Critical Caveat: Correlation vs Causation

While the five factors explain much of the cross-country variance in life expectancy at similar income levels, three important caveats apply:

1. **Endogeneity:** The five factors are not independent of income. Richer countries tend to have better institutions, lower inequality, and stronger health systems. Causal identification is difficult.

2. **Historical path dependence:** Current health outcomes reflect decades of investment (or neglect). A country that invested in public health from 1950 (Sri Lanka, Costa Rica) will have different outcomes from one that began investing only in 1990, even at the same current income.

3. **Global context matters:** The Preston Curve itself has shifted over time. Countries that achieved independence in 1960 faced different global health conditions (pre-HIV/AIDS, pre-ART, pre-GAVI) than those that began development in 2000.

## Summary Table: Part A — Four Types of Preston Curves

| Type | Description | Curve Effect | Example | Benefit Distribution |
|------|-------------|--------------|---------|---------------------|
| **(i) Base case — parallel shift** | Technology equally applicable in all countries | Uniform upward shift at all income levels | Smallpox eradication, EPI | **Equal** |
| **(ii) Rich-biased shift** | Technology benefits high-income countries disproportionately | Curve shifts up more at high incomes; gap widens | Early ART, advanced cancer treatments | **Regressive** |
| **(iii) Poor-biased shift** | Technology benefits low-income countries disproportionately | Curve shifts up more at low incomes; gap narrows | ORT, ITNs, DOTS for TB | **Progressive** |
| **(iv) Non-parametric hinge** | Structural kink at ~$2,045/capita | Slope changes sharply at the income threshold | Epidemiological transition | **Phase change** |

## Exam Tips

| Tip | Detail |
|-----|--------|
| **Part A: Draw the curves** | In an exam, sketching the three shift types (parallel, rich-biased, poor-biased) alongside the hinge adds analytical depth. Describe the axis labels and shape verbally. |
| **Use Preston's 75–90% figure** | The single most important statistic. Memorise it: "Preston found that 75–90% of the increase in life expectancy was due to upward shifts in the curve (health technology, education, public health), not income growth." |
| **Name the hinge threshold** | The $2,045/capita (2000 data) epidemiological transition threshold is distinctive. Mentioning it signals you have read beyond the textbook. |
| **Part B: Lead with the Cuba paradox** | The Cuba–US comparison is the most dramatic illustration of income not determining health. Open Part B with this to establish the thesis immediately. |
| **Always include the South Asia triangle** | Bangladesh–India–Pakistan comparison is regionally relevant and shows you understand the Bangladesh paradox in the health context. |
| **Connect to theory — Sen's capabilities approach** | The highest marks go to answers that frame the empirical divergence within a theoretical framework. Sen provides this: income is a means, not an end. |
| **Note reverse causality** | Mentioning that health → income (Bloom, Canning & Sevilla) as well as income → health shows awareness of econometric endogeneity — a distinction-level point. |
| **Use the five-factor framework** | Organise Part B clearly around the five factors. Examiners give credit for structured, systematic answers. |
| **Pritchett & Summers counterpoint** | Mention that the Preston Curve has also been used to argue that growth alone improves health (Pritchett & Summers 1996) — but note Preston's own finding that upward shifts dominated. |
| **Acknowledge HIV/AIDS** | Note that the 1990s–2000s HIV/AIDS epidemic in sub-Saharan Africa disrupted the historical convergence pattern — countries like South Africa and Botswana fell below the curve temporarily. |

## Professor's Corner

> *"The Preston Curve question integrates **two** distinct skills: (1) understanding an empirical relationship and its variants (Part A), and (2) explaining variance around that relationship (Part B). The strongest answers connect both parts — the same health systems, institutions, and public goods that shift the curve upward (Part A) are the ones that explain why some countries lie above or below it (Part B).*
>
> *On Part A, the key conceptual move is distinguishing between **movements along** the curve (income growth) and **shifts of** the curve (technology, education, public health). Preston's decomposition — 75–90% from shifts versus 10–25% from growth — is the single most important number in the lecture. The four types of technology availability (parallel, rich-biased, poor-biased, hinge) are a framework for understanding when health technology promotes convergence versus divergence in global health.*
>
> *On Part B, the theoretical anchor is **Sen's capabilities approach**. The question 'Why do similar-income countries have different life expectancy?' is the empirical refutation of the utility/income approach to wellbeing. If income were all that mattered for welfare, life expectancy would be perfectly correlated with GDP — it is not. Every country example is a case study in how social arrangements convert (or fail to convert) income into health. Cuba does it through universal primary care; Bangladesh through women's agency and community health workers; Japan through low inequality and healthy diet; the United States fails to do it because of fragmented insurance and high inequality.*
>
> *Finally, note the **implicit Bangladesh connection** throughout this question. Bangladesh is a positive outlier in the Preston Curve — lying above the curve given its income. The 'Bangladesh paradox' (Kabeer) and the life expectancy question are two expressions of the same phenomenon. The factors that explain Bangladesh's outlier status — women's agency, girls' education, NGO health programmes — are the same factors that distinguish it from India and Pakistan. Use this connection to show thematic consistency across the course."*
>
> — **ECON 503 Teaching Team**

## Key Definitions Quick Reference

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Preston Curve** | Empirical cross-sectional relationship between life expectancy and log GDP per capita; concave shape shows diminishing returns (Preston 1975) |
| **Epidemiological transition** | Shift from infectious disease burden to chronic disease burden as countries develop; marked by a hinge in the Preston Curve at ~$2,045/capita |
| **Capabilities approach** | Sen's framework: wellbeing is measured by what people can do and be (functionings), not by income or utility alone |
| **Health transition** | The shift from high to low mortality, driven by the interaction of health services, education, nutrition, and household behaviour (Caldwell 1986) |
| **Reverse causality** | The possibility that health causes income (through productivity, education, savings) rather than income causing health |
| **Health social system** | The institutional and social arrangements through which a society produces health outcomes (Riley 2001) |
| **Diminishing returns to income** | The concave shape of the Preston Curve: additional income has larger health effects at low incomes than at high incomes |
| **Non-parametric regression** | A statistical method that does not assume a specific functional form (e.g., log-linear), revealing the hinge in the Preston Curve |
| **Bangladesh paradox** | Bangladesh's achievement of social and health outcomes far exceeding its income-predicted levels (Kabeer 2011) |
