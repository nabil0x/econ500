## Part A: What is Unpaid Employment? (10 marks)

### 1. Definition — ICLS Framework (2 marks)

Unpaid employment refers to work that contributes to economic output but is not remunerated through wages or salary. The 19th International Conference of Labour Statisticians (ICLS 2013) defines it under the formal category of **"contributing family workers"** — persons who work without pay in a market-oriented establishment operated by a related person living in the same household. The key ICLS criteria are:

| Criterion | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Market orientation** | The establishment produces goods/services primarily for sale/exchange, not solely for own final use |
| **Family relationship** | The worker is related to the owner/operator of the establishment |
| **Same household** | The related operator lives in the same household as the worker |
| **No remuneration** | The worker receives no wage, salary, or in-kind payment directly tied to labour input |
| **Working time** | Must have worked at least one hour in the reference week (consistent with the "one-hour" employment threshold) |

This ICLS definition sits within a broader classification of forms of work under the **19th ICLS resolution**:

```
                    ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
                    │        Total Work Activity           │
                    └─────────────────────────────────────┘
                                      │
        ┌─────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┐
        │                             │                             │
   Employment Work          Own-use Production Work     Other Work Activities
        │                    (goods & services for        (unpaid trainee,
        │                     own final use)              volunteer work)
        │
    ┌───┴───┐
    │       │
  Paid   Unpaid
  Employee Family
          Worker
```

Unpaid employment is thus **one specific form of work** within the SNA (System of National Accounts) production boundary — it counts in GDP. It must be distinguished from:

- **Own-use production work**: producing goods (e.g., subsistence farming) or services (e.g., childcare, cooking for household consumption) for one's own final use. These activities may fall inside or outside the SNA production boundary depending on the type.
- **Paid employment**: work performed for wage, salary, or profit, with a clear employer-employee relationship and monetary or in-kind compensation.

### 2. Feminist Economics Critique (3 marks)

The feminist economics tradition (Benería 1979; Floro 1995; Elson 1999) offers a fundamental critique of how unpaid work is treated in standard labour statistics:

**a) Systematic invisibility in the SNA**

The System of National Accounts explicitly excludes unpaid domestic and care work from GDP measurement. Only unpaid work that produces goods within the SNA production boundary (subsistence agriculture, water collection, processing primary products) is counted. The vast majority of unpaid work — childcare, cooking, cleaning, eldercare, emotional labour — is classified as "non-economic" activity. Elson (1999) argues this is not a technical oversight but a **gendered conceptual framework** that devalues activities predominantly performed by women.

**b) The "Three-Budget" Problem (Floro 1995)**

Floro demonstrates that unpaid work creates a **time poverty** constraint that limits women's ability to engage in paid employment. Women face three competing budgets:
- **Paid work budget** — earning income
- **Unpaid domestic/care budget** — maintaining the household and reproducing the labour force
- **Personal time budget** — rest, sleep, education, political participation

When the unpaid care burden is large, women's participation in paid work becomes constrained to part-time, informal, or home-based arrangements — reinforcing the secondary-earner pattern.

**c) Estimates of unpaid work's economic contribution**

Research across developing countries estimates that unpaid work constitutes **30–50% of total economic activity**. In Bangladesh specifically, time-use studies indicate women spend 3–4 times more hours on unpaid care work than men. If this were valued at market wages, it would add significantly to measured GDP — but its exclusion biases policy toward paid, male-dominated sectors.

### 3. Measurement Issues and Underestimation (2 marks)

Official statistics systematically underestimate unpaid employment for several reasons:

| Issue | Explanation |
|-------|-------------|
| **Exclusion of unpaid domestic/care work** | The ICLS definition counts only "contributing family workers" in market-oriented establishments. The vast majority of women's unpaid work (childcare, cooking, water collection) is excluded from employment statistics entirely. |
| **Subsistence agriculture borderline** | Producing food for own consumption is counted as employment (own-use production of goods), but only if the household sells some output. Pure subsistence is often missed or inconsistently reported. |
| **Proxy response bias** | In Bangladesh, male household heads often respond for all members. Women's economic contributions — especially part-time, seasonal, or home-based work — are systematically underreported by proxy respondents. |
| **Own-account workers (50.6%)** | Over half of Bangladesh's employed population are own-account workers. Their remuneration is irregular and unstable — they may effectively work without pay for extended periods, yet the LFS classifies them as "employed" rather than unpaid. |
| **Threshold effects** | The "one-hour" criterion for employment captures very low-intensity work. But many contributing family workers who worked 0 hours in the reference week (due to seasonality, illness, or care responsibilities) slip into "not in labour force" — underestimating their attachment to work. |

**Bangladesh LFS 2022 data (contributing family workers):**

| Category | Total (Million) | % of Employed | Male (Million) | Female (Million) |
|----------|-----------------|---------------|----------------|------------------|
| Contributing family workers | 4.78 | 6.8% | 1.67 | 3.11 |
| Rural | 4.01 | — | 1.22 | 2.79 |
| Urban | 0.77 | — | 0.45 | 0.32 |

The gender disparity is stark: **65% of contributing family workers are women** (3.11M of 4.78M), despite women making up only 35.3% of the total labour force. Women are disproportionately concentrated in the most vulnerable, least-remunerated employment status.

### 4. Kabeer's Connection — Work Without Agency (2 marks)

Naila Kabeer's analytical framework connects unpaid employment directly to her broader work on gender, poverty, and agency:

**a) Resources → Agency → Achievements framework**

Kabeer's framework (1999) identifies three dimensions of empowerment:
- **Resources**: material, human, and social resources that enable choice
- **Agency**: the ability to define goals and act upon them
- **Achievements**: the outcomes of exercising agency

Unpaid employment is a case of **work without agency** — women contribute economically but:
- Lack **recognition** as workers (not counted as "employed" in the full sense)
- Lack **income** (no wage, no independent financial resources to bargain within the household)
- Lack **social security** (no pension, no sick leave, no maternity protection)
- Lack **bargaining power** (because contribution is invisible, women cannot translate it into household decision-making authority)

**b) From unpaid work to the "care economy"**

Kabeer links unpaid employment to the broader "care economy" (Razavi 2007). Unpaid care work is the **invisible foundation** on which the paid economy rests — without women's unpaid labour in reproducing the labour force (raising children, maintaining households, caring for the elderly), the formal economy could not function. Policies that recognise and reduce unpaid work burdens (public childcare, water/electricity infrastructure, labour-saving technology) are therefore **development investments, not consumption spending**.

**c) Agency spectrum in unpaid work**

Drawing on the spectrum from adaptive preferences to overt resistance (Kabeer, following Kandiyoti), women in unpaid employment typically exercise **hidden forms of agency**: negotiating the timing of work, using extended kin networks to share care burdens, practising "patriarchal bargains" that secure future claims on household resources in exchange for current unpaid contributions. These forms of agency are real but constrained — they reproduce patriarchal norms rather than transforming them.

### 5. Policy Significance (1 mark)

The invisibility of unpaid employment has concrete policy consequences:

| Policy Area | Bias Created by Ignoring Unpaid Work |
|-------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Labour law** | Contributing family workers are excluded from minimum wage, working hours protections, occupational safety regulation |
| **Social protection** | No unemployment benefit, pension, or maternity leave for unpaid family workers — disproportionately women |
| **Infrastructure investment** | Underinvestment in water, electricity, transport — which would reduce women's unpaid work time |
| **Macroeconomic policy** | Prioritising export-oriented industry (RMG) over agriculture and services where women's unpaid contributions are concentrated |
| **Tax policy** | No recognition of unpaid care work in tax systems; "dependency" models penalise secondary earners |

**Estimates** suggest unpaid work constitutes 30–50% of economic activity in developing countries (Benería 1992; UNDP 1995). Ignoring it systematically biases policy toward paid, male-dominated sectors — perpetuating gender inequality in labour markets.

## Part B: Key Indicators for Gender Assessment of Bangladesh Labour Force (10 marks)

A gender assessment of Bangladesh's labour force requires examining a range of indicators that reveal the structure, quality, and equity of women's and men's labour market outcomes. The following indicators are drawn primarily from the **Labour Force Survey 2022 (BBS)** and the **Global Gender Gap Index (WEF)** .

### Indicator 1: Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) — (1.5 marks)

**LFPR (15+): Male 80.00% vs Female 42.77% (LFS 2022)**

The LFPR is the most fundamental indicator of gender disparity in the labour market. It measures the proportion of the working-age population that is either employed or actively seeking work. Bangladesh's female LFPR (42.77%) remains among the lowest in South Asia, despite having risen significantly from 36.0% in 2010 and ~33.5% in 2013.

**What it reveals:**
- The **1.87× gap** between male and female LFPR reflects structural barriers: patriarchal norms restricting women's mobility, the disproportionate burden of unpaid domestic and care work, limited safe transport, and occupational segregation.
- **Rural-urban divergence**: Female LFPR is **51.0% in rural areas** but only **23.68% in urban areas**. This counterintuitive pattern arises because rural women work extensively in agriculture (own-account and contributing family work), while urban women face mobility restrictions and limited suitable employment options beyond RMG.
- The **trend is positive** — female LFPR nearly doubled from 17.2M women in the labour force (2010) to 25.78M (2022) — but the level remains far below male participation.

### Indicator 2: Employment-to-Population Ratio — (0.5 marks)

Closely related to LFPR, the employment-to-population ratio confirms the same pattern: approximately 76.5% of working-age men are employed versus 41.2% of women (author's calculation from LFS 2022). Since Bangladesh's unemployment rate is low and similar by sex (3.50% male, 3.59% female), the employment gap is almost entirely driven by the participation gap — women who are not in the labour force are **not counted as unemployed** but as "not in labour force", which masks the true extent of labour market exclusion.

### Indicator 3: Sectoral Distribution — Agriculture Concentration — (1 mark)

**Sectoral composition of employed (LFS 2022):**

| Sector | Total (%) | Male (%) | Female (%) |
|--------|-----------|----------|------------|
| Agriculture | 45.4% | ~40% | ~54% |
| Industry | 17.0% | ~18% | ~15% |
| Service | 37.6% | ~42% | ~31% |

**What it reveals:**
- **Women are over-concentrated in agriculture** — approximately 54% of employed women work in agriculture versus ~40% of men. This is largely unpaid contributing family work on household farms.
- **Women are underrepresented in services** (31% vs 42%) — the sector that typically offers higher-productivity employment.
- **RMG concentration**: Within industry, women are overwhelmingly concentrated in the ready-made garments (RMG) sector, which employs millions of women but at low wages, with limited upward mobility and precarious working conditions.
- This sectoral divide reflects **Boserup's hypothesis** of agricultural feminisation — as men migrate to urban industry/services, women assume greater agricultural roles, often without formal recognition or independent income.

### Indicator 4: Informal Employment — (1 mark)

**84.9% of total employed are in informal employment (LFS 2022). While the male informal employment rate is approximately 82%, the female rate is closer to 90%** (author's estimate based on employment status composition).

**What it reveals:**
- Informal employment encompasses own-account workers (50.6% of employed), contributing family workers (6.8%), and informal employees without contracts or social protection.
- Women are disproportionately concentrated in the **two most vulnerable categories**: own-account workers (~55% of employed women vs ~48% of men) and contributing family workers (3.11M women vs 1.67M men).
- Informal employment means: no written contract, no paid leave, no pension, no health insurance, no trade union protection. For women, it also means no maternity protection — a fundamental gap in decent work.
- **85.1% of all employment in Bangladesh is informal** — this is not a marginal phenomenon but the dominant employment pattern. A gender assessment must recognise that **women's informalisation is deeper and more precarious** than men's.

### Indicator 5: Wage Gap — (1 mark)

**Average monthly income (wage/salary employees): Male Tk 14,180 vs Female Tk 11,093 (LFS 2022)**

This represents a raw **wage gap of approximately 22%** (women earn 78% of men's wages). The gap is likely larger for casual workers and smaller for regular salaried workers.

**What it reveals:**
- The 22% gap is an **unadjusted figure** — it does not control for education, experience, hours worked, or sector. After controlling for these factors, the "unexplained" gap (usually attributed to discrimination) in Bangladesh has been estimated at 12–18% across different studies.
- The gap reflects:
  - **Occupational segregation**: women in lower-paying occupations (agriculture, elementary work)
  - **Industry segmentation**: women concentrated in lowest-paying subsectors within industries
  - **Within-occupation discrimination**: women paid less than men in the same occupation
  - **Time poverty constraint**: women's unpaid care burden limits overtime and higher-paying but inflexible work
- The wage gap has **real welfare consequences**: women's lower earnings reduce their bargaining power within households, increase their risk of poverty in widowhood/divorce, and limit their ability to accumulate assets.

### Indicator 6: Occupational Segregation — (0.5 marks)

**Distribution by major occupation (LFS 2022):**

Women are concentrated in:
- **Skilled agricultural, forestry, and fisheries work** — the largest category
- **Elementary occupations** — low-skill, low-pay work
- **Service and sales work** — a growing category but predominantly informal

Women are underrepresented in:
- **Managers**: Female share of management roles is very low (well below 20%)
- **Professionals**: While female tertiary enrolment has surged, professional employment lags — women with degrees face barriers in law, engineering, finance, and senior academia
- **Craft and related trades**: Male-dominated skilled trades

**Women's share of high-status occupations** (Table 14.2, LFS 2022) remains low, though the exact figure depends on the occupation definition. The LFS reports female share of employment in high-status occupations by broad sector — showing consistent male dominance.

### Indicator 7: Unpaid Work Burden — (0.5 marks)

While LFS 2022 does not include a full time-use module, it provides data on hours in own-use service provision (Table 13.1.1 and 13.1.2):

The LFS reports average hours spent on **own-use service work** (unpaid domestic and care work for own household) — a critical gender indicator. This data shows:
- Women spend **significantly more hours** on own-use services than men, regardless of labour force status
- **Employed women face a "double burden"** — they work both in paid employment and in unpaid domestic/care work, reducing their total available time for education, leisure, and political participation
- **Women not in the labour force** spend the most hours on own-use services — their contribution to social reproduction is entirely invisible in standard employment statistics

International time-use data for Bangladesh (collected separately from LFS) shows women spend **3–4 times more hours on unpaid care work than men**. This is consistent with the South Asian pattern.

### Indicator 8: Educational Composition — (0.5 marks)

**Literacy rate (15+)**: Approximately 74% female vs 78% male — the gap is narrowing.

**Education of working-age population (LFS 2022):**
- **Girls' enrolment** at secondary level now exceeds boys' — a major achievement of Bangladesh's education policies (female stipend programme)
- However, **education-to-employment transition** is worse for women — educated women face higher unemployment rates than educated men
- **Vocational training**: Women are underrepresented in technical and vocational education, which limits access to higher-paying skilled trades

**Paradox**: Despite closing the education gender gap, the labour market gender gap remains large. This "education-employment disconnect" suggests that demand-side constraints (employer discrimination, lack of suitable jobs, mobility restrictions) are as important as supply-side factors (skills, education).

### Indicator 9: Youth NEET (Not in Employment, Education, or Training) — (1 mark)

**NEET youth (15–29): Male 16.2% vs Female 27.1% (LFS 2022) — total 9.65M youth**

| Category | Male | Female | Total |
|----------|------|--------|-------|
| NEET population | 3.33M | 6.32M | 9.65M |
| NEET rate (%) | 16.2% | 27.1% | 22.0% |

**What it reveals:**
- **Nearly 2× more female youth are NEET** compared to male youth (27.1% vs 16.2%)
- Female NEET is overwhelmingly driven by **early marriage, childbearing, and domestic responsibilities** — not by discouragement or lack of jobs alone
- The NEET indicator captures women who are **invisible in both standard employment and education statistics** — they are not "unemployed" (not seeking work) but they are not productively engaged either
- Female NEET is a **policy priority**: it represents a massive waste of human capital, perpetuates the cycle of female economic dependence, and has intergenerational effects (mothers' education/employment → children's outcomes)
- Regional patterns show **47.6% of NEET youth are in rural areas** and 52.4% in urban areas

### Indicator 10: Vulnerable Employment — Own-Account and Contributing Family Workers — (0.5 marks)

**Status in employment (LFS 2022):**

| Status | Total (M) | % | Male (M) | Female (M) |
|--------|-----------|---|----------|------------|
| Employee | 26.82 | 38.0% | ~20 | ~7 |
| Own-account worker | 35.66 | 50.6% | ~24 | ~12 |
| Contributing family worker | 4.78 | 6.8% | 1.67 | 3.11 |
| Employer | 2.65 | 3.8% | ~2.5 | ~0.15 |
| Other | 0.56 | 0.8% | — | — |

**What it reveals:**
- **50.6% of all employed persons are own-account workers** — the single largest category. This includes informal self-employed in agriculture, petty trade, and home-based manufacturing.
- **Vulnerable employment** (own-account + contributing family workers) accounts for **57.4% of all employment** — the majority of Bangladeshi workers lack stable, formal employment.
- Women are **overrepresented in vulnerable employment**: ~55% of employed women are own-account workers and ~12% are contributing family workers (vs ~48% and ~3.5% for men respectively).
- Vulnerable employment means: irregular income, no employer-provided benefits, no workplace safety regulation, no union representation, no job security.
- The high share of own-account workers also reflects **limited formal job creation** — Bangladesh's growth has been driven by labour-intensive manufacturing (RMG) but has not generated sufficient formal employment for the growing labour force.

### Indicator 11: Global Gender Gap Index — Bangladesh's Ranking — (0.5 marks)

**Global Gender Gap Index (WEF 2023): Bangladesh ranks #59 globally**

| Sub-index | Rank | Key observation |
|-----------|------|-----------------|
| Overall | 59/146 | Best in South Asia |
| Economic Participation & Opportunity | 139/146 | Very poor — reflects low female LFPR, wage gap, occupational segregation |
| Educational Attainment | 123/146 | Improved but still low — female literacy lagging |
| Health & Survival | 1/146 | First globally — reflects favourable sex ratio and life expectancy |
| Political Empowerment | 8/146 | Very high — driven by long-serving female Prime Minister (Hasina) and Speaker (Shirin Sharmin Chaudhury) |

**What it reveals:**
- The stark **contradiction** in Bangladesh's gender performance: **best in South Asia overall**, but **near-bottom globally on economic participation (139/146)** .
- Bangladesh's high overall rank is pulled up by **political empowerment** (female head of government, 73 women in 350-seat parliament) and **health/survival** (female life expectancy 74.5 years, balanced sex ratio at birth).
- However, **economic participation rank (139/146)** reveals that political representation has not translated into labour market equality. This is the "Kabeer paradox" — limited agency at the household/community level coexists with high-level political breakthroughs.
- The gap between political and economic participation suggests that **structural barriers in the labour market** (informality, occupational segregation, wage discrimination, unpaid work burden) are more resistant to change than political glass ceilings.

### Synthesis: What These Indicators Reveal About Gender Inequality in Bangladesh's Labour Market

Together, the 11 indicators paint a consistent picture:

**1. Participation gap**: The most fundamental constraint — fewer than half of working-age women participate in the labour force (42.77%), compared to 80% of men. This gap is rooted in:

- **Patriarchal norms** restricting women's mobility and "acceptable" work
- **Unpaid care burden** — women's time consumed by domestic responsibilities
- **Limited suitable employment options** — beyond RMG and agriculture, few sectors employ women in significant numbers
- **Education–employment disconnect** — female education has improved dramatically, but labour market returns remain low

**2. Quality gap**: Even when women work, they work in **worse jobs** — more informal, lower-paid, less secure, and without social protection:

- Women are concentrated in vulnerable employment (own-account + contributing family)
- 65% of contributing family workers are women — work without pay, recognition, or agency
- Women earn 22% less than men on average; the gap is even larger in informal work
- Women face a "double burden" of paid and unpaid work

**3. Sectoral segregation**: Women and men operate in different labour markets:

- Women in agriculture (subsistence, unpaid) and RMG (low-wage, precarious)
- Men in construction, transport, manufacturing, and formal services (higher-wage, more stable)
- This segregation limits women's access to economic growth dividends — as the economy shifts toward industry and services, women are left behind in agriculture or stuck in low-productivity informal work

**4. Intergenerational transmission**: The NEET indicator (27.1% of young women vs 16.2% of young men) reveals a **reproduction mechanism** — daughters who are "not in employment, education, or training" will likely become mothers with constrained labour market options, perpetuating the participation gap across generations.

**5. The Kabeer synthesis**: Connecting to the theoretical framework from Tutorial 2 (Q1, Q2), Bangladesh's labour market gender assessment reveals:

- **Resources** (education, microfinance, RMG employment) have expanded for women, but these resources have not translated into equal **achievements** (labour market outcomes)
- **Agency** remains constrained — women work but lack the recognition, income, and bargaining power that makes work transformative
- **Patriarchal bargains** persist — women pursue security within patriarchal constraints (accepting unpaid family work, low-wage informal work) rather than challenging them directly
- The **positive outlier paradox** (Q1) applies: Bangladesh has achieved remarkable social progress (fertility decline, education gains, health improvements) alongside persistent labour market inequality. The labour market is the **last frontier** of gender transformation.

## Professor's Corner

This question bridges **labour economics** and **feminist economics** — two subfields that development economists must integrate. Key insights for examination:

1. **"Invisibility" is the central concept for Part A** — unpaid employment is invisible in standard statistics (SNA), invisible in policy, and invisible in household bargaining. Each section of Part A should return to this theme.

2. **Part B demonstrates that "more data ≠ better outcomes"** — Bangladesh's LFS 2022 provides rich gender-disaggregated data across dozens of indicators, but the data reveals stubborn inequality. Measurement alone does not produce change.

3. **The Kabeer framework (Resources → Agency → Achievements) is the connecting thread** between Parts A and B. Unpaid employment is a case of work without agency. The gender indicators in Part B show outcomes shaped by unequal resource distribution and constrained agency.

4. **Policy implications**: Both parts point to the same policy agenda:
   - Recognise unpaid work in national accounting (time-use surveys, satellite accounts)
   - Invest in public care infrastructure (childcare, elderly care) to reduce women's unpaid burden
   - Extend labour law and social protection to informal and unpaid workers
   - Address patriarchal norms through education and community-level interventions
   - Create formal employment opportunities for women beyond RMG and agriculture

5. **Comparative dimension**: Bangladesh's female LFPR (42.77%) is higher than Pakistan (~22%) and India (~25%) but lower than Vietnam (~72%), China (~62%), and Sri Lanka (~50%). The question could be extended to ask why Bangladesh performs better than Pakistan/India but worse than Southeast Asian comparators — a likely exam extension.
