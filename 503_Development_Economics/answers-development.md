### Question 1 {#q-kabeer-01}

**How do you explain Bangladesh's status as a 'positive outlier' in cross-country comparisons of social progress compared to countries with similar income levels? (20 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [20 marks]:** Definition of positive outlier (3) | Expected vs actual outcomes table (4) | The Bangladesh paradox — Kabeer's thesis (5) | Role of women's renegotiation of patriarchy (4) | Comparative evidence: India, Pakistan (4)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Core Kabeer question. Directly tests understanding of the Bangladesh paradox and the relationship between income and social progress.

Bangladesh is termed a 'positive outlier' because it has achieved social progress far exceeding what its per capita income would predict. Cross-country regressions typically show a strong positive relationship between GDP per capita and social indicators such as fertility decline, infant mortality reduction, and female life expectancy. Bangladesh consistently falls above the regression line — meaning it outperforms peer countries at similar income levels (Kabeer 2011).

The paradox has three dimensions. First, **economic**: conventional theory holds that social progress requires wealth first, yet Bangladesh outperforms richer countries. Second, **political**: standard governance indicators suggest corruption and weak state capacity should impede development, but Bangladesh delivered pro-poor outcomes anyway. Third, **cultural**: Bangladesh is a deeply patriarchal Muslim-majority society where rising religious conservatism coexists with improving gender equality — a contradiction to modernization theory's assumption that secularization precedes women's advancement.

Kabeer's explanation centres on ordinary women **renegotiating patriarchy** through cumulative everyday acts rather than revolutionary rupture. This occurred through three channels: (1) expanded women's paid employment, especially in the RMG sector employing 4+ million workers (60% women); (2) NGO-led microfinance and health programs that increased women's resource access; (3) state investment in girls' education and family planning. These created new resources (Bourdieu's material, human, social, and symbolic capital) that women used to bargain within households.

Comparative evidence strengthens the argument. Bangladesh's fertility rate fell to 2.2 by 2011, infant mortality to 37 per 1000, and female life expectancy rose to 70 years — all significantly better than India (2.6, 47, 67) and Pakistan (3.4, 59, 66), despite similar or lower income levels. Bangladesh's sex ratio at birth is a normal 105:100, while India's stands at an abnormal 111:100 reflecting persistent son preference. Drèze and Sen (1995) showed that districts with higher female economic activity combined with literacy had lower fertility and lower female disadvantage in child survival.

**Exam Tip:** Always contrast Bangladesh against India and Pakistan in a table. The key insight is that change occurred within patriarchy, not against it — this distinguishes Kabeer from both modernization theory (which expects linear progress) and dependency theory (which discounts agency).

> **Professor's Corner:** The highest marks go to answers that engage with the agency-structure debate. Kabeer uses Giddens' structuration theory and Bourdieu's practice theory to argue that structure constrains but does not determine outcomes — women are "knowledgeable agents" who navigate constraints. This theoretical sophistication separates A-grade answers from descriptive ones.

### Question 2 {#q-kabeer-02}

**What is Classical Patriarchy? What are the different forms of women's agency? Explain the evolution of women's agency in Bangladesh's development trajectory. (20 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [20 marks]:** Definition of classical patriarchy — Kandiyoti (5) | Forms of agency — adaptive preferences to overt resistance (5) | Evolution in Bangladesh — resources, bargaining, incremental change (7) | Theoretical references (3)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Central Kabeer concept. Patriarchy + agency = the core analytical framework.

**(i) Classical Patriarchy**

Classical patriarchy, as theorised by Deniz Kandiyoti (1988) and Mead Cain (1984), describes a system of male dominance found in a belt extending from North Africa through the Middle East to Northern South Asia, including Bangladesh. It is characterised by four structural features: **patrilineal** inheritance (property passes through the male line), **patrilocal** residence (wives move to the husband's family home), **patriarchal** authority (men hold decision-making power), and **endogamy** (marriage within the same group, reinforcing kin control over women).

Dyson and Moore (1983) distinguished the North Indian kinship system (covering Bangladesh) from the South Indian system. In the North, women's mobility is restricted, fertility is higher, and sex ratios are more skewed (106–114 males per 100 females versus 97–107 in the South). The consequences include Amartya Sen's "missing women" — over 100 million women globally absent due to systematic discrimination — strong son preference driving high fertility, dowry practices, early marriage, and restricted mobility outside the home.

**(ii) Forms of Women's Agency**

Agency is defined as the "capacity to define goals and act upon them within social constraints" (Giddens 1979). Kabeer identifies a spectrum of agency forms:

| Form | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Adaptive preferences** | Adjusting desires to what seems possible within constraints (Sen, Nussbaum) | Women saying they "don't want" education because it's unavailable |
| **Hidden transcripts** | Private expressions of resistance not visible to power holders (Scott 1985) | Women's songs, gossip, informal networks |
| **Bargaining** | Using resources to negotiate better terms within relationships | Earning income → more say in household decisions |
| **Overt resistance** | Public, organised challenge to patriarchal norms | Women's movements, collective action |

Kandiyoti's concept of **patriarchal bargains** is key: women pursue security and advantage within patriarchy rather than openly challenging it. Producing sons, for example, is a strategy for status and old-age security, not simply passive compliance. This explains why revolutionary change is rare but incremental renegotiation is constant.

**(iii) Evolution of Women's Agency in Bangladesh**

Bangladesh's development trajectory shows an evolution in women's agency without revolutionary transformation. Kabeer identifies four phases:

**Phase 1 (pre-1980s):** Women's agency exercised almost entirely within the family — through patriarchal bargains of son preference, fertility decisions, and household management. Adaptive preferences dominated. The state was indifferent to women's issues.

**Phase 2 (1980s–1990s):** Three forces created new resources: (a) the RMG export boom offered paid employment to millions of women, (b) NGO expansion (BRAC, Grameen Bank) provided microfinance and health services targeting women, (c) state investment in girls' education raised female literacy. These resources shifted women's bargaining power within households (Sen's household bargaining pathway).

**Phase 3 (2000s):** Women translated economic contribution into social achievements. The fertility rate halved from 4.5 (1980) to 2.2 (2011). Son preference declined sharply — most families now prefer balanced sex composition. Female life expectancy surpassed male. These outcomes reflect cumulative changes in household decision-making.

**Phase 4 (current):** Change remains incomplete. Female LFPR (42.77%) is still half the male rate (80%). Women are concentrated in low-quality informal employment (50.6% own-account workers). Yet the trajectory is distinct: change occurred through millions of small renegotiations, not legal or political revolution. As Kabeer puts it: "Bangladesh's social progress was achieved not despite patriarchy, but through its gradual renegotiation."

**Exam Tip:** Use the terms "patriarchal bargains", "adaptive preferences", and "incremental renegotiation" to show theoretical depth. The spectrum of agency is a framework that can be applied to any country context.

> **Professor's Corner:** A distinction-level answer notes the tension between Sen's capabilities approach (which emphasizes expanding what people can do and be) and Bourdieu's habitus (which emphasizes how social structures become internalised). Kabeer synthesises both: women need resources to exercise agency, but their goals are shaped by the patriarchal context they inhabit. Change requires both new resources AND shifts in how women perceive their own possibilities.

### Question 3 {#q-lfs-01}

**What is meant by 'Persons in Employment' and 'Labour Underutilization'? Are persons in own-use production work considered 'employed'? What is the proportion of unemployed youth in total unemployment and the NEET situation? (20 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [20 marks]:** UN definitions — employed and labour underutilization (6) | Own-use production work classification — LU4 vs employment (5) | Youth unemployment proportion + NEET analysis (6) | Bangladesh data application (3)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Foundational LFS definitions. Essential context for all Bangladesh employment questions.

**(A) Persons in Employment and Labour Underutilization**

The 19th International Conference of Labour Statisticians (ICLS, 2013) defines **persons in employment** as those of working age who, during a short reference period (past week), performed any work for pay or profit for at least one hour. This includes: (a) employed persons (employees, employers, own-account workers), (b) contributing family workers, and (c) persons temporarily absent from work. The "one-hour criterion" is the international standard.

**Labour underutilization** is a broader concept capturing four categories beyond open unemployment:

| Measure | Code | Definition |
|---------|------|------------|
| **Time-related underemployment** | LU1 | Employed persons working fewer hours than desired (below 35 hrs/week) — Bangladesh: 4.01% (2.82 million) |
| **Unemployment** | LU2 | Without work, available for work, and seeking work — Bangladesh: 3.53% (2.58 million) |
| **Potential labour force** | LU3 | Persons not in employment who are available but not seeking, OR seeking but not available |
| **Composite underutilization** | LU4 | LU1 + LU2 + LU3 — the broadest measure of labour slack |

LU4 is the most comprehensive measure because developing countries have low open unemployment but high hidden underutilization. Bangladesh's 3.53% unemployment rate is misleading — most workers cannot afford to be openly unemployed; they accept any work available.

**(B) Own-Use Production Work: Employed or Not?**

Under the 19th ICLS resolution, persons engaged exclusively in **own-use production work** (subsistence farming, unpaid domestic work, caring for household members) are **NOT classified as employed**. They fall outside the labour force entirely.

The rationale is that employment requires work for **pay or profit** — the transaction must have an economic dimension beyond household consumption. However, the boundary is contested. The **13th ICLS (1982)** had already included subsistence agriculture as employment. The 19th ICLS clarified: if any part of the agricultural output is sold, the person is employed. If all output is for own consumption and no processing for market occurs, the person is in own-use production work — not employed.

This distinction matters enormously for developing countries. In Bangladesh, 45.4% of employment is in agriculture (LFS 2022), but many women engaged in post-harvest processing, kitchen gardening, and livestock rearing for household consumption would be classified as **outside the labour force** under strict ICLS criteria, underestimating their economic contribution.

**(C) Youth Unemployment and NEET**

Youth (aged 15–29) constitute a disproportionate share of total unemployment globally and in Bangladesh. The ILO estimates that youth are two to three times more likely to be unemployed than adults. In Bangladesh (LFS 2022):

| Indicator | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Youth labour force | 26.82 million |
| Youth share of total unemployment | Higher than proportional (15–29 is ~25% of working age but accounts for >50% of unemployed) |
| NEET rate (female) | Substantially higher than male |
| Educated youth unemployment | Rising — mismatch between educational qualifications and labour market demand |

The **NEET** indicator (Not in Education, Employment, or Training) captures youth who are disconnected from both learning and earning. In Bangladesh, NEET rates are higher among females due to early marriage, domestic responsibilities, and social norms restricting mobility. NEET is a policy priority because detached youth are at risk of long-term labour market exclusion, skill erosion, and social marginalisation.

**Exam Tip:** Always pair the low unemployment rate (3.53%) with LU4 to show sophistication. A key exam point: in developing countries, the unemployment rate is a poor measure of labour market health — underemployment and informality matter more.

> **Professor's Corner:** The ICLS definitional changes are themselves a subject of scholarly debate. The 19th ICLS shift aimed to align with SNA (System of National Accounts) boundaries, but feminist economists (Beneria, Floro) critiqued it for rendering women's unpaid work invisible. The "one-hour criterion" captures the extensive margin of employment but misses quality dimensions — a worker employed one hour per week is as "employed" as one working 60 hours. Always note these limitations in your answer.
>

### Question 4 {#q-venezuela-01}

**From Venezuela's experience as an 'Oil Rich Country', explain their investment and governance structure. How does it explain their current economic and social crisis? (20 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [20 marks]:** Oil dependency structure — 95% export concentration (4) | Investment patterns — Dutch disease, lack of diversification (5) | Governance failures — institutional erosion, central bank capture (5) | Crisis dynamics — price shock → hyperinflation → humanitarian collapse (4) | Lesson for Bangladesh (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Classic resource curse case study. Frequently compared with Iran and Bangladesh export dependency.

Venezuela's collapse is not solely a story of economic mismanagement — it is a structural lesson on the dangers of export concentration and weak governance. Once the richest country in South America, Venezuela now faces hyperinflation (peaking above 1,000,000%), a 60%+ contraction in GDP, and the displacement of 6+ million refugees.

**Investment and Governance Structure**

Venezuela's economy was built around oil, which accounted for approximately 95% of export earnings. This created a **rentier state**: the government derived most revenue from external resource rents rather than domestic taxation, weakening the fiscal contract between state and citizens.

| Structural Feature | Mechanism | Consequence |
|--------------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Oil dependency** | ~95% of exports; no export diversification | Entire economy rises and falls with oil price |
| **Dutch disease** | Oil boom → currency appreciation → non-oil tradable sectors become uncompetitive | Domestic manufacturing and agriculture collapsed |
| **State-led investment** | Oil revenues funded social programmes and subsidies, not productive diversification | No buffer sectors when oil prices fell |
| **Central bank capture** | Government pressured the central bank to finance deficits via money creation | Hyperinflation destroyed savings and wages |
| **Expropriation** | Nationalisation of private enterprises (2000s) | Investment collapsed, oil production fell |
| **Weak rule of law** | No protection for property rights or contracts | Private investment dried up |

**How the Crisis Unfolded**

The 2014 oil price crash exposed the fragility of Venezuela's economic model. Oil revenues collapsed, leaving the government unable to finance imports of food and medicine. Rather than adjusting fiscal policy, the government printed money, triggering hyperinflation. Oil production itself collapsed from 3.2 million barrels per day (bpd) to under 500,000 bpd due to lack of investment and maintenance.

| Stage | Dynamic |
|-------|---------|
| **Pre-2014** | High oil prices funded consumption, not investment. Economy grew but no structural transformation |
| **2014 crash** | Oil price fell from ~$100 to ~$30/barrel. Export earnings collapsed. Current account deficit exploded |
| **2015–2018** | Money printing to cover deficits. Hyperinflation began. GDP contracted |
| **2019–present** | Production collapsed. Humanitarian crisis. Mass migration. GDP fell >60% from peak |

**Comparison to Bangladesh**

| Vulnerability | Venezuela | Bangladesh |
|---------------|-----------|------------|
| Export concentration | Oil = 95% | RMG = 86% |
| Governance risk | Captured institutions | Weak institutional capacity |
| Diversification | None | Limited |
| Lesson | Diversify exports | Same imperative |

Venezuela demonstrates that **export concentration amplifies vulnerability to external shocks**, and that **weak governance turns a manageable shock into a catastrophic collapse**. Bangladesh's RMG sector constitutes 86% of exports — a similar degree of concentration. Combined with governance challenges, a shock to the RMG sector (e.g., LDC graduation in 2026 with loss of GSP preferences, automation in global textile production) could trigger a comparable, if less extreme, adjustment.

**Exam Tip:** Structure your answer as: (1) oil dependency → (2) governance failures → (3) price shock trigger → (4) hyperinflation dynamics → (5) humanitarian outcome → (6) lesson for Bangladesh. Use the Venezuela-Bangladesh comparison as a conclusion.

> **Professor's Corner:** The theoretical lens here is the **resource curse** literature (Auty 1993, Sachs & Warner 1995). Venezuela is a near-perfect case: high resource dependence, weak institutions, and poor governance — the combination that most consistently produces negative development outcomes. Countries with strong institutions and resource wealth (Norway, Botswana) avoided the curse, proving the problem is not oil per se but the interaction of resource dependence with institutional quality.

### Question 5 {#q-trade-01}

**If you were to present Bangladesh's macro-economic scenario to a foreign audience, which indicators would you use and why? How would you present a brief macro-economic overview? (5+15 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [20 marks]:** Selection of indicators with justification (5) | GDP growth narrative (3) | Employment structure (3) | Trade dynamics (3) | Social indicators (3) | Fiscal and monetary situation (3)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Integrates LFS, trade, and macro data into a coherent country overview.

**(A) Selection of Indicators**

For a foreign audience, indicators must balance: (i) comparability across countries, (ii) relevance to Bangladesh's development stage, and (iii) coverage of both economic performance and social outcomes. The six indicator clusters:

| Cluster | Indicators | Why |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| **Growth** | GDP growth rate, GDP per capita, sectoral composition | Shows overall economic trajectory and structural change |
| **Employment** | LFPR (61.2%), unemployment (3.53%), sectoral shares (agriculture 45.4%, industry 17%, services 37.6%) | Labour markets are the transmission mechanism from growth to welfare |
| **Trade** | Trade-to-GDP (33.8%), RMG share of exports (86%), tariff rate (27%) | Export concentration is the key vulnerability |
| **Social** | Fertility (2.2), infant mortality (37/1000), female life expectancy (70 yrs) | Bangladesh's outlier performance — core development narrative |
| **Fiscal** | Revenue-to-GDP ratio (among world's lowest at ~8-9%), fiscal deficit, public debt | Fiscal constraints limit policy space |
| **Monetary** | Inflation rate, remittance inflows (~$20B/year), foreign exchange reserves | External sector stability |

**(B) Macro-Economic Overview**

**Growth story:** Bangladesh has sustained GDP growth above 6% for over a decade, reaching 7.9% pre-COVID and 6-7% post-pandemic recovery. This growth has been driven primarily by domestic demand (private consumption ~70% of GDP) and the RMG export sector. Per capita income crossed $2,800 in 2023, qualifying Bangladesh for LDC graduation in 2026.

**Structural transformation is incomplete.** Agriculture still employs 45.4% of the labour force but contributes only ~12% of GDP, indicating low productivity and surplus labour (Lewis model dynamics). Industry employs 17% and services 37.6%. The LFPR is 61.2% overall but highly gendered: male (80%) versus female (42.77%). The unemployment rate (3.53%) understates labour underutilisation — 50.6% of workers are own-account (self-employed), reflecting limited formal sector absorption.

**External sector vulnerability** is the dominant concern. Bangladesh's trade-to-GDP ratio declined from 48.1% (2012) to 33.8% (2022) — a declining trade orientation that contrasts sharply with Vietnam (>150%). Export concentration in RMG reached 86% of total exports. The average tariff rate of 27% (compared to 6% global average) creates anti-export bias, making domestic sales more profitable than exporting.

**Social achievements** are the country's most distinctive story. Despite modest income, Bangladesh outperforms peers on fertility (2.2), infant mortality (37/1000), and female life expectancy (70 years). This is the "Bangladesh paradox" — social progress exceeding income-predicted levels, driven by women's paid employment, NGO programmes, and public investment in girls' education and family planning.

**Fiscal constraints** are severe. Bangladesh's revenue-to-GDP ratio (~8-9%) is one of the lowest in the world, limiting government capacity to invest in infrastructure, health, and education. The tax base is narrow, with most workers in the informal sector outside the income tax net. LDC graduation will further pressure fiscal space by ending concessional financing and trade preferences.

**Exam Tip:** The (5+15) mark split means you need a brief list of indicators (the "which and why" part) followed by a full paragraph essay weaving them together. Use the six clusters as a framework, then tell the story: high growth + incomplete transformation + export vulnerability + social paradox + fiscal constraint.

> **Professor's Corner:** For a foreign audience, contextualise Bangladesh relative to regional peers. Key comparisons: (1) Vietnam — superior trade orientation and diversification; (2) India — similar demographic dividend but different governance; (3) Pakistan — Bangladesh outperforms on all social indicators despite lower income in the 1970s. The last comparison is particularly striking for an international audience.

### Question 6 {#q-trade-02}

**Briefly unveil the key challenges of external trade in Bangladesh amidst the current dynamic landscape. (15 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [15 marks]:** Declining trade orientation (3) | Export concentration — RMG dominance (3) | Capital flight via trade mis-invoicing (3) | Export data mismatch (2) | High tariff regime and anti-export bias (2) | Limited FTA participation (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Seven structural concerns summarised. Links directly to Washington Consensus and RMG debates in Political Economy.

Bangladesh's external trade faces seven interconnected structural challenges:

**1. Declining trade orientation.** Bangladesh's trade-to-GDP ratio fell from 48.1% (2012) to 33.8% (2022), while Vietnam's exceeds 150%. This makes Bangladesh one of the least trade-open economies in South Asia. The decline signals eroding international competitiveness and insufficient integration into global value chains.

**2. Extreme export concentration in RMG.** The RMG sector accounts for 86% of total exports (2023), up from 77% in the 2010s. Bangladesh's export concentration index is more than double that of India, Indonesia, and Vietnam. Leather, jute, and other sectors remain marginal. If RMG faces a shock — from LDC graduation, automation, or buyer consolidation — the entire export sector has no buffer.

**3. Capital flight through trade mis-invoicing.** Bangladesh loses approximately $8.27 billion annually (2009–2018 average) through over-invoicing of imports and under-invoicing of exports. The IMF noted financial account outflows of 0.5% of GDP in FY2022–23. This represents both governance failure and a drain on foreign exchange reserves.

**4. Export data mismatch.** A widening gap between EPB (Export Promotion Bureau) and Bangladesh Bank export data — reaching $13.8 billion in the first ten months of FY2023–24 (EPB: $47.47B; BB: $33.67B). Such discrepancies undermine investor confidence and policy planning.

**5. RMG sector sustainability.** The sector employs 4+ million workers (60% women) but faces: (a) declining value addition — raw material import costs rose to 83% of export value (FY22); (b) the need to shift from cotton to man-made fibre (MMF); (c) LDC graduation in 2026 entailing loss of GSP preferences; (d) potential buyer consolidation and global recession risks.

**6. High tariff regime creating anti-export bias.** Bangladesh's average tariff of 27% (versus 9.79% for low-income countries and 6% globally) makes imported inputs expensive, raising production costs for exporters and encouraging firms to sell domestically rather than export. The tariff structure penalises exporters twice — they pay high prices for imported inputs and compete internationally against countries with lower tariffs.

**7. Limited FTA participation.** Bangladesh has only one bilateral FTA — with Bhutan (2020), covering narrow scope. SAFTA remains constrained by non-tariff barriers. BIMSTEC FTA is delayed. Vietnam, by contrast, has CPTPP, the EU-Vietnam FTA, and multiple bilateral agreements, giving its exporters preferential market access that Bangladesh lacks.

| Challenge | Risk Level | Policy Priority |
|-----------|------------|-----------------|
| Export concentration (RMG 86%) | Critical | Diversify into MMF, electronics, pharma |
| Declining trade openness | High | Target trade-to-GDP >50% by 2030 |
| Capital flight ($8.27B/yr) | High | Digital trade tracking, customs reform |
| High tariffs (27%) | Medium-High | Strategic liberalisation of intermediate inputs |
| Limited FTAs | Medium | Join RCEP, expedite BIMSTEC |

**Exam Tip:** Organise your answer around the "seven concerns" framework from the Bangladesh Trade notes. Link to Political Economy: (a) high tariffs connect to Washington Consensus trade liberalisation debates, (b) RMG concentration connects to the RMG Sector notes on value addition erosion.

> **Professor's Corner:** The trade challenges reflect a deeper structural problem — Bangladesh's growth model is approaching its limits. The domestic-demand-led model (70% consumption) combined with RMG-led exports has succeeded for three decades, but LDC graduation, global technological change, and rising competition require a new model. The policy agenda — tariff reform, FTA expansion, export diversification — constitutes an implicit shift toward the Washington Consensus prescriptions that Bangladesh largely avoided during its "outlier" growth phase.
>

### Question 7 {#q-iran-01}

**How did Iran strategize its oil dependency and use of energy resources over decades amidst global challenges? How did the crises demonstrate pressure on economic and social outcomes? (10+10 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [20 marks]:** Iran's oil dependency strategy — historic overview (4) | Energy use patterns — subsidies and inefficiency (3) | Impact of sanctions (4) | Crisis transmission mechanisms — inflation, unemployment, social welfare (5) | Comparison to Venezuela (4)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Parallel case study to Venezuela. Tests understanding of oil dependency dynamics under distinct political conditions.

Iran's oil dependency strategy shares similarities with Venezuela but diverges in critical ways shaped by geopolitical isolation, sanctions, and domestic political structure.

**(A) Iran's Oil Dependency Strategy**

Iran has relied on oil exports as the primary source of foreign exchange and government revenue since the 1950s. The strategy evolved through distinct phases:

| Period | Strategy | Key Features |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| **1950s–1970s** | Oil-led modernisation | Shah's White Revolution; oil revenues funded industrialisation and military build-up |
| **1979–1988** | Revolution and war | Iran-Iraq war destroyed infrastructure; oil revenues diverted to war effort |
| **1990s–2000s** | Reconstruction and subsidies | Heavy energy subsidies; inefficient consumption; limited diversification |
| **2010s–present** | Sanctions and resistance | Nuclear sanctions (2012, 2018) severely restricted oil exports; "resistance economy" discourse |

Iran's distinctive strategy was **massive energy subsidisation** as a social contract. Energy prices in Iran have been among the world's lowest, creating: (a) extreme energy intensity (energy use per unit of GDP is 3-4 times the global average), (b) smuggling of subsidised fuel to neighbouring countries, (c) environmental degradation, and (d) enormous fiscal burden (subsidies consumed 15-20% of GDP at peak). This subsidy regime functioned as a form of social welfare — compensating for unemployment and low wages — but created structural inefficiency and made the economy deeply vulnerable to oil revenue shocks.

**(B) Crisis Transmission Mechanisms**

Economic and social pressure has been transmitted through three primary channels:

| Channel | Mechanism | Outcome |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Sanctions on oil exports** | Oil exports fell from 2.5M bpd (2011) to ~0.5M bpd (2020) | Government revenue collapsed; budget deficit ballooned |
| **Currency crisis** | Sanctions blocked access to SWIFT and foreign exchange | Rial depreciated >90% (2017–2021); imported inflation |
| **Inflation** | Money printing to cover deficits | Inflation exceeded 40% annually; savings destroyed |

**Economic outcomes:** GDP contracted by 6% in 2018 and 7% in 2019 after the US withdrawal from the JCPOA and re-imposition of sanctions. Unemployment rose above 15% (officially) and was substantially higher among youth (15–29). The informal economy expanded to absorb workers. Poverty rates increased from ~20% pre-sanctions to over 30%.

**Social outcomes:** The collapse of the rial dramatically reduced household purchasing power for imported goods, including food and medicine. Social indicators that had been improving — infant mortality, maternal mortality, life expectancy — stagnated or reversed in some regions. The 2019 subsidy price hike protests (triggered by a 200% increase in gasoline prices) represented the largest civil unrest since the 1979 revolution, indicating the breakdown of the oil-funded social contract.

**(C) Comparison with Venezuela**

| Dimension | Venezuela | Iran |
|-----------|-----------|------|
| Oil export share | ~95% | ~60-80% (pre-sanctions) |
| Crisis trigger | Oil price crash (2014) | Sanctions (2012, 2018) |
| Inflation | Hyperinflation (>1,000,000%) | High but controlled (40-50%) |
| Regime response | Printing money, expropriation | Subsidy reforms, managed currency |
| Migration | 6M+ refugees | "Brain drain" of educated youth |

Iran avoided Venezuela's total collapse because: (1) it maintained some economic diversification (agriculture, petrochemicals, auto manufacturing), (2) the regime retained coercive capacity, and (3) sanctions forced adaptation — import substitution, domestic production of previously imported goods. However, the long-term costs of this adaptive model include: low productivity growth, capital stock depletion, and persistent inflation.

**Exam Tip:** The comparison with Venezuela is essential — exam questions often explicitly ask for it. Structure as: both are oil-dependent rentier states, but different crisis triggers (price shock vs sanctions) produced different collapse trajectories. Use the subsidy regime as the key explanatory variable for Iran's specific vulnerability.

> **Professor's Corner:** Iran illustrates the **political economy of sanctions** — external pressure can force adaptation but at substantial welfare cost. The "resistance economy" (Eqtesad-e Moghavemati) promoted by the Supreme Leader since 2014 is an attempt at forced import-substituting industrialisation, echoing the Latin American ISI strategies of the 1950s-60s. Economic theory suggests such strategies are inefficient in the long run, but Iran had no alternative given sanctions. This demonstrates how geopolitical factors can override standard development policy prescriptions.

### Question 8 {#q-batch47-dev-01}

**How does the Neocolonial Dependence Model explain the existence and continuance of underdevelopment? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Core thesis — centre-periphery structure (2) | Mechanisms: surplus extraction, unequal exchange (2) | Key theorists: Frank, Sunkel, Dos Santos (1) | Historical roots — colonialism (1) | Contemporary neocolonial channels: IFIs, MNCs, technology dependency (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Core dependency theory question. Links to Political Economy dependency theory notes.

The Neocolonial Dependence Model, rooted in the broader dependency theory tradition (Andre Gunder Frank, Osvaldo Sunkel, Theotonio Dos Santos), argues that underdevelopment is not an original condition nor a stage all countries pass through, but is actively **produced and maintained** by the structure of the global capitalist system.

**Core thesis:** The world economy is divided into a **centre** (core capitalist countries — US, Western Europe, Japan) and a **periphery** (developing countries). The centre develops through exploiting the periphery. Underdevelopment in the periphery is not a lag or a temporary phase — it is the necessary counterpart of development in the centre. Frank's formulation "the development of underdevelopment" captures this: the same historical process that made the core rich made the periphery poor.

**Mechanisms of surplus extraction:**

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Unequal exchange** | Periphery exports primary commodities at declining terms of trade (Prebisch-Singer thesis) while importing expensive manufactures |
| **Profit repatriation** | Multinational corporations extract profits from periphery to home countries |
| **Technology dependency** | Periphery relies on core for technology, paying royalties and accepting obsolete equipment |
| **Debt and IFI conditionality** | IMF/World Bank loans perpetuate dependence through structural adjustment programmes |
| **Comprador class** | A local elite (comprador group) benefits from and perpetuates dependent relationships |

**Historical roots:** Colonialism created the initial structure — extractive institutions, commodity-exporting economies, and administrative systems serving colonial interests. Independence transferred political sovereignty but not economic autonomy. Neocolonial control operates through: (a) multinational corporations controlling strategic sectors, (b) international financial institutions imposing policy conditionality, (c) technology patents and intellectual property regimes, (d) unequal voting power in the IMF and World Bank.

**Continuance of underdevelopment:** The model persists because it serves the interests of both core countries and the comprador elite in the periphery. Any attempt at autonomous development (nationalisation, industrial policy, trade protection) faces resistance from core states, IFI conditionality, capital flight, or sabotage by domestic elites whose interests align with foreign capital.

**Exam Tip:** Keep answers tight for 7 marks — thesis, three mechanisms, one example. The key theoretical move is distinguishing underdevelopment (actively produced) from non-development (original condition).

> **Professor's Corner:** The neocolonial model has been critiqued for being overly deterministic — it leaves little room for successful catch-up (South Korea, Singapore, now Vietnam). However, its defenders argue these countries achieved growth by defying neocolonial prescriptions (strategic state intervention, controlled capital accounts, infant industry protection) rather than following them. This debate — external constraints versus internal agency — is the central theoretical tension in development economics.
>

### Question 9 {#q-batch47-dev-02}

**Contrary to the claims of dependence theorists, how does the neoclassical counterrevolution argue about underdevelopment? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Core argument — internal causes, not external (2) | Price distortions and government failure (2) | Key proponents: Bauer, Little, Krueger, Bhagwati (1) | Recommended solutions: stabilisation, liberalisation, privatisation (1) | Evidence claimed (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Direct contrast to Q8. Tests understanding of the major development debate.

The neoclassical counterrevolution (also called the neoliberal or market-oriented approach) directly rejects dependency theory's claim that external exploitation causes underdevelopment. Instead, it argues that underdevelopment is caused by **internal factors** — specifically, bad domestic policies, excessive state intervention, and price distortions.

**Core argument:** Developing countries are poor because their governments have suppressed market forces. The solution is to "get prices right" — remove price controls, trade barriers, and subsidies so that markets can allocate resources efficiently. Proponents include Peter Bauer (pioneering critique of foreign aid), Ian Little (trade policy), Anne Krueger (rent-seeking), and Jagdish Bhagwati (trade liberalisation).

**Five key claims:**

| Claim | What Dependency Theorists Say | Neoclassical Counter |
|-------|------------------------------|---------------------|
| **Cause of poverty** | External exploitation | Internal policy failures |
| **Role of markets** | Markets reproduce inequality | Markets drive efficient allocation |
| **Role of state** | State needed to counter external forces | State intervention creates distortions |
| **Trade** | Unequal exchange harms periphery | Comparative advantage benefits all |
| **Solution** | Structural transformation, protection | Stabilisation, liberalisation, privatisation |

**Price distortions:** The counterrevolution identified three key price distortions in developing countries: (1) overvalued exchange rates penalising exports and encouraging imports, (2) artificially low interest rates discouraging savings and encouraging capital-intensive projects, (3) suppressed agricultural prices (taxation of farmers) discouraging rural production. These distortions, not global exploitation, explained poor growth.

**Government failure:** Even where markets fail, the argument runs, government intervention is worse because of rent-seeking, corruption, information problems, and political motivations. Krueger (1974) showed how import restrictions create rents that generate costly competition for licenses, wasting resources.

**Recommended policy package:** The counterrevolution prescribed (a) **stabilisation** — fiscal discipline, monetary tightening, (b) **liberalisation** — trade liberalisation, financial deregulation, (c) **privatisation** of state-owned enterprises. This became the Washington Consensus, imposed through IMF/World Bank conditionality.

**Exam Tip:** The 12-mark version of this question requires naming specific theorists and policies. The 7-mark version needs the contrast with dependency theory made explicit. Use the table above as your structure.

> **Professor's Corner:** The neoclassical counterrevolution won the policy battle (structural adjustment was implemented across the developing world in the 1980s-90s) but lost the empirical war in many observers' assessment. Countries that partially adopted reforms (China, Vietnam, India post-1991) grew faster than those that fully adopted them (Latin American SAP recipients). This "East Asian miracle" paradox — state-interventionist economies outperforming liberalised ones — forced the counterrevolution to acknowledge the importance of institutions, giving rise to the "augmented Washington Consensus" (Stiglitz, Rodrik).
>

### Question 10 {#q-batch47-dev-03}

**What are the key arguments embraced by the Dualistic Development Thesis? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Definition of dualism (1) | Four key elements: coexistence, supremacy, limited interaction, lack of diffusion (3) | Lewis model connection (1) | Critiques (1) | Bangladesh application (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Classic structuralist concept. Connects to Lewis model in Macroeconomics.

The Dualistic Development Thesis describes a structural condition common in developing countries: the coexistence of two distinct economic sectors operating under fundamentally different rules.

**Four key arguments** (Higgins 1968, Lewis 1954, Boeke 1953):

| Argument | Explanation |
|----------|-------------|
| **1. Coexistence of two sectors** | A **modern** (capital-intensive, urban, industrial) sector and a **traditional** (labour-intensive, rural, subsistence) sector exist side by side within the same economy |
| **2. Central supremacy** | The modern sector dominates in terms of dynamism, productivity, and policy attention; it receives the bulk of investment, credit, and government support |
| **3. Limited interaction** | The two sectors operate largely independently. Labour, capital, and technology do not flow freely between them. The modern sector may even suppress the traditional sector (competition for land, credit diversion) |
| **4. Lack of diffusion** | Growth in the modern sector does not automatically spread (diffuse) to the traditional sector. This contradicts neoclassical expectation that factor prices equalise across sectors over time |

**The Lewis model** (1954) formalises dualism: the traditional sector has surplus labour (marginal product near zero) that can be transferred to the modern sector at a constant wage. This structural transformation — moving labour from low-productivity traditional to high-productivity modern employment — is the core development process.

**Empirical relevance for Bangladesh:** Agriculture employs 45.4% of the labour force but produces only ~12% of GDP — a classic dualistic productivity gap. The 50.6% of workers classified as own-account (self-employed) operate in the traditional sector with limited formal sector linkages. The RMG sector (modern sector) employs 4+ million but has limited backward linkages to agriculture.

**Critiques:** (1) Dualism is too binary — reality is a continuum (modern, semi-modern, traditional). (2) The traditional sector is not static; it adapts and innovates. (3) The model assumes unlimited labour supply, but seasonality and living wage constraints create effective labour shortages. (4) Dualism can underestimate the interdependence of sectors — agriculture feeds industry, rural savings finance urban investment.

**Exam Tip:** For 7 marks, focus on the four arguments. Add the Lewis model reference for depth. Use the Bangladesh agriculture productivity gap as a concrete example.

> **Professor's Corner:** Arthur Lewis won the Nobel Prize for his dual-sector model, but the concept predates him. Boeke (1953) studied "dual economies" in Indonesia — a notion rooted in colonial administration. The key theoretical contribution of dualism is that it challenges the neoclassical assumption of unified markets — developing economies are structurally segmented, and policies must account for this segmentation. This insight underpins structuralist development economics.
>

### Question 11 {#q-batch47-dev-04}

**How does the False Paradigm Model of International Dependence argue against structural models of economic development? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Definition of the False Paradigm Model (2) | Mechanism — Western-trained economists applying inappropriate models (2) | Specific critique of structural models (2) | Connections to dependency theory (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Third major development theory. Often compared with neocolonial dependence and dualistic development.

The False Paradigm Model, associated with Michael Todaro and the broader international dependence school, argues that underdevelopment is perpetuated by the application of **inappropriate Western theoretical models** to developing-country contexts.

**Core argument:** Economists trained in Western universities (and local elites educated abroad) bring with them theories, assumptions, and policy frameworks developed for industrialised economies. These models — neoclassical price theory, Keynesian demand management, linear stages of growth — incorporate assumptions that do not hold in developing countries: full employment, perfect information, competitive markets, elastic supply responses, and established institutional frameworks.

**Specific critiques of structural models:**

| Structural Model | False Paradigm Critique |
|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Rostow's stages** | Assumes all countries follow the same linear path as Europe; ignores colonial extraction that made the European path possible |
| **Lewis dual sector** | Assumes unlimited labour supply and constant urban wages; ignores institutional barriers (union power, minimum wages, housing constraints) |
| **Harrod-Domar growth** | Treats capital accumulation as sufficient for growth; ignores absorptive capacity, skills, and institutional constraints |
| **Import Substitution Industrialisation** | Based on Latin American experience; inappropriate for small economies lacking internal markets |
| **Neoclassical price liberalisation** | Assumes competitive markets exist or will emerge; ignores monopoly power, information asymmetries, and missing markets in developing economies |

**Consequences of false paradigms:** (1) Policy advice based on faulty assumptions leads to failed interventions. (2) Scarce resources are wasted on inappropriate projects. (3) Local knowledge and indigenous institutions are ignored or destroyed. (4) Developing countries remain dependent on external expertise, perpetuating neocolonial relationships.

**The solution,** according to the False Paradigm Model, is to: (a) ground development theory in the specific historical and institutional context of each country, (b) listen to local voices and grassroots knowledge, (c) reject universal models in favour of context-specific analysis, (d) train economists within developing countries rather than exporting Western education.

**Exam Tip:** The key distinction from the Neocolonial Dependence Model (Q8) is that the False Paradigm Model focuses on **ideas and knowledge** rather than material exploitation. Both argue external forces cause underdevelopment, but one emphasises economic structure and the other intellectual frameworks.

> **Professor's Corner:** The False Paradigm Model resonates with contemporary debates about "decolonising development economics" and the "randomista" controversy (whether RCTs or structural approaches better inform development policy). The critique is not that Western economics has nothing to offer, but that mechanical application of context-free models does violence to developing-country realities. The solution is not to abandon economic theory but to adapt it — the very approach that distinguished the "structuralist" development economists (Hirschman, Myrdal, Streeten) from both neoclassical and dependency schools.
>
> → *See also: Q8 (Neocolonial Dependence — another external explanation); 512-Political-Economy/modernization-theory/README.md (Rostow — the specific target of this critique)*

### Question 12 {#q-batch47-dev-05}

**Does economic growth ensure gender equity? Explain your answer with empirical evidence. (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Thesis — growth is insufficient (1) | Three channels: employment structure, unpaid work, social norms (3) | Empirical evidence from Bangladesh and cross-country (2) | Policy implications (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Links Kabeer's framework to growth theory. Tests ability to synthesise.

**No.** Economic growth does not automatically ensure gender equity. While growth can create opportunities for women's advancement (paid employment, education, improved health), the relationship is mediated by social institutions, labour market structures, and policy choices.

**Three reasons growth alone is insufficient:**

| Channel | How Growth Can Help | Why Growth Alone Fails |
|---------|--------------------|----------------------|
| **Labour markets** | Growth creates jobs that women can fill | Women concentrated in low-paid, informal, insecure work; occupational segregation persists |
| **Unpaid care work** | Growth enables investment in care infrastructure | Women still bear disproportionate unpaid work burden even as they enter paid employment |
| **Social norms** | Growth can weaken traditional constraints | Patriarchal norms persist despite economic change — "classical patriarchy" (Kandiyoti) adapts rather than disappears |

**Empirical evidence:**

**Cross-country:** The "feminisation U-hypothesis" (Goldin 1995) shows female LFPR first falls then rises with development. At low income, women work predominantly in family agriculture/enterprise (counted as employed). At middle income, women withdraw from the labour force as income rises and social status becomes linked to female domesticity. At high income, women re-enter through expanded service sector employment. This U-shape shows growth can initially reduce measured female labour force participation.

**Bangladesh evidence:** Female LFPR rose from ~26% (2010) to 42.77% (2022) — a substantial increase linked to growth. However: (a) women earn 30-40% less than men, (b) 87% of employed women are in informal work (own-account or unpaid family work), (c) women are concentrated in RMG and agriculture — not in high-productivity sectors, (d) female unemployment (3.59%) is slightly higher than male (3.50%) despite lower LFPR. Growth improved women's employment quantity but not employment quality.

**Kabeer's critique:** Sen's two pathways — societal (visible work increases societal valuation) and household (economic contribution increases bargaining power) — both require more than growth. They require: (a) that women have access to paid work, not just any work, (b) that social norms acknowledge women's economic contribution, (c) that policy supports women's labour market participation (childcare, transport, safety). Growth without these enabling conditions can leave gender inequality unchanged or even worsen it.

**Policy implications:** Gender equity requires targeted interventions: (1) closing the education-to-employment gap, (2) investing in care infrastructure to reduce unpaid work burden, (3) enforcing equal pay and anti-discrimination laws, (4) supporting women's entrepreneurship and access to credit, (5) addressing social norms through community-based programmes.

**Exam Tip:** Start with the direct answer (no), then explain why with the three channels, then provide evidence. The U-hypothesis and Bangladesh LFPR data are your strongest empirical cards.

> **Professor's Corner:** This question tests whether you understand the difference between **growth** (quantitative expansion of output) and **development** (qualitative improvement in wellbeing and capabilities). Neoclassical growth theory assumes equity outcomes follow from growth; the capabilities approach (Sen, Nussbaum) insists that equity requires deliberate action because markets reproduce existing power structures. The Bangladesh experience is a good test case: growth empowered some women (RMG workers) but left others behind (informal sector, rural women).
>

### Question 13 {#q-batch47-dev-06}

**Why do we think that "feminization of agriculture" in Bangladesh has taken place? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Definition of feminisation of agriculture (1) | Four causes: male out-migration, RMG pull, land fragmentation, poverty (3) | Evidence from Bangladesh LFS (1) | Consequences (1) | Connections to Kabeer framework (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Specific Bangladesh application of gender-and-development framework.

The feminisation of agriculture refers to the rising share of women in the agricultural labour force, as men shift to non-agricultural employment. In Bangladesh, this process has been driven by four interrelated factors:

| Driver | Mechanism | Evidence |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Male out-migration** | Rural men migrate to cities (RMG, construction, transport) or abroad (Middle East, Southeast Asia) for higher wages | Over 10 million Bangladeshi migrants abroad; internal rural-urban migration massive |
| **RMG sector pull** | The RMG sector (4+ million workers, 60% women) pulls women toward factory work — BUT also pulls men from agriculture, leaving women to manage farms | Female RMG employment rose from near-zero (1980s) to 2.5+ million |
| **Land fragmentation** | Population pressure fragments landholdings beyond viability for male household heads | Average farm size fell below 0.5 hectares; men seek non-farm income |
| **Poverty and distress** | Poor households cannot survive on male income alone; women enter agriculture out of necessity | Female agricultural employment concentrated in casual/day labour — the poorest category |

**Evidence from the LFS (2010–2022):** Female employment grew 53% (from 16.2M to 24.86M), far outpacing male employment growth (20%). Agriculture remains the largest employer for women (over 60% of employed women work in agriculture), even as men shift to industry and services. The gender composition of agricultural employment has shifted from majority-male to near parity.

**Consequences of feminisation:**

| Positive | Negative |
|----------|----------|
| Women gain agricultural knowledge and skills | Women lack land titles — cannot access credit, extension services |
| Women's economic contribution becomes visible | Women's work is classified as "contributing family worker" (unpaid) |
| Households maintain food production | Without male labour, women face labour constraints and lower yields |
| Women's decision-making in farming increases | Extension services and technology are male-targeted |

**Kabeer's framework:** The feminisation of agriculture is a classic "agency within constraint" story. Women are not choosing agriculture over other sectors — they are responding to male out-migration and RMG demand pulling men elsewhere. This expands women's responsibilities and knowledge (resources in Bourdieu's sense) but does not necessarily increase their bargaining power if their contribution remains unrecognised as "work" and if they lack land rights (symbolic capital).

**Exam Tip:** Structure around push-pull dynamics — men are pulled to RMG and migration, women are pushed into agriculture. The key evaluative point is whether feminisation represents empowerment (women gaining skills and decision-making) or burden (women bearing the triple load of productive, reproductive, and community work).

> **Professor's Corner:** The feminisation of agriculture is a global phenomenon (Asia, Africa, Latin America) but the drivers vary. In Africa, it is driven by male out-migration to mining and urban areas. In Latin America, by agro-export booms that employ men in export crops while women remain in subsistence. Bangladesh's pattern is distinctive because of the RMG sector's role: it simultaneously pulls women into industry AND (through male out-migration from rural areas) pushes other women into agriculture. The net effect on women's welfare depends on which pathway they follow.

### Question 14 {#q-batch47-dev-07}

**Explain the concept of "convergence theory". What are the statistical pitfalls in Baumol's unconditional convergence theory? How were these addressed? (5+9 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Definition of absolute vs conditional convergence (2) | Baumol's 1986 study and findings (3) | Three statistical pitfalls: selection bias, measurement error, omitted variables (5) | MRW solution — conditional convergence with steady-state controls (4)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Directly links to Solow model in Macroeconomics. Tests understanding of growth empirics.

**(A) Convergence Theory**

Convergence theory posits that poorer economies grow faster than richer ones, eventually catching up in income levels. Two versions:

| Type | Prediction | Basis |
|------|------------|-------|
| **Absolute (unconditional) convergence** | All economies converge to the same steady-state income level regardless of initial conditions | Assumes identical preferences, technology, and institutions across countries |
| **Conditional convergence** | Economies converge to their own steady state; poor countries grow faster only after controlling for steady-state determinants (savings rate, population growth, human capital) | Solow model with heterogeneous parameters |

The Solow growth model predicts conditional convergence: economies with lower initial capital per worker grow faster, controlling for the steady-state determinants. The further an economy is from its steady state, the faster it grows (transition dynamics).

**(B) Baumol's Unconditional Convergence Theory and Statistical Pitfalls**

William Baumol (1986) appeared to find strong evidence of convergence: his sample of 16 industrialised countries from 1870–1979 showed poorer countries growing faster. However, this finding suffered from three critical statistical pitfalls:

| Pitfall | Explanation | Why It Biases Results |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Selection bias (endogenous sample)** | Baumol selected countries that are now rich — the "survivors" | Countries that failed to industrialise (and thus remained poor) were excluded. If you only look at countries that ended up rich, you see convergence by construction |
| **Measurement error** | Historical GDP data for early periods are unreliable | Measurement errors in initial GDP per capita bias the estimated convergence coefficient toward finding convergence (regression to the mean — a statistical artefact) |
| **Omitted variables** | Baumol's regression of growth on initial income omitted steady-state determinants | Countries that grew faster had higher savings rates, better institutions, or more human capital — not just lower initial income |

**(C) MRW Solution**

Mankiw, Romer, and Weil (1992) addressed these pitfalls with three innovations:

**1. Larger, representative sample:** MRW used a sample of 98 non-oil countries (and 75 intermediate and 22 OECD), avoiding Baumol's survivor bias. They confirmed conditional convergence: the coefficient on initial income becomes negative and significant once steady-state determinants are controlled.

**2. Augmented Solow model:** MRW added human capital to the Solow model (physical capital + human capital + labour). Their estimating equation:

$$ \ln y(t) - \ln y(0) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln y(0) + \beta_2 \ln s_k + \beta_3 \ln s_h + \beta_4 \ln (n+g+\delta) + \varepsilon $$

Where $s_k$ is physical capital investment, $s_h$ is human capital investment, $n$ is population growth, and $g+\delta$ is technology growth plus depreciation (assumed constant at 0.05).

**3. Key findings:** (a) The augmented Solow model explains about 80% of cross-country income variation. (b) Conditional convergence holds — poor countries grow faster, controlling for steady-state determinants. (c) The speed of convergence is approximately 2% per year (consistent with Solow calibration). (d) Human capital matters as much as physical capital for explaining income differences.

**Remaining puzzles:** (1) The estimated convergence speed (2%) is slower than the 4-6% implied by Solow calibration. (2) The model explains OECD convergence well but not African non-convergence. (3) Technology is assumed to be a public good — in reality, technology diffusion is limited by absorptive capacity and institutions.

**Exam Tip:** The (5+9) split means the first part is a short definition of convergence (absolute vs conditional), the second is a detailed evaluation of Baumol and MRW. Focus the long answer on the three pitfalls and how MRW addressed each.

> **Professor's Corner:** The convergence debate is one of the most important in growth economics. MRW showed the Solow model fits cross-country data reasonably well once human capital is included — but subsequent research questioned whether human capital is properly measured (Barro & Lee education data) and whether the estimated coefficients capture reverse causality (faster growth causes more education investment). Recent work uses panel data and system GMM to address endogeneity. The "convergence club" literature suggests there may be multiple convergence clubs (rich countries converge among themselves, poor among themselves) rather than a single convergence path — consistent with poverty trap models.
>
> → *See also: Macroeconomics Solow model notes (theoretical basis); Macroeconomics endogenous growth notes (non-convergence prediction)*

### Question 15 {#q-batch47-dev-08}

**How does IMF conditionality measure the impact of economic and social policies of recipient countries? What are the potential benefits and drawbacks of IMF conditionality? (7+7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Measurement mechanisms — performance criteria, structural benchmarks, reviews (4) | Typical objectives (3) | Benefits — stabilisation, credibility, discipline (4) | Drawbacks — sovereignty loss, social costs, moral hazard, pro-cyclicality (4)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Controversial policy topic. Links to Washington Consensus in Political Economy.

**(A) How IMF Conditionality Measures Impact**

IMF conditionality operates through a framework of monitoring and enforcement mechanisms attached to loan disbursements. Key instruments:

| Instrument | Description | How It Measures Impact |
|------------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Quantitative Performance Criteria (QPCs)** | Specific, measurable targets (e.g., fiscal deficit < 3% of GDP, reserve accumulation > $X) | Through statistical reports submitted quarterly; deviations trigger loan suspension |
| **Structural Benchmarks** | Policy actions (e.g., pass a tax reform law, privatise a state bank) | Implementation verified through prior actions; not easily quantified but monitored qualitatively |
| **Programme Reviews** | Quarterly or semi-annual assessments of overall compliance | Combined QPC and benchmark review determines whether next tranche is disbursed |
| **Prior Actions** | Conditions that must be met before the loan is approved | Ensures immediate policy change |
| **Indicative Targets** | Non-binding targets for broader economic variables (e.g., inflation, growth) | Tracked for early warning but not tied to disbursement |

**Typical objectives** behind conditionality include: (a) restore macroeconomic stability (reduce inflation, stabilise exchange rates), (b) correct fiscal imbalances (reduce deficits, broaden tax base), (c) promote structural reform (liberalisation, privatisation), (d) rebuild foreign exchange reserves, (e) restore confidence among international investors. These reflect the IMF's mandate of maintaining global financial stability and its institutional preference for market-oriented policies.

**(B) Potential Benefits and Drawbacks**

| Dimension | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|-----------|----------|-----------|
| **Macroeconomic stability** | Provides framework for fiscal discipline and monetary control; can break inflationary inertia | Conditionality is often **pro-cyclical** — requiring austerity during recessions worsens downturns (IMF 2011 Independent Evaluation Office critique) |
| **Credibility** | IMF seal of approval signals to private investors that the country is reforming; can catalyse additional capital flows | Conditionality signals loss of policy autonomy; investment response is often weak if investors doubt reform sustainability |
| **Structural reform** | External pressure can overcome domestic resistance to needed reforms (trade liberalisation, subsidy reform) | Reforms designed in Washington may be inappropriate for local context — "one-size-fits-all" criticism |
| **Social welfare** | IMF has introduced "social spending floors" in some programmes to protect health and education | Evidence from 1980s–90s SAPs shows increased poverty, malnutrition, and inequality; cuts to subsidies hurt the poor |
| **Moral hazard** | Conditionality reduces moral hazard by ensuring borrowers do not take IMF resources for granted | Too much conditionality (up to 140 conditions per programme in some cases) overwhelms administrative capacity and creates "conditionality overload" |
| **Sovereignty** | — | Conditionality fundamentally limits national policy space; elected governments can be forced to implement unpopular policies |

**Empirical evidence:** The literature is mixed. Some studies find IMF programmes improve the current account and reserves but have no significant effect on growth (controlling for selection bias — countries in crisis are more likely to seek IMF help). Others find negative effects on investment and income distribution. The 2011 IEO report found that IMF programmes achieved short-term stabilisation objectives but at substantial social cost, and that structural conditionality particularly had weak justification and limited effectiveness.

**Bangladesh context:** Bangladesh has had a limited engagement with IMF conditional lending (precautionary arrangements, not full-scale SAPs), partly explaining its ability to maintain policy space — a contrast with Latin American and African countries that underwent deep IMF-mandated reforms.

**Exam Tip:** For the first 7 marks, focus on the monitoring framework (QPCs, benchmarks, reviews). For the second 7 marks, present benefits and drawbacks as a balanced table and conclude that the net assessment is context-dependent. Avoid taking a strong ideological position — show awareness of both sides.

> **Professor's Corner:** The scholarly consensus has shifted against extensive conditionality. The IMF's own research (e.g., Boughton 2015) acknowledges that programmes with fewer, more focused conditions perform better than those with many conditions. The shift to "country ownership" — where the borrowing government designs the reform programme — reflects this learning. However, critics argue that in practice the IMF still controls the agenda, and "ownership" is rhetorical rather than real. The debate over conditionality remains at the heart of North-South economic relations.

### Question 16 {#q-batch47-dev-09}

**Why did the world population grow so fast after the industrial revolution? What are the key components in the nexus of population growth and economic development? (7+7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Mortality decline — causes and timing (3) | Fertility lag — demographic transition theory (3) | Role of industrial revolution in reducing death rates (2) | Key nexus components: dependency ratio, human capital, Malthus vs Boserup (4) | Policy implications (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Classical population-economics debate. Connects to themes of demographic dividend and labour supply.

**(A) Why Population Grew Fast After the Industrial Revolution**

World population was roughly stable at under 1 billion for most of human history until around 1800. It reached 1 billion around 1800, 2 billion by 1927, 4 billion by 1974, and 8 billion by 2022. This explosive growth — the "population explosion" — was caused by a **decline in mortality rates** while **fertility rates remained high**, producing rapid natural increase.

**Mechanisms of mortality decline:**

| Factor | How It Reduced Deaths | Timing |
|--------|----------------------|--------|
| **Improved nutrition** | Agricultural revolution (crop rotation, new world crops) increased food supply; fewer famine deaths | 1750–1850 (Europe) |
| **Public health** | Clean water, sanitation, sewage systems reduced infectious disease | 1850–1900 |
| **Medical advances** | Vaccination (smallpox), antibiotics (penicillin 1928), antiseptics | 1880–1950 |
| **Transport and trade** | Rail and steamship allowed food distribution to famine-prone areas | 1850+ |

**The demographic transition theory** explains the sequence: pre-industrial societies have high birth rates and high death rates (stable population). Industrialisation reduces death rates first (nutrition, public health, medicine) while birth rates remain high (cultural lag — children remain valued for labour, old-age security, and due to limited contraception). This creates a **demographic gap** between falling deaths and persistent births, producing rapid population growth. Eventually, birth rates also fall (urbanisation, female education, declining child mortality reduces demand for children, contraception availability), and population stabilises at low birth/death rates.

**Why this mattered for development:** The rapid population growth initially appeared threatening (Malthusian fears) but eventually generated the "demographic dividend" — a period when the working-age population grows faster than total population, freeing resources for investment and growth.

**(B) Key Components in the Population-Development Nexus**

| Component | Mechanism | Development Implication |
|-----------|-----------|------------------------|
| **Demographic dividend** | Falling fertility → fewer dependents per worker → more savings and investment per capita | East Asian "miracle" — one-third of growth attributed to demographics (Bloom & Williamson) |
| **Human capital** | Slower population growth → more investment per child → healthier, more educated workforce | Quality-quantity trade-off (Becker) — smaller families invest more per child |
| **Dependency ratio** | Ratio of non-working (0-14, 65+) to working-age (15-64) population | High dependency → consumption burden; low dependency → window for growth |
| **Labour supply** | Population growth → expanding labour force | Can be dividend (if productive employment exists) or liability (if surplus labour without jobs) |
| **Resource pressure** | Malthus: population grows geometrically, food arithmetically | Boserup counter: population pressure drives innovation in agricultural techniques |

**Two key theorists:**

**Malthus (1798):** Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio (1, 2, 4, 8...) while subsistence increases in an arithmetical ratio (1, 2, 3, 4...). Therefore, population growth inevitably exceeds food supply, leading to famine, disease, and war as "positive checks." This prediction failed historically because technological change (the industrial and agricultural revolutions) increased food supply faster than population grew.

**Boserup (1965):** Reversed Malthus. Population pressure is the **driver** of agricultural innovation, not a constraint. As population density increases, farmers intensify land use (shorter fallow periods, irrigation, fertiliser). Evidence from African agricultural intensification supports Boserup's thesis.

**Contemporary relevance:** Bangladesh has experienced a dramatic fertility decline from 6.3 (1975) to 2.2 (2011) — one of the fastest in developing-country history. This created a demographic dividend as the working-age share rose. However, absorbing the growing labour force into productive employment remains the central challenge — reflected in high youth unemployment and informal employment (50.6% own-account workers).

**Exam Tip:** For the first 7 marks, focus on the mortality decline mechanisms and demographic transition theory. For the second 7 marks, organise around the five components and the Malthus-Boserup debate. Use Bangladesh fertility data as your empirical anchor.

> **Professor's Corner:** The demographic dividend is not automatic — it requires policies that create productive employment for the expanding workforce. East Asia succeeded because labour-intensive manufacturing exports absorbed the demographic boom. Bangladesh has the demographic conditions for a dividend but the labour absorption challenge (RMG alone cannot employ everyone) limits its realisation. The difference between a "demographic dividend" and a "demographic disaster" is the quality of economic policy and the structure of labour demand.
>

### Question 17 {#q-batch47-dev-10}

**Write short notes on: (i) Comprador Group (ii) Unpaid employment (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Comprador definition, role in dependency theory, historical illustration (3.5) | Unpaid employment definition, measurement, gender dimension, policy significance (3.5)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Short-note format. Tests precise knowledge of two distinct concepts.

**(i) Comprador Group**

The comprador group is a domestic elite class in developing countries that serves as an intermediary for foreign capital, benefiting from and perpetuating dependent economic relationships. The term originates from Portuguese ("buyer") — referring to local agents who facilitated European colonial trade in Asia.

In dependency theory (Frank 1967, Dos Santos 1970), the comprador class is a crucial mechanism of neocolonial control. Its members — import-export merchants, local partners of MNCs, political elites who facilitate foreign investment — have interests aligned with foreign capital rather than national development. They benefit from: (a) commissions on foreign trade, (b) access to imported luxury goods, (c) positions as local managers of multinational firms, (d) political advantages from foreign government connections.

The existence of a comprador class explains why dependent countries struggle to pursue autonomous development — the local elite actively resists policies that would reduce external dependence (trade protection, capital controls, industrial policy) because their wealth depends on maintaining the dependent relationship. Bangladesh examples include: import-export intermediaries benefiting from RMG supply chains, local agents of foreign mining/pharma companies, and political elites connected to foreign aid flows.

**Exam Tip:** Define clearly, locate in dependency theory, give a Bangladesh example. Keep precise for the short-note format.

**(ii) Unpaid Employment**

Unpaid employment refers to work that contributes to economic output but is not remunerated through wages or salary. The ICLS defines it as "contributing family work" — persons who work without pay in a market-oriented establishment operated by a related person living in the same household. It is a subset of **own-use production work** and **informal employment**.

In Bangladesh (LFS 2022), 6.8% of employed persons are contributing family workers. However, this official figure likely underestimates the true extent because: (a) women's unpaid domestic and care work is excluded from employment statistics entirely, (b) many own-account workers (50.6%) effectively work without stable remuneration, (c) subsistence agriculture work is borderline between unpaid employment and own-use production.

**Feminist economics critique** (Beneria, Floro, Elson): Unpaid work — predominantly performed by women — is systematically invisible in standard labour statistics. The System of National Accounts (SNA) excludes unpaid domestic and care work from GDP, despite estimates that it constitutes 30-50% of economic activity in developing countries. This invisibility has policy consequences: if women's work is not counted, policies that affect it (childcare provision, infrastructure investment, labour law reform) are deprioritised.

**Kabeer's connection:** Unpaid employment is a form of work without agency — women contribute economically but lack the recognition and resources (income, social security, bargaining power) that paid employment provides. The distinction between paid and unpaid work is central to understanding how economic growth can coexist with persistent gender inequality.

**Exam Tip:** For the short-note format, structure as: definition (1 sentence) → measurement (1 sentence) → critique (2 sentences) → Bangladesh data (1 sentence) → connection to course themes (1 sentence).

> **Professor's Corner:** The unpaid employment concept bridges development economics and feminist economics. The "care economy" literature (Razavi 2007) argues that unpaid care work is the invisible foundation on which the paid economy rests — without it, the labour force could not reproduce itself. Policies that recognise and reduce unpaid work burdens (public childcare, infrastructure that reduces time spent collecting water/fuel) are development investments, not consumption spending.

### Question 18 {#q-batch47-dev-11}

**In terms of 'life expectancy', why are some countries lagging behind others with similar economic conditions? Explain with country experiences. (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Thesis — income alone does not determine health outcomes (1) | Five factors: health systems, public goods, inequality, institutional quality, cultural factors (3) | Country examples — Bangladesh vs India vs Pakistan, Cuba/US comparison (2) | Policy implications (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Tests understanding of non-income determinants of development. Complements the Bangladesh paradox.

Life expectancy is not determined by income alone. Countries with similar GDP per capita can have vastly different health outcomes because life expectancy depends on how economic resources are translated into health — through public policy, social organisation, and institutional arrangements.

**Five factors explaining the divergence:**

| Factor | Mechanism | Country Example |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Health system quality** | Universal coverage, primary care access, public health infrastructure | Cuba's life expectancy (79 yrs) equals US despite <10% of income — primary care focus, preventive emphasis |
| **Public goods provision** | Clean water, sanitation, vaccination programmes | Sri Lanka achieved high life expectancy (77 yrs) at low income through early investment in public health |
| **Inequality** | More equal societies invest more in public goods; the poor cannot afford private health care | The US spends 17% of GDP on health but has lower life expectancy (77 yrs) than universal-coverage systems (Japan 84, Switzerland 83) |
| **Institutional quality** | Effective government, low corruption, rule of law | Botswana's life expectancy (60 yrs, pre-ART) vs South Africa's (55 yrs — apartheid legacy) at similar income levels |
| **Cultural and behavioural factors** | Diet, smoking, exercise, social cohesion | Japan's low heart disease reflects diet and social factors beyond the health system |

**South Asian comparison:** Bangladesh (life expectancy 70 yrs, income ~$2,800) outperforms both India (67 yrs, income ~$2,500) and Pakistan (66 yrs, income ~$1,600) despite similar or lower income. Kabeer's explanation — women's agency, girls' education investment, community health workers, NGO-led health programmes — shows that social mobilisation and targeted public policy matter more than income alone.

**Cuba paradox:** Cuba's life expectancy (79 years) is comparable to the United States (77-79 years) despite an income difference exceeding 10-fold. Explanations: (a) universal, free health care as a constitutional right, (b) emphasis on primary care and prevention over expensive tertiary care, (c) high doctor-to-population ratio, (d) public health campaigns embedded in community organisations. However, post-Soviet economic collapse (1990s) showed that health systems need economic foundations — Cuba's health indicators deteriorated temporarily during the "Special Period."

**Broader implications:** The variance in life expectancy at similar income levels demonstrates that development policies must look beyond growth to how resources are distributed and used. Investing in health systems (particularly primary care), public goods (water, sanitation), and reducing inequality may yield greater health improvements than growth alone. This reinforces the capabilities approach (Sen): what matters is not total output but what people are able to do and be with available resources.

**Exam Tip:** For 7 marks, use one strong comparison (Cuba vs US for global; Bangladesh vs India/Pakistan for regional) and explain the factors behind the disparity. The key theoretical move is distinguishing between income (means) and capabilities (ends).

> **Professor's Corner:** The life expectancy question is a direct test of the **capabilities approach** (Sen, Nussbaum) versus the **utility/income approach** (standard neoclassical). If income were all that mattered for wellbeing, life expectancy would perfectly correlate with GDP — but it does not. The "health transition" literature (Caldwell 1986, Riley 2001) shows that the achieved level of health depends on the "health social system" — the interaction of health services, education, nutrition, and household behaviour. Countries that invest early in this system (Sri Lanka, Costa Rica, Kerala state in India) achieved health outcomes decades ahead of their income levels.

### Question 19 {#q-batch48-dev-01}

**What is endogeneity in regression analysis, and how does it arise? Explain with a health-income example. How can Instrumental Variables (IV) address this? (14 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Definition of endogeneity (2) | Three sources: omitted variables, measurement error, simultaneity (3) | Health-income example (3) | IV solution — valid instruments, exclusion restriction (4) | Limitations (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Core econometric concept for development research. Links to causality debates.

**(A) What Is Endogeneity and How Does It Arise?**

Endogeneity occurs when an explanatory variable is correlated with the error term in a regression model:

$$ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon, \quad \text{where } \text{Cov}(x, \varepsilon) \neq 0 $$

This violates OLS Assumption 3 (zero conditional mean). The consequence is that OLS estimates are **biased and inconsistent** — they do not converge to the true parameter value even with infinite sample size.

**Three sources of endogeneity:**

| Source | Mechanism | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Omitted variable bias** | An unobserved factor affects both x and y | Ability affects both education and earnings; omitting ability biases the education coefficient |
| **Measurement error** | x is measured with error; the error correlates with x | Self-reported income contains reporting error; attenuation bias toward zero |
| **Simultaneity (reverse causality)** | y causes x as well as x causes y | GDP affects health expenditure; health expenditure affects GDP |

**(B) Health-Income Example**

Suppose we estimate the effect of health on labour market outcomes:

$$ \text{Income}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Health}_i + \varepsilon_i $$

**Why health is endogenous:**

| Source | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Reverse causality** | Higher income improves health (better nutrition, health care, living conditions). The causal arrow runs both ways |
| **Omitted variables** | Education, time preference, and genetic factors affect both health and income. Someone with higher time preference invests less in health AND less in career |
| **Measurement error** | Self-reported health status is subjective; people with the same objective health may report differently based on income or social desirability bias |

The OLS estimate of $\beta_1$ is therefore biased — it captures not only the causal effect of health on income but also the effect of income on health and the effect of omitted variables on both.

**(C) Instrumental Variables Solution**

IV addresses endogeneity by using an **instrument** (Z) that satisfies two conditions:

| Condition | Formal Requirement | Intuition |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Relevance** | Cov(Z, x) ≠ 0 | The instrument predicts the endogenous variable |
| **Exclusion restriction** | Cov(Z, ε) = 0 | The instrument affects y ONLY through x (no direct path, no omitted path) |

**Two-stage least squares (2SLS) procedure:**

1. **First stage:** Regress the endogenous variable (Health) on the instrument(s): $\text{Health}_i = \pi_0 + \pi_1 Z_i + \upsilon_i$
2. **Second stage:** Regress the outcome (Income) on the predicted health values: $\text{Income}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \widehat{\text{Health}}_i + \varepsilon_i$

The predicted health $\widehat{\text{Health}}_i$ contains only the exogenous variation in health (driven by the instrument), so $\beta_1$ is consistently estimated.

**Example instruments for health-income:** (a) family history of illness (genetic variation in health not affected by income), (b) access to health services determined by policy (e.g., distance to nearest clinic), (c) health shocks (accidents) unrelated to income, (d) policy changes (Medicaid expansion, introduction of universal health care).

**(D) Limitations of IV**

| Limitation | Issue |
|------------|-------|
| **Weak instruments** | If Cov(Z, x) is weak, IV estimates are biased and have large standard errors |
| **Exclusion restriction untestable** | The assumption that Z affects y only through x cannot be directly tested; must be justified theoretically |
| **LATE interpretation** | IV estimates the Local Average Treatment Effect for compliers (those whose treatment status shifts with the instrument) — not the average effect for the whole population |
| **Small sample bias** | IV is biased in finite samples; the bias approaches OLS bias as instrument strength decreases |

**Exam Tip:** For the full 14-mark question, spend 3-4 minutes on each part (definition, health example, IV solution, limitations). Use the health-income example throughout to keep the answer focused.

> **Professor's Corner:** The endogeneity problem is central to development econometrics because most variables of interest (institutions, policy, health, education) are endogenous — they are chosen by actors, not randomly assigned. The "credibility revolution" in development economics (Angrist & Pischke 2010) was driven by awareness that without addressing endogeneity, regression estimates are not causal. Natural experiments, difference-in-differences, and regression discontinuity designs are now preferred over OLS for establishing causal effects in development research.
>

### Question 20 {#q-batch48-dev-02}

**Compare logit and probit models. What are their key similarities and differences? How do you interpret their coefficients? (14 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Similarities — both binary choice, S-shaped, latent variable (3) | Differences — logistic vs normal distribution, tail behaviour (4) | Coefficient interpretation — log-odds, odds ratios, marginal effects (5) | Practical guidance for choice (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Standard binary choice models. Common in development microeconometrics.

**(A) Similarities**

Logit and probit are both models for binary dependent variables ($y \in \{0, 1\}$). They share:

| Similarity | Detail |
|------------|--------|
| **Purpose** | Model the probability that $y = 1$ given covariates $x$ |
| **S-shaped response** | $P(y=1|x) = F(x'\beta)$ where $F$ is a cumulative distribution function — probability is bounded between 0 and 1 |
| **Latent variable basis** | Both assume a latent (unobserved) continuous variable $y^* = x'\beta + \varepsilon$ with $y = 1$ if $y^* > 0$, $y = 0$ otherwise |
| **Nonlinear** | Marginal effects depend on the value of $x$, not constant as in LPM |
| **Estimation** | Both estimated by Maximum Likelihood (MLE) |
| **Asymptotic properties** | Both yield consistent, asymptotically normal, efficient estimates under standard conditions |

**(B) Differences**

| Feature | Logit | Probit |
|---------|-------|--------|
| **Link function** | Logistic CDF: $\Lambda(z) = e^z / (1 + e^z)$ | Normal CDF: $\Phi(z) = \int_{-\infty}^z \phi(t) dt$ |
| **Error distribution** | $\varepsilon$ follows logistic distribution (variance $\pi^2/3 \approx 3.29$) | $\varepsilon$ follows standard normal distribution (variance 1) |
| **Tails** | Fatter tails — probability approaches 0/1 more slowly | Thinner tails — probability approaches 0/1 more quickly |
| **Coefficient scale** | ~1.8 times larger than probit for same data (because variance is larger) | ~0.55 times logit coefficients |
| **Interpretation** | Exact: odds = $\exp(\beta)$; $P(y=1|x)$ = exactly logistic | No closed form for $P(y=1|x)$; must use numerical integration |
| **Computational** | Slightly faster (no numerical integration needed) | Slightly slower |
| **Common use** | Epidemiology, economics (logistic is more common) | Biostatistics (tradition), some econometrics |

**(C) Coefficient Interpretation**

**Logit coefficient ($\beta$):** A one-unit increase in $x$ increases the **log-odds** of $y=1$ by $\beta$. Log-odds are not intuitive, so we convert:

$$ \text{Odds} = \frac{P(y=1)}{P(y=0)} = e^{x'\beta} $$

- **Odds ratio ($e^\beta$):** A one-unit increase in $x$ multiplies the odds of $y=1$ by $e^\beta$. If $e^\beta = 1.7$, odds increase by 70%.
- **Marginal effect:** $\frac{\partial P(y=1|x)}{\partial x_k} = \beta_k \cdot \Lambda(x'\beta) \cdot [1 - \Lambda(x'\beta)]$ — depends on the value of $x$, typically evaluated at the mean or averaged across observations.

**Probit coefficient:** Cannot be directly interpreted as log-odds. Convert to marginal effects:

$$ \frac{\partial P(y=1|x)}{\partial x_k} = \beta_k \cdot \phi(x'\beta) $$

A probit marginal effect of 0.12 means a one-unit increase in $x$ increases the probability of $y=1$ by 12 percentage points (at the specified $x$ value).

**Multinomial logit:** Used when $y$ has $>2$ unordered categories (e.g., Employed, Self-employed, Unemployed). Coefficients are interpreted **relative to a base category**. If "Male" coefficient for "Self-employed" (base = "Unemployed") = 1.2, then the log-odds of being self-employed rather than unemployed is 1.2 higher for men than women. The odds ratio is $e^{1.2} \approx 3.32$ — men have 3.32 times the odds of being self-employed versus unemployed compared to women.

**Practical guidance:** Logit and probit rarely give substantively different results in practice. The choice is largely a matter of discipline convention (economists often prefer logit for the odds-ratio interpretation; biostatisticians prefer probit). Key criterion: if you care about **extreme probabilities** (very high or very low), the different tail behaviour may matter — logit assigns higher probability to extreme outcomes.

**Exam Tip:** The coefficient interpretation part (Q2c in the exam) can be a standalone calculation question. Know the formulas: logit coefficient → odds ratio ($e^\beta$), marginal effect = $\beta \cdot P \cdot (1-P)$.

> **Professor's Corner:** The logit/probit decision is not as consequential as textbooks suggest. Amemiya (1981) showed that the two models produce nearly identical predictions except at the tails. A more important choice is between binary choice models and the **Linear Probability Model** (LPM). LPM has known problems (heteroskedasticity, predicted probabilities outside [0,1], non-normal errors) but is widely used in applied work because coefficients are directly interpretable as marginal effects and fixed-effects estimation is straightforward. The guidance: use LPM for robustness, logit/probit for efficiency and bounded predictions.
>

### Question 21 {#q-batch48-dev-03}

**What is cointegration? How can we test for cointegration? Why prefer the ARDL bounds testing approach over Johansen? (14 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Definition of cointegration — long-run equilibrium among non-stationary variables (3) | Engle-Granger two-step method (3) | Johansen system approach (3) | ARDL bounds testing advantages — mixed I(0)/I(1), small samples, single equation (5)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Essential time-series concept for development macroeconometrics.

**(A) What Is Cointegration?**

Two or more non-stationary (unit root) variables are **cointegrated** if a linear combination of them is stationary — meaning they share a common long-run equilibrium relationship despite trending individually in the short run.

$$ y_t \sim I(1), \; x_t \sim I(1), \; z_t = y_t - \beta x_t \sim I(0) $$

**Intuition:** If $y_t$ and $x_t$ are both random walks, they will drift apart in general — unless some force (economic theory: purchasing power parity, interest rate parity, consumption function) ties them together in the long run. Cointegration formalises this: the variables are "tied together" by an equilibrium relationship; deviations from equilibrium are temporary and self-correct.

**Economic examples:**
- Consumption and income (they share a long-run proportion)
- Exchange rates and prices (PPP holds in the long run)
- Money demand: real money balances, income, and interest rates
- Interest rates across countries (uncovered interest parity)

**(B) Testing for Cointegration**

**Engle-Granger Two-Step Method (1987):**

| Step | Procedure | Key Points |
|------|-----------|------------|
| **1. Test unit roots** | Confirm all variables are $I(1)$ using ADF or PP tests | If variables have different orders, EG cannot be used |
| **2. Estimate static OLS** | $y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + \varepsilon_t$ | "Cointegrating regression" |
| **3. Test residuals for stationarity** | Apply ADF test to $\hat{\varepsilon}_t$ (residuals) | Use MacKinnon critical values (not standard Dickey-Fuller) — because residuals are estimated |

**Limitations:** (1) Only one cointegrating relationship. (2) Sensitive to which variable is normalised (placed on LHS). (3) Two-step means errors from step 1 carry into step 2. (4) Cannot handle more than two variables efficiently.

**Johansen Maximum Likelihood Approach (1991, 1995):**

A system-based approach that can identify **multiple cointegrating vectors** in a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM):

$$ \Delta y_t = \Pi y_{t-1} + \Gamma_1 \Delta y_{t-1} + \ldots + \Gamma_{p-1} \Delta y_{t-p+1} + \varepsilon_t $$

where $\Pi = \alpha \beta'$, $\beta$ contains the cointegrating vectors, and $\alpha$ contains the adjustment (speed of correction) parameters. Two likelihood-ratio tests — trace test and maximum eigenvalue test — determine the number of cointegrating vectors ($r$).

| Advantage | Limitation |
|-----------|------------|
| Handles multiple cointegrating relationships | Requires all variables to be $I(1)$ |
| System approach — no normalisation issue | Large sample required |
| Tests for $r$ (number of cointegrating vectors) | Sensitive to lag length specification |

**(C) ARDL Bounds Testing — Why Prefer It?**

The Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) bounds testing approach (Pesaran, Shin & Smith 2001) has become the preferred method in applied development econometrics for several reasons:

| Advantage over Johansen | Explanation |
|------------------------|-------------|
| **Mixed I(0)/I(1) variables** | ARDL works when variables are $I(0)$, $I(1)$, or a mix — Johansen requires all $I(1)$. This is critical because many macroeconomic variables are borderline between the two |
| **Small sample performance** | ARDL is more efficient in small samples (typical in developing-country time series with 30-40 annual observations) |
| **Single equation estimation** | ARDL estimates one equation at a time — simpler to specify and interpret than the VECM system |
| **Cointegration test embedded** | The bounds test (F-test on lagged levels) directly tests for cointegration without needing unit root pre-testing with certainty |
| **Handles endogeneity** | ARDL with sufficient lags is robust to weak endogeneity of regressors |
| **Short-run and long-run separately** | Error Correction representation separates short-run dynamics from long-run equilibrium |

**ARDL procedure:**
1. Estimate the unrestricted error correction model (ECM): $\Delta y_t = \alpha + \sum \beta_i \Delta y_{t-i} + \sum \gamma_j \Delta x_{t-j} + \delta_1 y_{t-1} + \delta_2 x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$
2. Compute the **F-statistic** for the joint significance of lagged levels: $H_0: \delta_1 = \delta_2 = 0$
3. Compare to **Pesaran bounds** — two sets of critical values (lower bound for $I(0)$, upper for $I(1)$)
4. If F-stat exceeds upper bound, reject $H_0$ → cointegration exists regardless of whether variables are $I(0)$ or $I(1)$

**When Johansen is still preferred:** (a) with more than 5-6 variables (ARDL becomes cumbersome), (b) when theory predicts multiple cointegrating relationships, (c) when the sample is large enough, (d) when system inference on adjustment parameters across equations is needed.

**Exam Tip:** For 14 marks, spend about 4 minutes on definition and intuition, 5 minutes on testing methods (EG and Johansen), and 5 minutes on ARDL advantages. Use a concrete development example (e.g., FDI-growth nexus) to illustrate the application.

> **Professor's Corner:** The ARDL bounds test revolutionised applied time-series econometrics in development because it freed researchers from the "unit root pre-testing problem" — unit root tests have low power, especially in small samples, so pre-testing leads to frequent pre-testing errors that cascade into the cointegration analysis. ARDL's ability to handle mixed integration orders was a major practical advance. However, its main limitation is sensitivity to lag length selection (use AIC/SIC, but different criteria may suggest different lags) and its assumption of a single long-run relationship — for modelling systems with multiple equilibrium relationships, Johansen remains essential.
