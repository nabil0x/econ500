### Question 2 {#q-kabeer-02}

**What is Classical Patriarchy? What are the different forms of women's agency? Explain the evolution of women's agency in Bangladesh's development trajectory. (20 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [20 marks]:** Definition of classical patriarchy — Kandiyoti (5) | Forms of agency — adaptive preferences to overt resistance (5) | Evolution in Bangladesh — resources, bargaining, incremental change (7) | Theoretical references (3)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Central Kabeer concept. Patriarchy + agency = the core analytical framework.

**(i) Classical Patriarchy**

Classical patriarchy, as theorised by Deniz Kandiyoti (1988) and Mead Cain (1984), describes a system of male dominance found in a belt extending from North Africa through the Middle East to Northern South Asia, including Bangladesh. It is characterised by four structural features: **patrilineal** inheritance (property passes through the male line), **patrilocal** residence (wives move to the husband's family home), **patriarchal** authority (men hold decision-making power), and **endogamy** (marriage within the same group, reinforcing kin control over women).

Dyson and Moore (1983) distinguished the North Indian kinship system (covering Bangladesh) from the South Indian system. In the North, women's mobility is restricted, fertility is higher, and sex ratios are more skewed (106–114 males per 100 females versus 97–107 in the South). The consequences include Amartya Sen's "missing women" — over 100 million women globally absent due to systematic discrimination — strong son preference driving high fertility, dowry practices, early marriage, and restricted mobility outside the home.

**(ii) Forms of Women's Agency**

Agency is defined as the "capacity to define goals and act upon them within social constraints" (Giddens 1979). Kabeer identifies a spectrum of agency forms:

| Form | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Adaptive preferences** | Adjusting desires to what seems possible within constraints (Sen, Nussbaum) | Women saying they "don't want" education because it's unavailable |
| **Hidden transcripts** | Private expressions of resistance not visible to power holders (Scott 1985) | Women's songs, gossip, informal networks |
| **Bargaining** | Using resources to negotiate better terms within relationships | Earning income → more say in household decisions |
| **Overt resistance** | Public, organised challenge to patriarchal norms | Women's movements, collective action |

Kandiyoti's concept of **patriarchal bargains** is key: women pursue security and advantage within patriarchy rather than openly challenging it. Producing sons, for example, is a strategy for status and old-age security, not simply passive compliance. This explains why revolutionary change is rare but incremental renegotiation is constant.

**(iii) Evolution of Women's Agency in Bangladesh**

Bangladesh's development trajectory shows an evolution in women's agency without revolutionary transformation. Kabeer identifies four phases:

**Phase 1 (pre-1980s):** Women's agency exercised almost entirely within the family — through patriarchal bargains of son preference, fertility decisions, and household management. Adaptive preferences dominated. The state was indifferent to women's issues.

**Phase 2 (1980s–1990s):** Three forces created new resources: (a) the RMG export boom offered paid employment to millions of women, (b) NGO expansion (BRAC, Grameen Bank) provided microfinance and health services targeting women, (c) state investment in girls' education raised female literacy. These resources shifted women's bargaining power within households (Sen's household bargaining pathway).

**Phase 3 (2000s):** Women translated economic contribution into social achievements. The fertility rate halved from 4.5 (1980) to 2.2 (2011). Son preference declined sharply — most families now prefer balanced sex composition. Female life expectancy surpassed male. These outcomes reflect cumulative changes in household decision-making.

**Phase 4 (current):** Change remains incomplete. Female LFPR (42.77%) is still half the male rate (80%). Women are concentrated in low-quality informal employment (50.6% own-account workers). Yet the trajectory is distinct: change occurred through millions of small renegotiations, not legal or political revolution. As Kabeer puts it: "Bangladesh's social progress was achieved not despite patriarchy, but through its gradual renegotiation."

**Exam Tip:** Use the terms "patriarchal bargains", "adaptive preferences", and "incremental renegotiation" to show theoretical depth. The spectrum of agency is a framework that can be applied to any country context.

> **Professor's Corner:** A distinction-level answer notes the tension between Sen's capabilities approach (which emphasizes expanding what people can do and be) and Bourdieu's habitus (which emphasizes how social structures become internalised). Kabeer synthesises both: women need resources to exercise agency, but their goals are shaped by the patriarchal context they inhabit. Change requires both new resources AND shifts in how women perceive their own possibilities.
