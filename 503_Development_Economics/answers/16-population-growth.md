### Question 16 {#q-batch47-dev-09}

**Why did the world population grow so fast after the industrial revolution? What are the key components in the nexus of population growth and economic development? (7+7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Mortality decline — causes and timing (3) | Fertility lag — demographic transition theory (3) | Role of industrial revolution in reducing death rates (2) | Key nexus components: dependency ratio, human capital, Malthus vs Boserup (4) | Policy implications (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Classical population-economics debate. Connects to themes of demographic dividend and labour supply.

**(A) Why Population Grew Fast After the Industrial Revolution**

World population was roughly stable at under 1 billion for most of human history until around 1800. It reached 1 billion around 1800, 2 billion by 1927, 4 billion by 1974, and 8 billion by 2022. This explosive growth — the "population explosion" — was caused by a **decline in mortality rates** while **fertility rates remained high**, producing rapid natural increase.

**Mechanisms of mortality decline:**

| Factor | How It Reduced Deaths | Timing |
|--------|----------------------|--------|
| **Improved nutrition** | Agricultural revolution (crop rotation, new world crops) increased food supply; fewer famine deaths | 1750–1850 (Europe) |
| **Public health** | Clean water, sanitation, sewage systems reduced infectious disease | 1850–1900 |
| **Medical advances** | Vaccination (smallpox), antibiotics (penicillin 1928), antiseptics | 1880–1950 |
| **Transport and trade** | Rail and steamship allowed food distribution to famine-prone areas | 1850+ |

**The demographic transition theory** explains the sequence: pre-industrial societies have high birth rates and high death rates (stable population). Industrialisation reduces death rates first (nutrition, public health, medicine) while birth rates remain high (cultural lag — children remain valued for labour, old-age security, and due to limited contraception). This creates a **demographic gap** between falling deaths and persistent births, producing rapid population growth. Eventually, birth rates also fall (urbanisation, female education, declining child mortality reduces demand for children, contraception availability), and population stabilises at low birth/death rates.

**Why this mattered for development:** The rapid population growth initially appeared threatening (Malthusian fears) but eventually generated the "demographic dividend" — a period when the working-age population grows faster than total population, freeing resources for investment and growth.

**(B) Key Components in the Population-Development Nexus**

| Component | Mechanism | Development Implication |
|-----------|-----------|------------------------|
| **Demographic dividend** | Falling fertility → fewer dependents per worker → more savings and investment per capita | East Asian "miracle" — one-third of growth attributed to demographics (Bloom & Williamson) |
| **Human capital** | Slower population growth → more investment per child → healthier, more educated workforce | Quality-quantity trade-off (Becker) — smaller families invest more per child |
| **Dependency ratio** | Ratio of non-working (0-14, 65+) to working-age (15-64) population | High dependency → consumption burden; low dependency → window for growth |
| **Labour supply** | Population growth → expanding labour force | Can be dividend (if productive employment exists) or liability (if surplus labour without jobs) |
| **Resource pressure** | Malthus: population grows geometrically, food arithmetically | Boserup counter: population pressure drives innovation in agricultural techniques |

**Two key theorists:**

**Malthus (1798):** Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio (1, 2, 4, 8...) while subsistence increases in an arithmetical ratio (1, 2, 3, 4...). Therefore, population growth inevitably exceeds food supply, leading to famine, disease, and war as "positive checks." This prediction failed historically because technological change (the industrial and agricultural revolutions) increased food supply faster than population grew.

**Boserup (1965):** Reversed Malthus. Population pressure is the **driver** of agricultural innovation, not a constraint. As population density increases, farmers intensify land use (shorter fallow periods, irrigation, fertiliser). Evidence from African agricultural intensification supports Boserup's thesis.

**Contemporary relevance:** Bangladesh has experienced a dramatic fertility decline from 6.3 (1975) to 2.2 (2011) — one of the fastest in developing-country history. This created a demographic dividend as the working-age share rose. However, absorbing the growing labour force into productive employment remains the central challenge — reflected in high youth unemployment and informal employment (50.6% own-account workers).

**Exam Tip:** For the first 7 marks, focus on the mortality decline mechanisms and demographic transition theory. For the second 7 marks, organise around the five components and the Malthus-Boserup debate. Use Bangladesh fertility data as your empirical anchor.

> **Professor's Corner:** The demographic dividend is not automatic — it requires policies that create productive employment for the expanding workforce. East Asia succeeded because labour-intensive manufacturing exports absorbed the demographic boom. Bangladesh has the demographic conditions for a dividend but the labour absorption challenge (RMG alone cannot employ everyone) limits its realisation. The difference between a "demographic dividend" and a "demographic disaster" is the quality of economic policy and the structure of labour demand.
