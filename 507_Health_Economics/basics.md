## What Is Health Economics About?

Health economics studies how scarce resources are allocated to and within the health care sector. It answers: *Why is medical care different from other goods? How do people decide when and how much care to seek? How should society choose which health interventions to fund?*

Unlike standard markets, health care is characterised by uncertainty, asymmetric information, and the fact that demand can be unpredictable — you don't choose to get sick. This makes health economics a distinct field with its own analytical tools.

---

## Key Definitions

| Term | Simple Explanation |
|------|--------------------|
| **Health economics** | The study of how economic principles apply to health and health care |
| **Production function of health** | How inputs (medical care, lifestyle, environment) combine to produce health outcomes |
| **Grossman model** | A model where health is both a consumption good (feeling well) and an investment good (more healthy days to work/earn) |
| **Optimal health stock** | The level of health where the marginal benefit of another healthy day equals its marginal cost |
| **Supplier Induced Demand (SID)** | When providers recommend more care than medically necessary because they benefit financially |
| **Lemons Principle** | In markets with asymmetric information, bad quality drives out good quality |
| **Asymmetric information** | One party knows more than the other — patients know their symptoms, insurers know the pool |
| **Coinsurance** | The percentage of costs the patient pays out-of-pocket |
| **CEA** | Cost-Effectiveness Analysis — compares costs and health outcomes in natural units (e.g., cost per life saved) |
| **CBA** | Cost-Benefit Analysis — compares costs and benefits in monetary units |
| **CUA** | Cost-Utility Analysis — compares costs per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained |
| **Adverse selection** | When high-risk individuals are more likely to buy insurance, driving up premiums |
| **Clinical Decision Analysis (CDA)** | A systematic approach to making medical decisions under uncertainty using probabilities and utilities |
| **Bayesian theorem** | A formula for updating probabilities as new evidence becomes available |
| **EQ-5D** | A standardised instrument for measuring health-related quality of life across 5 dimensions |
| **Value of life** | Monetary estimates of what society is willing to pay to reduce mortality risk |
| **Triage** | The process of prioritising patients in emergency departments based on severity of condition |

## The Central Question: How Do We Allocate Scarce Resources in Health Care?

Health care markets fail in ways that standard markets do not. The central question is how to manage these failures:

### The Three Health Care Challenges

1. **Asymmetric Information** — Patients don't know what care they need (provider knows more); insurers don't know patients' risk (patient knows more). This creates SID (on the provider side) and adverse selection (on the insurance side).

2. **Uncertainty & Insurance** — Illness is unpredictable. Risk-averse individuals want insurance, but insurance markets face adverse selection and moral hazard. The tax system influences how and whether people buy insurance.

3. **Cost Containment vs Access** — Health care costs rise faster than GDP in most countries. Public instruments (regulation, subsidies, public provision) try to balance cost, access, and quality.

### What Makes Medical Care Different?

```
Medical care is:
- Uncertain in demand (you don't plan to get sick)
- Asymmetric in information (doctor knows more than patient)
- Subject to supplier-induced demand
- Often subsidised or publicly provided
- Characterised by third-party payment (insurance)
```

## The Math You Need

| Tool | What It Does |
|------|-------------|
| **Bayesian theorem** | P(D|T) = P(T|D) × P(D) / P(T) — update disease probability given test results (CDA) |
| **Expected utility** | Multiply each outcome's utility by its probability, sum them — used in clinical decision trees |
| **Cost-effectiveness ratio** | C/E — compare cost per unit of health outcome across interventions |
| **Incremental C/E ratio (ICER)** | (C₁ − C₀) / (E₁ − E₀) — cost per additional unit of health gained by a new intervention |
| **TTO utility** | U = x/t — in Time Trade-Off, utility equals the fraction of remaining life one would trade for perfect health |
| **SG utility** | U = 1 − p — in Standard Gamble, utility equals the probability of perfect health at which the patient is indifferent |
| **QALYs** | Years of life × utility weight — comparable health outcome metric across interventions |
