### Question 1 {#q-food-ag-01}

**Why is demand for meat rising in developing countries? (4 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [4 marks]:** Income elasticity of meat demand (1) | Urbanization and dietary change (1) | Structural shift explanation (1) | Global food-feed competition context (1)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Establishes the food-feed contradiction (Patnaik). Links income distribution to food security outcomes.

Demand for meat is rising in developing countries due to three interconnected drivers. **First, rising per capita income is the primary factor.** Meat products have a high income elasticity of demand — Utsa Patnaik calculates that a 10% rise in income in India corresponds to a 14-16% increase in meat demand. As GDP grows at 6-8% annually in countries like India and China, meat consumption escalates rapidly. **Second, urbanization transforms dietary patterns.** Urban populations adopt more protein-rich, processed, and meat-intensive diets as a marker of rising living standards, moving away from traditional grain-based staples. **Third, the global demonstration effect spreads Western consumption norms** through media, food retail expansion, and the restaurant industry, accelerating the shift toward animal-based protein.

The consequence is a structural tension: rising meat demand in the global South, combined with already-high per capita meat consumption in the North (US: ~900 kg grain-equivalent per person vs ~130 kg in poor countries), intensifies the food-feed competition that Patnaik identifies as the first of three structural contradictions driving the global food crisis.

**Exam Tip:** Connect immediately to Patnaik's food-feed contradiction. The 4-mark limit means state the three drivers concisely and end with the implication — more land diverted to feed grain.

> **Professor's Corner:** The strongest answers go beyond "people are richer" to explain the **inefficiency** — 7 kg of grain and 15,000 litres of water produce 1 kg of beef (95% calorie loss). That frames meat demand as a distributional problem, not just a consumption preference.
>

### Question 2 {#q-food-ag-02}

**Excessive buffalo production has a negative impact on agricultural land and food production in India. Explain. (6 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [6 marks]:** Land-use competition mechanism (2) | Feed grain conversion data (2) | India's paradox — growth with rural calorie decline (2)

The rise of buffalo production in India illustrates the land-use conflict at the heart of Patnaik's food-feed contradiction. **Buffalo meat (carabeef) production for export has expanded rapidly** as India has become the world's largest buffalo meat exporter. This creates a cascade of negative effects on agricultural land and food production.

**First, land diversion from food to feed crops.** Buffalo rearing requires substantial fodder and feed grain. As commercial buffalo operations scale up, land once used for growing food staples (rice, wheat, pulses) shifts to cultivating animal feed, directly reducing the supply of food for human consumption. **Second, the conversion inefficiency is extreme** — it takes approximately 7 kg of grain to produce 1 kg of animal protein. This represents a 95% calorie loss: the grain that could directly feed people is processed through animals with massive thermodynamic inefficiency. **Third, India's paradox** illustrates the human cost. Despite 8% GDP growth, the average Indian family in 2005 consumed 110 kg less grain per year than in 1991. NSS data shows calorie intake declining for 80% of the rural population. Animal product demand is growing at 9% annually, but the benefits of meat production accrue to exporters and large producers while small farmers lose access to land and grain.

The buffalo meat boom thus represents a regressive transfer of agricultural resources — from producing staple foods for India's poor to producing luxury protein for export markets. This mirrors the colonial-era dynamic of India exporting wheat while its people starved, as documented by **Mike Davis** in *Late Victorian Holocausts*.

**Exam Tip:** Use specific data — 110 kg less grain per family, 95% calorie loss, 9% annual animal product growth. Show you understand the mechanism, not just the outcome.

> **Professor's Corner:** Advanced answers note that India's buffalo export boom is driven by the same IMF/WB policy framework that pushed export-oriented agriculture. The state subsidizes feed grain production while dismantling public food distribution — a policy choice that prioritizes foreign exchange earnings over domestic food security.
>

### Question 3 {#q-food-ag-03}

**Bangladesh has seen a rise in Napier grass production in the last decade. How do you assess this in the context of land use and food security? (5 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [5 marks]:** Napier grass as feed crop — land-use substitution (2) | Food security assessment (2) | Link to broader cash-crop pattern (1)

The rise of Napier grass (fodder for cattle) in Bangladesh represents a subtle but significant shift in land use that mirrors the global food-feed competition at a local scale. **Napier grass cultivation diverts agricultural land from direct food production** (rice, vegetables) to animal feed, reducing the land base available for feeding Bangladesh's 160+ million people.

**The food security implications are concerning.** Bangladesh already faces a nutrition deficit — milk consumption is 42 ml/person/day against the FAO recommendation of 280 ml, and 48% of children suffer chronic undernutrition. Land devoted to Napier grass for dairy feed must be weighed against the opportunity cost in terms of staple food production. However, the assessment requires nuance: if Napier grass enables domestic dairy production that replaces imported milk powder, it could improve nutrition and reduce import dependency. **The critical question is scale** — small-scale Napier grass integrated into mixed farming systems can be beneficial; large-scale conversion of food-growing land to fodder monoculture threatens food security.

**The broader pattern** follows Patnaik's second contradiction (food for domestic consumption vs food for export). When Napier grass production is linked to commercial dairy operations serving urban markets or export-oriented meat production, it reallocates land from subsistence food production to commercial feed, increasing vulnerability for rural households who depend on directly consumed staples.

**Exam Tip:** A balanced answer is best — acknowledge the potential dairy benefits but emphasize the food security risks, especially for a country with 48% child undernutrition.

> **Professor's Corner:** The Napier grass case parallels the India buffalo story at a smaller scale. Watch for the **IMF/WB policy context** — if Bangladesh is pushed toward commercial dairy exports under WTO rules, Napier grass becomes part of a cash-crop pattern rather than a food security strategy.

### Question 4 {#q-food-ag-04}

**WTO's Agreement on Agriculture (AoA) — promise of free trade vs reality. The agreement has negatively impacted developing countries. WTO protects Western interests while jeopardizing the stability of developing countries. Explain. (15 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [15 marks]:** AoA three pillars explained (3) | Promise vs reality — subsidy reduction failure (4) | Dumping and its effects (3) | Selective liberalization (2) | Food security impacts (3) | Alternative policies (bonus)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Central question linking WTO, free trade, food security, and Washington Consensus.

**The Promise.** The WTO Agreement on Agriculture (AoA), signed in 1995 as part of the Uruguay Round, promised developing countries a more level playing field in global agricultural trade. Built on three pillars — **Market Access** (replace non-tariff barriers with tariffs and reduce them), **Export Subsidies** (discipline below-cost export dumping), and **Domestic Subsidies** (cap internal support to farmers) — the AoA was supposed to liberalize agricultural trade, raise world commodity prices for developing country farmers, and enhance food security through trade.

**The Reality.** The promise collapsed on every front:

**1. Subsidies were not reduced — they were reclassified.** The US and EU maintained massive agricultural subsidies by shifting them into the WTO's "Green Box" (supposedly non-trade-distorting). The US set its subsidy limits at already-reached levels rather than reducing them. EU dairy subsidies worth nearly €1 billion went to Arla Foods alone. Studies show decoupled "Green Box" payments nonetheless increase production and lower world prices — making a mockery of the classification system.

**2. Dumping destroyed developing country agriculture.** Western agricultural commodities were exported well below cost of production: cotton at 57% dumping margin, wheat at 40%, corn at 25-30%, rice at 20%. These subsidized exports flooded developing country markets, undercutting local farmers who could not compete with artificially cheap imports. The result: farmers displaced, local food systems dismantled, and import dependency deepened.

**3. Liberalization was selective and hypocritical.** Developing countries were forced to open their agricultural markets, but products where they were competitive (sugar, cotton) remained protected in the West. Only tropical commodities that rich countries do not produce (tea, coffee, cocoa, bananas) were genuinely liberalized. The World Bank's pre-AoA prediction of $250 billion in gains was revised down to $40-60 billion after signing.

**4. Food security deteriorated.** Under IMF and World Bank pressure, developing countries dismantled public food procurement and distribution systems — grain boards, strategic reserves, price supports. Food import bills for Least Developed Countries rose 40% in just the first year after the AoA (1995-96) and were four times higher by 2008. A vicious cycle emerged: cheap imports depressed local prices, reducing domestic production, requiring more imports, deepening dependency.

**5. Market concentration makes a mockery of "free trade."** Three to five multinational firms control 40%+ of global commodity trade in most agricultural sectors — Cargill, Nestlé, Archer Daniels Midland. Nestlé's 2007 profit ($9.7 billion) exceeded the GDP of 65 poorest countries. Only 10-15% of global food is traded internationally, yet the AoA pressured all agriculture to become trade-oriented, overriding local food systems and sovereignty.

**The Washington Consensus connection.** The AoA was the agricultural arm of the broader Washington Consensus program — trade liberalization, deregulation, and export-oriented growth imposed via IMF/WB conditionality. Countries that resisted (China, Vietnam) preserved policy space for food security; those that complied most fully (Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa) saw food dependency and rural poverty deepen.

**Conclusion:** The AoA was not a free trade agreement for agriculture — it was a mechanism for Western agribusiness to access developing country markets while protecting Western farmers. The promise of mutual gain obscured a structure of unequal exchange that continues to disadvantage developing countries.

**Exam Tip:** This 15-mark question is the biggest on the paper. Structure it clearly: promise, reality (4 sub-points), and the Washington Consensus link. Use specific data (57% cotton dumping, €1 billion Arla subsidies, 40% LDC import bill rise).

> **Professor's Corner:** The strongest answers add the **alternatives** — food sovereignty, agroecology (Cuba's 80% input loss to urban gardens feeding 50% of Havana), Fair Trade, Nayakrishi Andolon in Bangladesh (170,000 farm households). These show you understand alternatives, not just the critique.

### Question 5 {#q-export-model-01}

**What are the major critiques of the export-oriented development model? (4 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [4 marks]:** Two major critiques clearly stated with explanation (2 each)

The export-oriented development model — growth driven by producing manufactured goods for Western markets — has been subject to four major critiques from political economy perspectives:

**1. Race to the bottom.** Countries compete to attract foreign investment by lowering labor standards, environmental regulations, and corporate taxes. This creates a downward spiral in working conditions and environmental protection — a "race to the bottom" that benefits multinational capital at the expense of workers and communities.

**2. Enclave economy formation.** Export sectors often operate as isolated enclaves with few backward linkages to the domestic economy. Inputs (raw materials, machinery) are imported, value addition is minimal, and profits are repatriated. The export sector's growth does not transform the broader economy. Bangladesh's RMG sector increasingly exhibits this pattern as raw material imports consume 83-90% of export value.

**3. Volatility and external vulnerability.** Export-oriented economies become dependent on demand conditions in wealthy importing countries. A recession in the US or EU directly impacts production, employment, and growth in the exporting country. The 2008 financial crisis and COVID-19 pandemic both demonstrated this vulnerability — Bangladesh's RMG sector faced $3 billion in cancelled orders during the pandemic.

**4. Dutch disease.** Successful export sectors can cause currency appreciation, making other tradable sectors (agriculture, other manufacturing) uncompetitive. The economy becomes increasingly concentrated in one or two export sectors, reducing diversification and resilience.

**Exam Tip:** For 4 marks, pick two critiques and explain each in 2-3 sentences. Do not try to cover all four — depth over breadth.

> **Professor's Corner:** The dependency theory tradition provides the structural foundation for all four critiques. **Frank** and **Rodney** would see the export-oriented model as a continuation of the colonial division of labor — the periphery still produces for the centre.

### Question 6 {#q-export-model-02}

**Give an example of an export-oriented industry in Bangladesh that altered the land, labor, and environment of a region. (4 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [4 marks]:** Specific industry named (1) | Land impact (1) | Labor impact (1) | Environment impact (1)

**The Ready-Made Garments (RMG) industry in Bangladesh** provides the clearest example of an export-oriented industry that has fundamentally transformed land, labor, and environment in the Dhaka-Chittagong industrial corridor and surrounding regions.

**Land:** Thousands of hectares of agricultural land, particularly in Savar, Gazipur, Narayanganj, and Chittagong, were converted from rice paddy and vegetable farming to factory buildings, worker housing, and supporting infrastructure. This conversion displaced farming communities and reduced local food production capacity. The concentration of factories along the Dhaka-Chittagong highway created an industrial corridor of factories, warehouses, and slums.

**Labor:** The RMG sector employs over 4 million workers, approximately 60% of whom are young women from rural areas. This represents one of the largest internal labor migrations in Bangladesh's history, fundamentally altering family structures, marriage patterns, and gender relations. Women who would have remained in agricultural or domestic work entered formal wage employment, leading to what **Naila Kabeer** documents as a renegotiation of patriarchal bargains. However, labor conditions remain poor — low wages, long hours, lack of unionization, and safety failures (Rana Plaza 2013).

**Environment:** The industry generates massive textile waste, chemical pollution from dyeing and finishing processes, and untreated effluent discharged into rivers. The Buriganga, Turag, and Shitalakshya rivers around Dhaka have become severely polluted from industrial discharge. Groundwater depletion is severe due to intensive water use in textile processing.

**Exam Tip:** Be specific — name locations (Savar, Gazipur), give numbers (4 million workers, 60% women), and mention Rana Plaza. This shows grounded knowledge.

> **Professor's Corner:** The RMG example also fits the **cash crop pattern** — a single export sector dominating the economy with limited backward linkages. Dependency theorists would note that 86% of Bangladesh's export earnings come from one sector controlled by global buyers who capture most of the value.

### Question 7 {#q-export-model-03}

**Draw a parallel from colonial Africa — what does this tell us about the continuation of international economic exploitation? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [7 marks]:** Colonial Africa example with specifics (3) | Parallel to present-day Bangladesh (2) | Theoretical conclusion — continuation of exploitation (2)

**Colonial Africa parallel — cash crop extraction in French West Africa.**
Under French colonial rule, West African peasants were forced to grow cash crops — peanuts in Senegal and Niger, cotton in Mali and Burkina Faso — for export to French industry. The mechanisms were force and taxation: peasants were required to produce set quotas (5-10 kg of cotton per adult in Mali by 1929), land was appropriated for plantations, and traditional food-growing systems were dismantled. Peanuts bought from Senegalese peasants in the 1930s were sold in Britain at 7 times the price paid to producers — a stark example of unequal exchange. Entire regions that had been food self-sufficient became dependent on imported food paid for by cash crop earnings.

**The parallel with Bangladesh today is striking.**
Bangladesh's RMG sector plays the same functional role as colonial cash crops — producing for export to wealthy countries under terms set by foreign buyers. The structural features are identical: land converted from food production to export-oriented use; labor mobilized (often coercively by economic necessity) into export sectors; minimal domestic value addition retained; dependence on distant consumers and buyers for livelihood; and vulnerability to price and demand fluctuations beyond local control. The RMG sector's raw material import bill now consumes 83-90% of export value, meaning Bangladesh retains only 10-17% of the final product value — analogous to the colonial peanut farmer receiving a fraction of the London sale price.

**What this tells us about the continuation of international economic exploitation.**
The parallel demonstrates that the colonial pattern of economic extraction did not end with political independence. Instead, it was reconfigured through new mechanisms: **International Financial Institutions** (IMF/WB structural adjustment, Washington Consensus) replaced colonial administrations as enforcers; **Multinational Corporations** replaced colonial trading companies as the agents of surplus extraction; and **WTO trade rules** replaced colonial decrees as the legal framework enforcing open markets in the periphery while protecting the core.

**Dependency theorists** — **André Gunder Frank**, **Walter Rodney**, and **Samir Amin** — argue that this is not coincidence but the logic of global capitalism: the development of the centre requires the underdevelopment of the periphery. The colonial division of labor (periphery produces raw materials, centre manufactures) has been updated to a new international division of labor (periphery performs low-skill assembly, centre captures design, branding, and distribution value), but the unequal exchange persists — as the cash crop/peanut example and the RMG/garment example both demonstrate.

**Exam Tip:** Make the parallel explicit, not suggestive — "Just as French colonial authorities forced Senegalese peasants to grow peanuts for export at minimal prices, global buyers force Bangladeshi RMG factories to produce garments under conditions that capture only the lowest value-add portion of the supply chain."

> **Professor's Corner:** The **terms of trade deterioration** data strengthens this argument. In 1960, 3 tons of bananas bought 1 tractor; by 1970, it took 11 tons. Similarly, 25 tons of rubber bought 6 tractors in 1960 but only 2 by 1975. The structural mechanism identified by **Prebisch and Singer** in 1950 continues today — primary and low-value producers face declining terms of trade relative to manufactured and knowledge-intensive goods.

### Question 8 {#q-export-model-04}

**UNCTAD report (2013) — developing countries should focus on domestic demand instead of 'western' demand.**
**a) What are the risks of external demand driven industrialization? (4 marks)**
**b) Why emphasize domestic demand? (4 marks)**
**c) What is the role of the state and domestic private investors in expanding domestic demand? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [15 marks]:** Part (a) — two risks clearly explained (4) | Part (b) — two reasons for domestic demand (4) | Part (c) — state role (3), domestic investors (2), evaluation (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — The UNCTAD argument is the most important policy framework in the course. It directly challenges the Washington Consensus and export-led growth model.

**a) Risks of external-demand driven industrialization (4 marks)**

**1. Demand volatility and transmission of external shocks.** When growth depends on Western consumers, a recession in the US or EU directly reduces export orders, employment, and GDP growth. The 2008 financial crisis and COVID-19 pandemic both demonstrated this — Bangladesh's RMG sector lost $3 billion in cancelled orders during the pandemic. Export-dependent economies are passive recipients of shocks generated elsewhere.

**2. A "race to the bottom" in labor and environmental standards.** To remain competitive in export markets, countries suppress wages, weaken labor protections, and relax environmental regulations. This depresses domestic purchasing power (workers cannot afford what they produce) and creates environmental degradation that future generations must bear.

**3. Enclave economy with limited domestic linkages.** Export sectors often import inputs (machinery, raw materials, technology) rather than sourcing locally. Value addition is minimal — Bangladesh's RMG value addition has fallen to 10-17% of export value. The sector operates as an enclave with few backward linkages to the rest of the economy.

**4. Concentration and vulnerability.** Export-oriented economies tend to specialize in a narrow range of products (Bangladesh: 86% of exports from RMG). This concentration makes them vulnerable to sector-specific shocks, technological disruption, and buyer concentration (a few global retailers control market access).

**b) Why emphasize domestic demand? (4 marks)**

**1. Stability and autonomy.** Domestic demand is more stable than export demand because domestic policymakers have instruments (fiscal policy, monetary policy, minimum wage, social protection) to manage it. Growth driven by domestic consumption does not depend on conditions in distant economies that the developing country cannot influence.

**2. Virtuous cycle of wage-led growth.** When workers earn decent wages, they consume goods and services, which creates demand for local production, which generates employment, which further boosts consumption. This contrasts with export-led growth where wages are suppressed to maintain competitiveness, limiting the domestic market.

**3. More inclusive development.** Domestic demand growth can be distributed more broadly across sectors and regions than export-led growth, which typically concentrates in a few industrial zones. It supports small and medium enterprises, agriculture, and services alongside manufacturing.

**4. Reduced vulnerability to terms of trade deterioration.** Export-led commodity producers face declining terms of trade (Prebisch-Singer thesis). Domestic demand-led growth breaks this dependency by producing for local consumers whose purchasing power can be strengthened through policy.

**c) Role of the state and domestic private investors (7 marks)**

**The state has a central and active role:**

**1. Redistributive policies to boost mass purchasing power.** The state must implement progressive taxation, minimum wage laws, social security systems, and public services (health, education) that increase the disposable income of working people. Without redistribution, the domestic market remains too small to drive growth.

**2. Industrial policy to build domestic productive capacity.** The state should provide credit subsidies, infrastructure investment, technical training, and research support to domestic firms. This includes protecting infant industries through tariffs and procurement policies until they achieve competitive scale — the strategy used by the US, Germany, and East Asian industrializers.

**3. Public investment in agriculture and food security.** Given that 45.4% of Bangladesh's workforce is in agriculture (LFS 2022), raising rural incomes is essential for domestic demand expansion. The state must invest in irrigation, storage, cold chains, credit, and extension services.

**4. Regulation of foreign investment and imports.** Unrestricted FDI and imports can destroy domestic productive capacity before it matures. The state must manage the pace and terms of liberalization to preserve space for domestic industry development.

**Domestic private investors must:**

**1. Shift from export-orientation to domestic market development.** The comprador bourgeoisie — the class that profits from mediating between foreign capital and the local economy — must be incentivized to invest in serving domestic consumers rather than external markets.

**2. Invest in backward linkages and value addition.** Instead of importing inputs and performing minimal assembly, domestic firms should invest in producing inputs locally, building brands, and capturing more of the value chain within Bangladesh.

**3. Collaborate with the state in long-term productive investments.** Private capital tends toward short-term, quick-return activities (trade, real estate). The state must create incentives (tax breaks, subsidized credit, public-private partnerships) for long-term investments in manufacturing, technology, and agriculture.

**The UNCTAD framework** fundamentally rejects the Washington Consensus prescription that markets alone will allocate resources efficiently and that export-led growth is the only viable development path. Instead, it calls for a **developmental state** that actively manages trade, investment, and distribution to build a domestically rooted industrial economy.

**Exam Tip:** For the 7-mark part (c), structure around three state roles (redistribution, industrial policy, investment) and three private sector roles (shift to domestic market, backward linkages, long-term investment). Conclude with the UNCTAD-vs-WC contrast.

> **Professor's Corner:** The UNCTAD report was issued in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, which exposed the fragility of export-led growth. **Ha-Joon Chang**'s work on industrial policy provides the historical evidence that all successful developers used state intervention. The key question is political: can the state overcome the interests of the comprador bourgeoisie and export-oriented capital to implement a domestic-demand strategy?

### Question 9 {#q-modernization-01}

**a) What led to the development of Modernization theory back in the 1940s and 50s? (3 marks)**
**b) Examine 4 critiques of Modernization theory. (12 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [15 marks]:** Part (a) — three contextual factors (3) | Part (b) — four critiques with theorists and examples (12)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — The foundational theory of the course. Everything else (dependency, Washington Consensus) responds to or critiques this.

**a) Origins of Modernization theory (3 marks)**

Three factors converged in the 1940s-50s to produce Modernization theory:

**1. Cold War geopolitics.** The US needed a development framework to align newly independent developing nations with capitalism against Soviet communism. **Harry Truman's 1949 inaugural address** — the "bold new programme for making benefits of scientific advances available for underdeveloped areas" — framed development as a Western-led project to win hearts and minds.

**2. The post-WWII international order.** Colonialism was no longer politically acceptable, but Western powers wanted to maintain influence. The language of "development" replaced the language of "civilizing mission." The Marshall Plan's success in rebuilding Europe provided a template: massive capital infusion plus technical assistance could transform backward economies.

**3. Decolonization and the problem of the "Third World."** As Asian and African countries gained independence, Western policymakers and academics asked: how can these "traditional" societies become "modern"? The answer that emerged — the linear stages model — became Modernization theory.

The core assumption: developing countries are poor because they are **traditional and backward**. They must follow the same path Western countries took — a universal, linear process from traditional agrarianism to modern industrial capitalism.

**Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth (1960)** — subtitled *A Non-Communist Manifesto* — codified this into five stages: Traditional Society → Pre-conditions for Take-off → Take-off → Drive to Maturity → Age of Mass Consumption.

**b) Four critiques of Modernization theory (12 marks)**

**Critique 1: Eurocentrism and cultural imperialism.**
Modernization theory assumes Western culture, values, and institutions are universally superior and that non-Western societies must abandon their traditions to develop. This is a deeply ethnocentric worldview that dismisses the histories and knowledge systems of other civilizations. **J. Matunhu** argues the theory demands "deculturalization" — the destruction of local cultures, languages, and social structures as the price of "development." Pre-colonial Africa had powerful empires (Mali, Songhay, Great Zimbabwe) with sophisticated governance, trade networks, and knowledge systems — these were not "traditional" in the pejorative sense MT assumes. **Example:** The theory treats subsistence agriculture as backward, ignoring that it sustained communities for centuries and provided food security that commercial agriculture has not replaced.

**Critique 2: Oversimplified linear thinking.**
Development is not a straight line from stage 1 to stage 5. Countries can move backward due to war, crisis, natural disaster, or policy failure. The linear model cannot explain why Venezuela — once Latin America's wealthiest country — has experienced economic collapse, or why post-colonial African states have struggled despite following MT prescriptions. The theory ignores contingency, path dependence, and the possibility of multiple development trajectories. **Example:** The Washington Consensus applied linear MT thinking in the 1990s — "liberalize and you will develop" — but Latin American countries that followed WC prescriptions saw increased unemployment and inequality, while China (which ignored the prescription) achieved rapid growth.

**Critique 3: Ignores colonial history and the creation of underdevelopment.**
Modernization theory treats underdevelopment as a natural starting point — countries are poor because they have not yet modernized. **Dependency theorists** (Frank, Rodney, Wallerstein) argue the opposite: underdevelopment was **created** by colonialism and continues to be reproduced through the global economic system. The West developed **by** underdeveloping the rest — through colonial extraction, slave labor, land appropriation, and unequal trade. Africa was food self-sufficient before colonialism; the continent's current hunger is not the result of "tradition" but of centuries of systematic extraction. **Rodney**'s *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972) documents this process in detail.

**Critique 4: Ignores how the West actually developed.**
Modernization theory presents a sanitized version of Western development that omits colonialism, slavery, genocide, land dispossession, and imperial extraction. Western industrialization was not achieved through internal effort and thrift alone — it was built on centuries of extracting resources from the periphery. **Example:** Britain's industrial revolution was fueled by cotton from slave plantations in the Americas, sugar from the Caribbean, and textiles imported from India that Britain then destroyed India's own textile industry to protect. **Friedrich List** argued in the 19th century that Britain preached free trade to others precisely because it had developed behind high protectionist walls. The US and Germany also used tariffs, subsidies, and state intervention to build their industrial bases — the opposite of the open-market policies MT recommends to developing countries.

**Additional critique (for depth): Top-down approach ignores the poor.**
Development in the MT framework is imposed by elites, foreign experts, and international institutions. The poor are passive recipients, not active participants. This creates a technocratic, anti-democratic development practice that ignores local knowledge, priorities, and agency.

**Exam Tip:** For the 3-mark part (a), list three factors concisely. For the 12-mark part (b), develop each critique as a full paragraph with named theorists (Matunhu, Frank, Rodney, List) and specific examples. The best answers show how each critique connects to the next — forming a cumulative case against MT.

> **Professor's Corner:** The strongest answers note that MT was **not** a neutral academic theory — it was a Cold War weapon. Rostow's subtitle *A Non-Communist Manifesto* made this explicit. The theory was designed to counter Marxist narratives of imperialism and to provide a capitalist alternative to Soviet development models. Understanding this geopolitical function deepens the analysis of all four critiques.

### Question 10 {#q-washington-consensus-01}

**What is the Washington Consensus? What policies did the WB/IMF prescribe to developing countries? Discuss the underlying motive and impact of: a) Fiscal Discipline, b) Removal of trade barriers, c) Privatization of state-owned industries. (14 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Definition and context (2) | Fiscal discipline — motive and impact (4) | Trade liberalization — motive and impact (4) | Privatization — motive and impact (4)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — The most important policy framework of the last 40 years. Connects to every other topic.

**Definition and Context.**

The **Washington Consensus** (WC) is a set of market-oriented policy prescriptions promoted by the IMF, World Bank, and US Treasury — the "Washington" institutions — for developing countries from the 1980s onward. The term was coined by **John Williamson (1990)** to describe what Washington technocrats already agreed upon. "Washington" covers both political Washington (Congress, administration) and technocratic Washington (IMF, World Bank, US Treasury, Federal Reserve, think tanks). The WC emerged from the debt crisis of 1982 (triggered by Volcker's 1979 interest rate hike) and became the dominant development framework of the post-Cold War era. **Babb & Kentikelenis** argue the WC was not just a set of ideas but a **coercive mechanism** driven by US power, Wall Street interests, and IFI loan conditionality.

The full WC includes 10 policy prescriptions: (1) fiscal discipline, (2) redirect public spending, (3) tax reform, (4) market interest rates, (5) competitive exchange rates, (6) trade liberalization, (7) FDI liberalization, (8) privatization, (9) deregulation, (10) secure property rights.

**a) Fiscal Discipline — Motive and Impact (6 marks component)**

**Underlying motive:** The WC diagnosis was that developing countries' deficits caused inflation, balance-of-payments crises, and discouraged private investment. Fiscal discipline — keeping budget deficits below 1-2% of GNP — was presented as necessary for macroeconomic stability and investor confidence.

**Impact:** Austerity programs required cuts to public spending on subsidies, social services, health, education, and infrastructure. In many countries, this fell hardest on the poor. Public food distribution systems were dismantled, school fees introduced, and health services privatized. The IMF's own evaluation found that fiscal conditionality often worsened poverty and inequality without achieving sustained fiscal stability. **Example:** In Bangladesh, dairy subsidies were eliminated in 1996 under WC-consistent pressure, leaving 1.4 million small dairy farms without support while EU-subsidized imports flooded the market.

**b) Removal of Trade Barriers — Motive and Impact (4 marks component)**

**Underlying motive:** Trade liberalization was prescribed to eliminate the "anti-export bias" of protectionist import-substitution policies. Import tariffs, quotas, and non-tariff barriers were to be removed to expose domestic industry to competition, which would force efficiency gains and allow comparative advantage to guide resource allocation.

**Impact:** Tariffs collapsed across the developing world (Latin America: average tariffs from 42% to under 10%). The result was often deindustrialization rather than efficiency. Local producers could not compete with imports from subsidized Western agriculture and established East Asian manufacturers. **Selective liberalization:** Western countries kept their protection for sensitive sectors (sugar, cotton, dairy) while demanding openness in developing countries. The WTO's AoA institutionalized this asymmetry. Bangladesh's dairy tariffs fell from 75% in 2007 to a proposed 5%, while EU dairy subsidies continued. Bangladesh's average tariff (27%) remains higher than WC prescriptions recommend, but even this limited protection is under pressure.

**c) Privatization of State-Owned Industries — Motive and Impact (4 marks component)**

**Underlying motive:** State-owned enterprises (SOEs) were deemed inefficient, loss-making, and prone to political interference. Privatization was prescribed to improve efficiency through market discipline, generate revenue for the state, and reduce fiscal burdens. Foreign ownership was seen as beneficial — bringing capital, technology, and management expertise.

**Impact:** Thousands of SOEs were sold, often at fire-sale prices and frequently to foreign investors. The results were mixed to negative: employment fell sharply, services deteriorated in some sectors (water, electricity), and assets were transferred abroad. Privatization without effective regulation often replaced public monopoly with private monopoly. **Example:** Milk Vita — Bangladesh's state-supported dairy processor — once procured 15 million litres of milk from small farmers. After EU import competition devastated local dairy, Milk Vita's procurement fell below 3 million litres. Privatization proponents would have sold it; the result would have ended any institutional support for small dairy farmers.

**Broader assessment.** The countries that most fully adopted WC reforms (Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa) failed to achieve the promised growth. Countries that selectively ignored WC prescriptions (China, Vietnam, India) achieved rapid growth. By 2006, **Dani Rodrik** declared "Nobody really believes in the Washington Consensus anymore." However, **Babb & Kentikelenis** argue the WC did not die — it went "undercover." The IMF continues to promote structural reforms in crisis lending (Eurozone bailouts), and the World Bank uses Ease of Doing Business rankings to push similar policies. Reforms already enacted are difficult to reverse.

**Exam Tip:** For the three policies, always give the stated motive AND the actual impact. Show awareness that the impact often contradicted the motive. Use country examples — Argentina (fiscal), Ghana (trade liberalization), Bolivia (privatization of water).

> **Professor's Corner:** The deepest analysis distinguishes between **Williamson's original moderate version** (which included a role for the state) and the **radical implementation** by IMF/WB. Williamson himself criticized the "over-extension" of his consensus. This nuance — that even the architect opposed the actual application — is a sophisticated exam point.

### Question 11 {#q-dependency-01}

**What is Dependency Theory / World Systems Theory? What are its origins and two main arguments? What are two critiques? (14 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Origins (2) | Two main arguments with theorists (6 each = 12 total... adjust: origins 2 + arguments 6 + critiques 6)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — The direct counter-argument to Modernization theory. Essential for any question about development, underdevelopment, and global inequality.

**Origins (2 marks).**

Dependency Theory emerged in the 1950s-60s as a direct reaction to Modernization theory. It was rooted in **Marxist analysis of capitalism and imperialism** and developed primarily by Latin American and Caribbean scholars studying why decades of "development" had not reduced poverty or transformed their economies. Key foundations include:

- **The Prebisch-Singer thesis (1950):** **Raúl Prebisch** and **Hans Singer** independently showed that the terms of trade for primary commodity exporters decline over time relative to manufactured goods. This refuted the classical trade theory assumption that trade benefits all participants equally.
- **André Gunder Frank**'s *The Development of Underdevelopment* (1967) formulated the core thesis: underdevelopment is not an original state but a product of capitalism's global expansion. **Frank**: "Underdevelopment is a result of economic capture and control of backward regions by advanced metropolitan capitalism."
- **Immanuel Wallerstein**'s **World Systems Theory** expanded this into a global framework: a single capitalist world-economy divided into core, semi-periphery, and periphery zones.
- **Walter Rodney**'s *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972) provided the historical documentation — showing how colonialism systematically extracted African wealth, destroyed food self-sufficiency, and created structures of dependency that persisted after independence.

**Two main arguments (6 marks).**

**Argument 1: Centre-Periphery Exploitation (Unequal Exchange).**
The global economy is divided into a **Centre (core)** — wealthy, industrialized countries — and a **Periphery** — poor, commodity-exporting countries. The core controls technology, capital, markets, and military power; the periphery provides raw materials, cheap labor, and markets for finished goods. The relationship is not mutually beneficial but exploitative: the periphery sells primary commodities at prices that decline relative to manufactured goods (declining terms of trade), while buying expensive manufactured imports. The result is a net transfer of surplus from periphery to core — **unequal exchange**.

**Frank**: "The development of the metropolis WAS the underdevelopment of the periphery." **Evidence:** in 1960, 3 tons of bananas bought 1 tractor; by 1970, 11 tons were needed. In 1960, 25 tons of rubber bought 6 tractors; by 1975, only 2 tractors. **Lappé (1977)** calculated that Africa's total loss of foreign exchange due to falling commodity prices exceeded all foreign funds invested, loaned, or granted to the continent.

**Argument 2: The "Development of Underdevelopment."**
Capitalist development in the core is not separate from underdevelopment in the periphery — they are two sides of the same process. The wealth of the North was built through centuries of extraction from the South: slavery, colonization, land appropriation, unequal trade, and more recently, debt, conditional aid, and WTO rules. Underdevelopment is **actively created and maintained** by the global capitalist system, not a natural condition that poor countries can grow out of by following the right policies.

**Wallerstein**'s World Systems Theory adds the **semi-periphery** — countries that exploit the periphery while being exploited by the core — and argues the system is cyclical, with core countries rising and falling over time. But the structural inequality of the world-economy is a permanent feature of capitalism, not a transitional stage.

**Two critiques (6 marks).**

**Critique 1: Overly deterministic and pessimistic.**
Dependency theory leaves little room for agency — poor countries are portrayed as passive victims of global capitalism with no ability to change their position. It cannot explain why some developing countries have achieved rapid growth and industrialization — the **East Asian miracles** (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore) and more recently, China and Vietnam. These countries were once peripheral but successfully transformed their economies through strategic integration into global markets combined with active state industrial policy. Dependency theory's prediction that the periphery cannot develop under capitalism has been empirically disproven.

**Response from dependency theorists:** East Asian success was achieved through **selective** integration and **active state intervention** — the opposite of the neoliberal policies the Washington Consensus later prescribed. These states protected infant industries, controlled capital flows, and managed trade — strategies dependency theory would support. Their success does not refute dependency; it confirms that breaking dependency requires state-led industrial policy, not free markets.

**Critique 2: Fails to specify mechanisms of transformation.**
Dependency theory is better at critiquing the existing system than at proposing realistic alternatives. **Frank** argued that only socialist revolution could break dependency — a prescription that has proven unrealistic in most contexts. The theory offers little guidance for practical policymaking: how should a developing country balance integration with protection? How can it attract foreign investment without being dominated by it? What mix of domestic and export orientation is optimal?

**Response:** Dependency theory's value is as a **diagnostic framework**, not a policy manual. It explains why free trade and market liberalization (the WC prescriptions) fail: because they operate within a rigged system. Policy recommendations emerge from the critique — food sovereignty, domestic demand-led growth, South-South cooperation, state industrial policy — though dependency theorists disagree on specifics.

**Exam Tip:** Always name theorists: **Prebisch-Singer** for terms of trade, **Frank** for development of underdevelopment, **Rodney** for Africa, **Wallerstein** for World Systems. For critiques, mention **East Asia** as the empirical challenge and **policy prescription gap** as the theoretical limitation.

> **Professor's Corner:** The most sophisticated answers note the **analytical tension** between dependency theory and the East Asian experience. Did East Asia develop because it followed the market (neoliberal reading) or despite the market (state-led reading)? The evidence — massive state intervention in Korea and Taiwan, protectionist walls around China — supports the latter. This means dependency's core insight (the system is rigged against late developers) holds, but its pessimism about the possibility of transformation was excessive.
>

### Question 12 {#q-batch47-pol-01}

**a) How can international negotiation help to avoid a trade war? (3 marks)**
**b) Why does the GATT-WTO system generally try to prevent non-tariff intervention in trade? How does the WTO differ from the GATT? (5 marks)**
**c) How have the roles of the IMF and the World Bank, and the relationship between these two institutions, changed as a result of foreign debt and financial crisis? (6 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Part (a) — negotiation mechanisms (3) | Part (b) — non-tariff barriers rationale (2) + GATT vs WTO differences (3) | Part (c) — IMF role change (2), World Bank role change (2), relationship change (2)

**a) How international negotiation helps avoid trade war (3 marks)**

International negotiation prevents trade wars through several mechanisms:

**1. Rule-based dispute resolution.** The WTO provides a binding dispute settlement mechanism (DSM) — the closest thing to an international court for trade. Countries bring grievances to the WTO rather than retaliating unilaterally, preventing escalation. The losing party must bring its policies into compliance or face authorized retaliation.

**2. Transparency and monitoring.** The WTO's Trade Policy Review Mechanism subjects all members to regular peer review of their trade policies. This transparency reduces the informational asymmetries and misunderstandings that can trigger retaliatory measures.

**3. Reciprocity and multilateralism.** Trade negotiations bundle multiple concessions across many countries and sectors, making it costly for any single country to defect. The MFN (Most Favored Nation) principle extends concessions to all members, reducing the incentive for bilateral trade conflicts.

**b) GATT vs WTO and non-tariff intervention (5 marks)**

**Why the GATT-WTO system prevents non-tariff intervention:** Non-tariff barriers (NTBs) — quotas, licensing requirements, technical standards, sanitary measures — are harder to monitor, quantify, and negotiate than tariffs. They can be used to achieve the same protectionist effect as tariffs while appearing legitimate (as health, safety, or environmental regulations). The GATT/WTO system prefers **tariffication** — converting all NTBs into tariffs — because tariffs are transparent, non-discriminatory (MFN principle), and can be progressively reduced through negotiation.

**Key differences between GATT and WTO:**

| Feature | GATT (1947-1994) | WTO (1995-present) |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Legal status** | Provisional agreement, never ratified | Formal international organization |
| **Dispute resolution** | Weak — required consensus to adopt rulings (losing party could block) | Binding — rulings automatically adopted unless consensus against (reverse consensus) |
| **Scope** | Goods only | Goods (GATT) + Services (GATS) + Intellectual Property (TRIPS) |
| **Membership** | ~120 countries by 1994 | 164 members (virtually universal) |
| **Enforcement** | Limited | Authorized retaliation for non-compliance |

The WTO's stronger enforcement mechanism makes it more effective at preventing trade wars, but the expanded scope (TRIPS, GATS) has been criticized for extending WTO rules into areas that restrict developing country policy space (e.g., patent protection for pharmaceuticals, liberalization of services).

**c) Changing roles of IMF and World Bank post-debt crisis (6 marks)**

**Before the 1980s debt crisis:**
- **IMF:** Short-term balance of payments support, exchange rate stabilization, surveillance. Limited conditionality — focused on macroeconomic adjustment.
- **World Bank:** Long-term development project lending (infrastructure, agriculture, education). "Project finance" model.
- **Relationship:** Distinct mandates — IMF for stability, World Bank for development. Limited overlap.

**After the 1982 debt crisis (triggered by Volcker's interest rate hike):**
- **IMF expanded dramatically.** It became the gatekeeper for all developing country borrowing — no country could access private finance or bilateral aid without IMF approval. Conditionality expanded from macroeconomic targets to include structural reforms: privatization, trade liberalization, deregulation. The IMF effectively became an enforcer of the Washington Consensus.
- **World Bank shifted from projects to policy.** Structural Adjustment Loans (SALs) replaced project finance as the main lending instrument. By the late 1980s, roughly 30% of World Bank disbursements were for adjustment lending — loans conditioned on policy reform. The Bank adopted the IMF's macro-conditionality framework and added its own sectoral conditions.
- **The relationship converged.** The two institutions operated increasingly as a unified policy apparatus — the IMF set the macro framework (fiscal discipline, exchange rate), and the World Bank enforced sectoral reforms (agriculture liberalization, privatization, deregulation). Cross-conditionality emerged: the World Bank required IMF program approval as a condition for its loans, and bilateral donors required compliance with both.

**The 2008 financial crisis** further shifted the IMF's role. It expanded lending (tripling its resources) but also became more flexible — creating new lending instruments with less conditionality (Flexible Credit Line). Critics argue this flexibility was applied selectively: Eurozone countries received large loans with less stringent conditions than developing countries had faced.

**Exam Tip:** For part (c), structure around three periods: pre-1982 (distinct roles), 1982-2000s (convergence around WC), post-2008 (continued convergence with some flexibility). Use specific mechanisms: cross-conditionality, Structural Adjustment, policy conditionality.

> **Professor's Corner:** The key theoretical point is that the post-debt crisis transformation **merged** the roles of IMF and World Bank into a single disciplinary apparatus for enforcing neoliberal reform on developing countries. This is exactly what dependency theorists identify as the mechanism of neocolonial control — political independence without economic sovereignty.

### Question 13 {#q-batch47-pol-02}

**What are the critiques of the export-oriented model? Use Bangladesh RMG and colonial Africa as examples. (14 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Two main critiques (4 each) | Bangladesh RMG example (3) | Colonial Africa parallel (3)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Direct application of dependency theory and export model critique to real cases.

**Two major critiques of the export-oriented development model:**

**Critique 1: Race to the Bottom.** Countries competing for foreign investment and export market access suppress wages, weaken labor protections, relax environmental standards, and offer tax holidays. This creates a downward spiral that benefits multinational buyers at the expense of workers, communities, and the environment. The export sector's "competitiveness" is built not on productivity gains but on cost-cutting at the social level.

**Critique 2: Enclave Economies with Minimal Backward Linkages.** Export sectors in developing countries often function as isolated enclaves: raw materials and inputs are imported, minimal processing occurs locally, and finished goods are exported with little connection to the domestic economy. The sector does not generate the structural transformation — creating upstream and downstream industries — that characterizes genuine industrialization. Value addition remains low, and the domestic economy does not benefit beyond the immediate employment effect.

**Bangladesh RMG example (4 marks).**

Bangladesh's Ready-Made Garments sector, employing 4+ million workers (60% women), exemplifies both critiques:

- **Race to the bottom:** Bangladesh's comparative advantage in RMG is built on the lowest labor costs in the industry. Minimum wages are among the world's lowest, unionization is suppressed, and safety standards were catastrophic (Rana Plaza 2013 — 1,134 deaths). The "race to the bottom" analysis predicts exactly this pattern: countries undercut each other on labor standards to attract orders from global buyers.
- **Enclave economy:** The RMG sector is increasingly a cut-and-trade operation. Raw material imports consumed 69% of export value in FY21, rising to 83% in FY22, and over 90% in early FY22. Bangladesh's value addition — the portion of export value created domestically — has fallen to 10-17%. The sector imports fabric, cuts and sews, and exports garments. Backward linkages (textile mills, accessories) remain underdeveloped. The growth of the sector has not transformed the broader industrial structure — 86% of exports still come from RMG, with minimal diversification. **Capital flight** is also suspected: a central banker anonymously flagged possible over-invoicing of raw material imports, which would mean dollars are leaving the country disguised as import payments.

**Colonial Africa parallel (3 marks).**

Under colonial rule, African economies were restructured to serve European industrial needs — forced production of cash crops (peanuts, cotton, cocoa, coffee) for export to the metropole. The structural features were identical to the RMG export model:

- **Land** converted from food production to export crops
- **Labor** mobilized (coercively, via taxation and force) into export production
- **Value** captured at the European processing and distribution stage, not by African producers
- **Dependency** on European markets for both export earnings and food imports
- **Enclave** — the export sector had minimal linkages to the domestic subsistence economy

**Example:** French West African peasants forced to grow cotton and peanuts. Peanuts bought in Senegal in the 1930s were sold in London at **7 times** the price paid to producers. The colonial export sector enriched the metropole, not the colony.

**Continuation of exploitation.** The parallel tells us that the mechanism of extraction has been reconfigured but not abolished. The colonial forms of coercion (direct force, taxation) have been replaced by market mechanisms (WTO rules, IMF conditionality, global supply chain power asymmetries). But the structure remains: developing countries produce low-value goods for wealthy consumers, capture minimal value, and remain dependent on conditions in the core. **Walter Rodney** argued that political independence did not bring economic independence — the export-oriented model perpetuates the colonial division of labor under new institutional forms.

**Exam Tip:** Link the two critiques explicitly to the two examples. RMG = race to the bottom + enclave. Colonial Africa = historical origin of the same pattern. Conclude with Rodney's argument about continuity.

> **Professor's Corner:** The RMG sector's trajectory is deeply concerning. If value addition continues to fall (raw materials already >90% of export value in early 2022), Bangladesh risks becoming a **cutting-and-trading platform** rather than a manufacturing economy — functionally equivalent to colonial cash crop production. LDC graduation in 2026 (loss of GSP preferences) will exacerbate this vulnerability.

### Question 14 {#q-batch47-pol-03}

**What are the economic explanations for MNCs? Examine the OLI paradigm and effects on home and host states. (14 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Economic explanations for MNC existence (4) | OLI paradigm explained (6) | Effects on home and host states (4)

**Economic explanations for Multinational Corporations.**

Traditional trade theory (comparative advantage) explains why countries trade, but not why firms produce in multiple countries. Several economic explanations account for the rise of MNCs:

**1. Market imperfections.** If markets for intermediate goods, technology, or knowledge were perfect, firms could license or contract for these inputs. MNCs arise because these markets are imperfect — transaction costs, information asymmetries, and contracting difficulties make internal production (within the firm) more efficient than arm's-length market transactions.

**2. Intangible assets.** MNCs possess firm-specific intangible assets — brands, patents, technology, managerial expertise — that can be exploited across multiple locations at low marginal cost. **Stephen Hymer (1960)** first argued that MNCs need some ownership advantage to overcome the inherent disadvantages of operating in foreign markets (language, culture, legal systems, distance).

**3. Product life cycle theory. (Raymond Vernon, 1966)** Products go through stages: innovation (produced in home country, high price), maturity (production shifts to other developed countries), and standardization (production moves to developing countries for cost advantages). MNCs evolve to manage this geographical shifting of production.

**4. Internalization theory.** When transaction costs of market-based exchange (finding partners, negotiating contracts, monitoring quality, enforcing intellectual property) are high, firms internalize these transactions within the corporate hierarchy. MNCs are the organizational form this internalization takes across national borders.

**The OLI Paradigm (Dunning, 1977/1988).**

The **OLI Paradigm** (Eclectic Paradigm) synthesizes these explanations. It states that a firm will engage in Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) — becoming an MNC — only when three conditions are simultaneously met:

**O — Ownership Advantage.**
The firm possesses assets that give it a competitive edge over local firms in the host country: proprietary technology, brand reputation, management expertise, economies of scale, access to capital. This advantage must be **transferable** across borders and **exclusive** to the firm. Without O, the firm cannot compete with established local firms who know the market better.

**L — Location Advantage.**
It must be more profitable to exploit the ownership advantage **in a particular foreign location** rather than in the home country. Location advantages include: cheaper labor, natural resources, market size, trade barriers (tariff-jumping FDI), tax incentives, infrastructure, and political stability. Without L, the firm would export or license rather than invest abroad.

**I — Internalization Advantage.**
The firm must benefit from exploiting its ownership advantage **within its own corporate hierarchy** rather than through market transactions (licensing, franchising, joint venture). Internalization avoids: transaction costs, quality control problems, technology leakage, and contractual disputes. Without I, the firm would license the asset to a local firm rather than establishing a foreign subsidiary.

**The OLI framework is a necessary and sufficient condition**: all three must be present for a firm to become an MNC.

**Effects on Host States (developing countries):**

| Potential Benefits | Potential Costs |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| Capital inflow | Profit repatriation (capital outflow) |
| Technology transfer | Inappropriate technology (capital-intensive) |
| Employment creation | Poor working conditions, low wages |
| Export market access | Transfer pricing (tax avoidance) |
| Management skills transfer | Crowding out local firms |
| Tax revenue | Tax holidays and incentives |

**The empirical record is mixed.** East Asian countries (Korea, Taiwan, China) used MNCs strategically — requiring technology transfer, local content, and joint ventures — and captured significant benefits. Latin American and African countries that accepted MNC investment with minimal regulation saw more costs — profit repatriation, tax avoidance, environmental damage, and limited technology transfer.

**Effects on Home States (developed countries):**

| Potential Benefits | Potential Costs |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| Higher returns on capital | Domestic job losses (offshoring) |
| Access to cheaper imports | Deindustrialization |
| Global brand presence | Tax avoidance (profit shifted abroad) |
| | Weakened labor bargaining power |

**Dependency theory perspective:** MNCs are the modern vehicle of surplus extraction. **Samir Amin** argues MNCs perpetuate the core-periphery divide by controlling technology, brands, and distribution channels while locating only low-value assembly in the periphery. Profit repatriation and transfer pricing represent a net transfer of surplus from host to home countries — a continuation of unequal exchange under new institutional forms.

**Exam Tip:** Structure clearly — first explain why MNCs exist (4 reasons), then OLI (name each condition with example), then balance of effects. The critical evaluation at the end (dependency theory lens) adds depth.

> **Professor's Corner:** The strongest answers note that the OLI paradigm is a **positive** theory (explaining why MNCs exist) but not a **normative** one (whether they benefit host countries). The effects depend on the host state's regulatory capacity and bargaining power. States that regulate MNC investment capture benefits; states that compete for FDI by lowering standards lose.

### Question 15 {#q-batch47-pol-04}

**a) Does free trade benefit everyone? Discuss how removal of trade barriers may inflict conflicting interest among different groups of the economy and society. (7 marks)**
**b) Dairy farmers in Bangladesh have poured milk on the street demanding protection. What is your opinion on protectionism in the dairy industry? (7 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Part (a) — distributional effects of free trade (3) + conflicting interest groups (4) | Part (b) — case analysis (7)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — The central policy debate of the course, applied to a specific case study.

**a) Does free trade benefit everyone? (7 marks)**

Standard trade theory (Ricardian comparative advantage, Heckscher-Ohlin) demonstrates that free trade increases total output and welfare for a country as a whole. **However, the benefits are not distributed evenly — there are clear winners and losers within every economy.**

**Conflicting interest groups affected by trade liberalization:**

| Winners from Free Trade | Losers from Free Trade |
|------------------------|----------------------|
| Consumers (lower prices) | Domestic producers competing with imports |
| Export-oriented industries | Import-competing industries |
| Workers in export sectors | Workers in import-competing sectors |
| Owners of abundant factors | Owners of scarce factors |
| Multinational corporations | Small and medium local enterprises |
| Importers and distributors | Agricultural subsistence farmers |

**The Stolper-Samuelson theorem** demonstrates this formally: trade liberalization benefits the owners of a country's abundant factor and harms the owners of its scarce factor. In developing countries (abundant in unskilled labor, scarce in capital), trade liberalization should benefit workers and harm capital owners. In practice, the opposite has often occurred — because labor mobility is limited, safety nets are absent, and capital is more mobile internationally than labor.

**Beyond class conflict, there are other dimensions of conflict:**
- **Urban vs rural:** Trade liberalization often benefits urban consumers and export industries while harming rural agricultural producers.
- **Generational:** Younger, more mobile workers may benefit; older workers with industry-specific skills who cannot retrain are displaced.
- **Regional:** Industrial regions connected to export markets benefit; regions dependent on import-competing industries suffer.
- **Gender:** Women workers may gain manufacturing employment (RMG) but working conditions are often poor and insecure.

**b) Protectionism in the Bangladesh dairy industry (7 marks)**

**Background:** Bangladesh has 1.4 million small dairy farms (2-3 cows each), supporting approximately 7 million people. Milk consumption is 42 ml/person/day — far below the FAO recommendation of 280 ml. 48% of children suffer chronic undernutrition.

**The case for protection:**

**1. Unfair competition, not free trade.** Bangladesh dairy is naturally competitive — production costs are 50% cheaper than in Europe, and the sector creates 350 jobs per million kg of milk compared to 7.6 jobs in the EU (46 times more employment-intensive). The problem is not that Bangladesh dairy is inefficient — it is that EU dairy is **heavily subsidized**. Arla Foods (Danish-Swedish cooperative) has received nearly €1 billion in EU subsidies since 2000, enabling it to export to Bangladesh at 15-31% below cost. This is **dumping**, not free trade.

**2. Infant industry argument.** Bangladesh's dairy industry has potential for growth — demand is estimated at 7.2 million tonnes, while current production is only 2.7 million tonnes. With modest support (tariff protection, subsidies, infrastructure), the industry could meet domestic demand, improve nutrition, and reduce import dependency. **Friedrich List's infant industry argument** — used by the US, Germany, and East Asian industrializers — applies directly: temporary protection until the industry reaches competitive scale.

**3. Food security and poverty.** Protecting dairy supports 7 million people in rural areas where 45.4% of the workforce is in agriculture. If the dairy sector collapses, these farmers have no alternative livelihoods. The social cost of adjustment (mass rural unemployment, poverty, migration to slums) far exceeds the consumer benefit of cheap imported milk powder.

**4. Environmental and sovereignty considerations.** Import-dependent food systems are vulnerable to global price shocks, supply chain disruptions, and the biofuel-driven corn-to-ethanol competition that pushed global food prices in 2007-08. Domestic production is a food security buffer.

**The case against protection:**

**1. Consumer prices.** Tariffs on imported milk powder raise prices for consumers, particularly urban poor and children who rely on fortified milk products. Bangladesh's average tariff of 27% is already high; further increases would disproportionately burden low-income households.

**2. Efficiency and modernization.** Critics argue that protection shelters inefficient producers and delays necessary modernization. If protected indefinitely, the dairy sector may never achieve the productivity levels needed to compete.

**3. Retaliation risk.** WTO rules restrict the tariffs Bangladesh can impose. High dairy protection could trigger complaints from trading partners.

**My position: Strategic protection is justified.**

The Bangladesh dairy case is a textbook infant industry situation: (i) the industry has genuine comparative advantage (50% lower costs, 46x more jobs); (ii) the competition it faces is not free but subsidized (EU dumping at 31% below cost); (iii) the social and food security benefits of domestic production are substantial. A **phased protection strategy** is appropriate — maintain tariffs at 50-75% with a 10-year reduction schedule tied to productivity improvements, combined with investments in cold chain infrastructure, farmer cooperatives, and extension services. **Milk Vita** — the state processor — should be revived as a procurement guarantee for small farmers. This is not permanent protection but strategic time-buying for a sector with proven potential.

**Exam Tip:** For part (b), state your position clearly and justify it with the specific data — 1.4 million farms, 7 million people, 50% cheaper production, 46x more jobs. Show you understand both sides but argue with evidence.

> **Professor's Corner:** The free trade vs protectionism debate cannot be resolved in the abstract — it depends on the specific conditions. The key insight from **List** and **Chang** is that every successful industrializer used protection at some stage. The question is not "should we protect?" but "under what conditions does protection work?" Dairy in Bangladesh meets those conditions: competitive base, large employment, food security, and genuine dumping to correct rather than permanent sheltering.

### Question 16 {#q-batch47-pol-05}

**a) What is Mercantilism? (6 marks)**
**b) Discuss how protectionist policies played a vital role during the initial phase of industrial development of the western world. (8 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [14 marks]:** Part (a) — definition and features (6) | Part (b) — historical evidence (8)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Shows the historical counter-example to free trade orthodoxy. Essential for free trade vs protectionism debate.

**a) What is Mercantilism? (6 marks)**

**Mercantilism was the dominant economic doctrine of European nation-building from the 16th to the 18th centuries.** Its core premise was that national wealth and power were measured by the accumulation of precious metals (gold, silver), and that the state should actively manage trade to achieve a positive balance of exports over imports.

**Key features of mercantilist policy:**

**1. Export maximization, import minimization.** The state promoted exports through subsidies, tax incentives, and quality controls, while restricting imports through tariffs, quotas, and outright bans. A trade surplus meant gold inflows, increasing national wealth.

**2. Protection of domestic industry.** Infant industries were protected by high tariffs and import restrictions until they achieved competitive scale. The Navigation Acts (Britain) reserved colonial trade for British ships and excluded foreign competition.

**3. Colonial extraction.** Colonies existed to provide raw materials to the metropole and serve as captive markets for manufactured goods. Manufactures in colonies were actively suppressed (e.g., Britain destroyed India's textile industry to protect Lancashire).

**4. State-directed economic activity.** Strong central states chartered monopoly trading companies (British East India Company, Dutch VOC), granted patents and monopolies, subsidized strategic industries, and regulated quality and production standards.

**5. Bullionism.** The accumulation of gold and silver was the ultimate objective. Exports were encouraged and imports restricted to ensure a net inflow of precious metals.

**Influence on later economic thought:** **Adam Smith**'s *Wealth of Nations* (1776) was a direct attack on mercantilism, arguing that real wealth is not gold but productive capacity, and that free trade benefits all parties. Classical economics subsequently dismissed mercantilism as a fallacy. **However, modern economic historians (List, Chang, Reinert)** have rehabilitated the insight that **protection played a vital role in industrialization** — even though the bullionist theory was wrong.

**b) Protectionist policies in early Western industrialization (8 marks)**

Contrary to the free trade narrative that the West industrialized through laissez-faire markets, **every major industrial power used extensive protection during its catch-up phase.**

**Britain: The original protectionist turned free trade preacher.**
Britain built its industrial supremacy behind high protectionist walls. The Navigation Acts (1651-1849) excluded foreign shipping from British colonial trade. The Calico Acts (1700-1721) banned imported Indian cotton textiles to protect the nascent British cotton industry. The Corn Laws (1815-1846) protected British agriculture from cheap grain imports. **Only after Britain had achieved industrial supremacy did it adopt free trade** — repealing the Corn Laws in 1846, by which time British industry had nothing to fear from competition. **Friedrich List** noted this hypocrisy: Britain "kicked away the ladder" by which it had climbed to the top, preaching free trade to latecomers to prevent them from following the same path.

**United States: The most protectionist country of the 19th century.**
From the Tariff Act of 1789 through the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930, the US maintained some of the highest tariff rates in the world. **Alexander Hamilton**'s *Report on Manufactures* (1791) provided the intellectual foundation — infant industry protection to build US manufacturing. Average US tariffs remained above 40% through the 19th century, reaching 50% in the 1820s and 49% under the McKinley Tariff of 1890. **Abraham Lincoln**, a protectionist Republican, raised tariffs during the Civil War to 47%. US industry developed behind these walls, becoming competitive globally by the early 20th century — at which point the US became a free trade advocate (post-WWII).

**Germany: Friedrich List and the Zollverein.**
**Friedrich List** (1789-1846), the intellectual father of the infant industry argument, argued that free trade benefits established industrial powers but harms developing countries. In *The National System of Political Economy* (1841), List proposed that Germany — then an agricultural backwater — needed protective tariffs to build its manufacturing base. The Zollverein (German customs union) of 1834 created a protected internal market while maintaining external tariffs. Germany's tariff protection continued under Bismarck, enabling the rapid industrial growth that by 1900 had made Germany Europe's leading industrial power.

**East Asian late industrializers (20th century).**
The pattern continued: South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan all used extensive protection during their industrialization phases. **Ha-Joon Chang**'s *Kicking Away the Ladder* (2002) documents that these countries' tariff levels were **higher** than those of Western Europe and North America at comparable stages of development. South Korea's average manufacturing tariff exceeded 30% through the 1960s-70s; by the 1990s, when Korean industry was globally competitive, tariffs were reduced.

**The central lesson.** The historical record is clear: protectionist policies were a necessary condition for industrial catch-up in every successful case. The West's current advocacy of free trade for developing countries contradicts its own historical practice. **As List argued, free trade between unequals benefits the more advanced partner** — it locks in the existing division of labor and prevents latecomers from building the manufacturing capacity needed for genuine development.

**Exam Tip:** Structure part (b) around four cases — Britain, US, Germany, East Asia. Use List's "kicking away the ladder" metaphor as the unifying thesis. Conclude with the policy implication for contemporary developing countries.

> **Professor's Corner:** This question is the empirical foundation of the UNCTAD/List critique of the Washington Consensus. If every successful industrializer used protection, why are developing countries today told they must liberalize? The answer, in dependency theory terms, is that free trade maintains the existing hierarchy of wealth and power. **Chang**'s work is the best modern synthesis of this argument.

### Question 17 {#q-food-crisis-01}

**Explain Utsa Patnaik's analysis of the global food crisis — what are the three structural contradictions driving the crisis? (10 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [10 marks]:** Core thesis — not population but structure (2) | Food vs feed contradiction (3) | Food vs export contradiction (3) | Food vs fuel contradiction (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Patnaik is the key theorist for food security questions. Her framework connects land use, trade policy, and inequality.

**Core thesis.**

**Utsa Patnaik** argues that the global food crisis (particularly the 2007-08 price spike) was NOT caused by population growth, rising demand in China and India, or climate shocks — the standard Malthusian explanations. Instead, the crisis is driven by **three structural contradictions** built into the global food system under neoliberal capitalism. These contradictions reflect fundamental conflicts over how land and grain are allocated.

**Contradiction 1: Food for humans vs feed for animals.**

Rising global meat consumption diverts massive quantities of grain from direct human consumption to animal feed. The conversion is deeply inefficient:

| Product | Calorie Efficiency | Grain Input |
|---------|-------------------|-------------|
| 1 kg beef | ~95% calorie loss | 7+ kg grain (24,150 cal → 1,140 cal) |
| 1 kg chicken | Significant loss | 2 kg grain |
| 1 litre milk | Moderate loss | 0.2 kg grain |

**The inequality dimension:** Rich countries consume approximately 900 kg of grain per person per year, of which 80% is indirect (animal feed). Poor countries consume approximately 130 kg per person, almost entirely direct (rice, wheat, maize). The average American consumes **7 times more grain-equivalent** than a person in a developing country — but almost all of it is through meat, eggs, and dairy.

As rising incomes in developing countries push up meat demand (India: 10% income rise → 14-16% increase in meat demand; animal product demand growing at 9% annually), the share of global grain going to animal feed increases, reducing the availability of grain for direct human consumption. This is not a Malthusian problem of too many people — it is a distributional problem of increasingly unequal grain allocation between rich meat-eaters and poor grain-eaters.

**Contradiction 2: Food for domestic consumption vs food for export.**

Under IMF/World Bank/WTO pressure, developing countries were pushed to abandon food self-sufficiency and specialize in export-oriented agriculture. Countries that once fed themselves now export high-value crops (flowers, vegetables, shrimp) and import staple foods. This represents a **colonial continuity**: under colonialism, colonies grew cash crops for Europe; today, developing countries follow the same pattern under the rubric of "comparative advantage" and "export-led growth."

**Evidence from India:** Despite 8% GDP growth, the average Indian family consumed 110 kg less grain in 2005 than in 1991. NSS data shows calorie intake declining for 80% of the rural population. India grows enough food, but the food is being diverted — to animal feed, to export, and to high-value non-food crops. **Patnaik's paradox**: economic growth alongside declining food consumption for the majority.

**Contradiction 3: Food vs fuel (biofuels).**

The US government's policy of subsidizing corn-based ethanol for fuel created a direct competition between food and fuel. Between 2002 and 2008, US corn used for ethanol rose from 27 million tons to over 100 million tons — approximately one-third of the entire US maize crop. This massive diversion of grain from food to fuel markets contributed directly to the 2007-08 global food price spike.

**The mechanism:** As US corn was diverted to ethanol, global grain supplies tightened, prices rose, and developing countries that relied on grain imports faced soaring food bills. The food import bills of Least Developed Countries rose 40% in 1995-96 (post-AoA) and were 4 times higher in 2008 than in 2000. The US biofuel mandate effectively transferred hunger from the global poor to US gas tanks — a clear example of how policy choices in the core produce food crises in the periphery.

**Poverty line manipulation — the third dimension of Patnaik's critique.**

Patnaik also exposes how official poverty lines have been manipulated downward to hide the crisis. India's 2005 poverty line of ₹356/month (approximately ₹12/day) is not enough to buy 1 kg of rice. China's 2007 poverty line of ¥2.92/day is below the cost of a kilo of rice (¥4). These are **destitution lines**, not poverty lines. By lowering the poverty standard, governments make it appear that poverty is falling even as hunger is rising.

**Conclusion.** The food crisis is not a natural disaster or an inevitable Malthusian outcome — it is a **policy-driven crisis** caused by three structural contradictions in the global food system, each rooted in the logic of neoliberal capitalism and enforced by international institutions (IMF, World Bank, WTO). Resolving the crisis requires addressing these contradictions, not blaming population growth.

**Exam Tip:** State the core thesis clearly — Patnaik rejects population-based explanations. Then walk through each contradiction with specific data (95% calorie loss, 110 kg less grain, ⅓ of US corn for fuel). End with the poverty line critique for bonus depth.

> **Professor's Corner:** Patnaik's framework connects to every topic in the course. The food-feed contradiction links to rising meat demand (Q1a) and Napier grass (Q1c). The food-export contradiction links to cash crops and WTO AoA. The food-fuel contradiction links to US agricultural policy. The poverty line critique links to LFS data on employment and income. A sophisticated answer shows these connections.

### Question 18 {#q-cash-crops-01}

**Analyze the conflict between cash crops and food crops in developing countries. How does this represent a conflict between dependency and autonomy? (10 marks)**

**Answer:**

> **Marking Guide [10 marks]:** Central thesis — dependency vs autonomy (2) | Colonial origins (2) | Deteriorating terms of trade (2) | Mechanisms of dependency (2) | Policy recommendations (2)
>
> ⭐ **High-Yield** — Brings together dependency theory, food security, and trade policy in a single framework.

**Central thesis.**

The conflict between cash crops (grown for export) and food crops (grown for domestic consumption) is fundamentally a conflict between **dependency** and **autonomy**, as argued by **Darkoh & Ould-Mey** (1992). Cash crop production integrates developing countries into global markets on unequal terms — producing what they do not consume and consuming what they do not produce. Food crop production represents self-reliance — controlling the food supply, insulating the population from global price volatility, and preserving land and labor for local needs.

**Two competing positions:**

| Position | View on Cash Crops |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Left critique** | Cash crops = naked capitalism continuing colonial exploitation of peasants; land, labor, and surplus extracted for metropolitan benefit |
| **Right/neoliberal view** | Cash crops = the only way developing countries can earn foreign exchange needed for health, education, and nutrition; comparative advantage should guide allocation |

Darkoh & Ould-Mey argue that the left position is historically and empirically more accurate for Africa.

**Colonial origins.**

**Pre-colonial Africa was largely food self-sufficient.** Diverse farming systems produced adequate food for local populations. Colonialism forcibly restructured African agriculture for European industrial needs:

- **Force and taxation** compelled peasants to grow cash crops (peanuts, cotton, cocoa, coffee) instead of food
- **Land was appropriated** for plantations and European settler farming
- **Traditional food systems were dismantled** — knowledge, seed varieties, and land management practices were lost
- **Infrastructure (railways, ports)** was built to extract cash crops, not to serve local food systems

**Example — Peanut exploitation in Senegal:** Peanuts bought from West African peasants in the 1930s were sold in Britain at **7 times** the price paid to producers — a markup that is pure surplus extraction.

**Post-colonial continuity:** Independence did not end the cash crop pattern; it often intensified it. **Mali** under French rule required 5-10 kg of cotton per adult in 1929; by 1970, the Malian government forced **48 kg per adult**. Cash crops continued to receive the best land, credit, research, and infrastructure — while food crops were neglected.

**Deteriorating terms of trade.**

The **Prebisch-Singer thesis** demonstrates that primary commodity exporters face declining terms of trade relative to manufactured goods:

- 1960: 3 tons of bananas = 1 tractor. 1970: **11 tons** = 1 tractor.
- 1960: 25 tons of rubber = **6 tractors**. 1975: 25 tons = **2 tractors**.

**Lappé (1977)** calculated that Africa's total foreign exchange loss from falling commodity prices exceeded all foreign funds invested, loaned, or granted to the continent. The more cash crops a country produces, the more it must produce to buy the same imports — a treadmill that deepens dependency.

**Mechanisms of dependency.**

The cash crop economy functions as a "driving belt" connecting African economies to developed countries through several mechanisms:

**1. Unequal exchange.** The gap between what the periphery sells cheap (raw materials) and what it buys expensive (manufactured goods) represents a net transfer of surplus to the core.

**2. Comprador bourgeoisie.** A local business class (import-export traders, plantation owners, state officials) profits from the cash crop system and has an interest in maintaining it. This class blocks policies that would shift resources toward food self-sufficiency.

**3. Debt trap.** Small farmers take loans for seeds, fertilizer, and equipment, and must produce cash crops to repay them. Falling commodity prices mean they must produce more to service the same debt, pushing them further into cash crop specialization and away from food production.

**4. Erosion of subsistence economy.** Cash crop production drains labor from food production, introduces dependence on purchased inputs, and makes households vulnerable to price shocks. When cash crop prices collapse, households cannot fall back on subsistence production because their land, labor, and skills have been redirected.

**5. IMF/WB conditionality.** Structural adjustment programs pushed African countries to eliminate subsidies, dismantle marketing boards, devalue currencies, and open markets — all reinforcing cash crop specialization. Countries that wanted to invest in food self-sufficiency were denied loans or faced punitive conditions.

**Policy conclusions (Darkoh & Ould-Mey's framework).**

Darkoh & Ould-Mey propose eight policy directions to break the dependency cycle:

1. **Reject the export-led growth model** — prioritize food self-sufficiency
2. **Empower peasants** — especially women, who constitute 60-80% of agricultural labor
3. **Increase intra-African trade** — reduce dependence on Northern markets
4. **Adopt coherent national food security policies** — strategic grain reserves, price supports
5. **Pursue collective self-reliance** — sub-regional food security arrangements
6. **Invest in agricultural science and technology** — appropriate for local conditions
7. **Train and resource women farmers** — the primary food producers
8. **Generate political will** — the main obstacle is not resources but political elites tied to the cash crop system

**Conclusion.** The cash crop vs food crop conflict cannot be resolved by more efficient market integration — because the problem is the structure of integration itself. As long as developing countries remain locked into producing low-value commodities for export on deteriorating terms of trade, food security and genuine development will remain elusive. The path to autonomy runs through food self-sufficiency, peasant empowerment, and a break with the neoliberal policy framework imposed by the Washington Consensus.

**Exam Tip:** Use the colonially-structured cash crop system as the historical backdrop. Connect to modern Bangladesh — is RMG a "cash crop" equivalent? Use the dependency-autonomy framing throughout. Cite Darkoh & Ould-Mey as the key theorists.

> **Professor's Corner:** The cash crops question is where the entire course converges: dependency theory (core-periphery), Washington Consensus (IMF/WB policy framework), free trade in agriculture (WTO AoA), food crisis (Patnaik's food-vs-export contradiction), and Bangladesh's RMG (the modern cash crop). A top answer shows these connections explicitly.
