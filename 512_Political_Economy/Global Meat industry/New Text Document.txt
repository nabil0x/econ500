# Origins of the Food Crisis in India and Developing Countries

**Author: Utsa Patnaik (Monthly Review, 2009)** 

---

## INTRODUCTION (Opening paragraphs)

### Paragraph 1: Food crisis after 1991 reforms

* Since **1991**, India adopted **neoliberal economic reforms** (liberalization, privatization, reduced state spending).
* After this, **food output per person declined** and **grain consumption fell**.
* Agriculture entered a **deep depression**, and **unemployment increased**.
* Income shifted **from the poor majority to a rich minority**.
* As a result, many people now **eat less food and wear older clothes**.
* The **global economic slowdown** made this worse by increasing unemployment.

**Core point:**
👉 The food crisis is caused by **policy choices**, not by lack of food alone.

---

### Paragraph 2: Long-term vs short-term causes

* The author separates:

  * **Long-term causes** (structural changes in agriculture, trade, income distribution)
  * **Immediate causes** (price shocks, global recession)
* These problems affect **India and other developing countries**, including **China**.

---

### Paragraph 3: Three major contradictions

The author identifies **three structural contradictions** causing food crisis:

1. **Food for humans vs feed for animals**
2. **Food for domestic consumption vs food for export**
3. **Food vs fuel (biofuels)**

These contradict popular claims that rising food prices are caused simply by population growth or higher demand in China and India.

## SECTION 1: HUMAN FOOD VS ANIMAL FEED COMPETITION

### Paragraph: Medieval Europe comparison

* In medieval Europe, food was scarce because productivity was low.
* People couldn’t grow enough grain for both **humans and livestock**.
* Animals were slaughtered in winter due to lack of feed.

**Purpose:**
👉 Shows that food–feed conflict is historically known.

---

### Paragraph: Colonial period

* Europe depended on **grain and raw materials from colonies**.
* Colonies were forced to export:

  * Sugar, rice, cotton, beverages
* This caused **decline in food availability in colonies**.
* Colonies fed Europe while their own populations became malnourished.

---

### Paragraph: Modern food–feed competition

* Today, developing countries produce **animal feed and meat for rich countries**.
* Grain is converted into meat, which is **highly inefficient**.

#### Key fact (Table 1):

* **1 kg beef gives**:

  * 1,140 calories
  * 226 g protein
* **Grain used to produce that beef gives**:

  * 24,150 calories
  * 700 g protein

👉 Huge **loss of nutrition** during conversion.

---

### Paragraph: Who consumes animal products?

* Demand for meat, milk, eggs is concentrated among the **rich**.
* This diverts grain **away from poor people**.
* Grain is consumed:

  1. **Directly** (rice, wheat, roti)
  2. **Indirectly** (meat, milk, eggs)

---

### Paragraph: Grain conversion ratios

* Advanced countries use industrial livestock systems.
* Examples:

  * 1 liter milk = 0.2 kg grain
  * 1 kg eggs/chicken = 2 kg grain
  * 1 kg beef = **at least 7 kg grain**

**Conclusion:**
👉 Rich diets consume far more grain than poor diets.

---

### Paragraph: Income and grain consumption

* As income rises:

  * Direct grain consumption first rises, then falls
  * Indirect grain consumption (via meat) rises sharply
* Total grain consumption **increases with income**.

---

### Paragraph: U.S. example

* U.S. grain consumption:

  * ~900 kg per person per year
  * **80% is indirect (animal feed)**
* Poor countries:

  * ~130 kg per person per year

👉 Rich countries consume **7 times more grain per person**.

## SECTION 2: WHY THE POOR GO HUNGRY

### Paragraph: Adam Smith’s mistake

* Adam Smith said food demand has a natural limit (stomach size).
* He didn’t anticipate **meat-heavy diets**.
* Rich countries consume far more grain **without eating more food directly**.

---

### Paragraph: Beef example

* One daily beef burger in the U.S. or Brazil:

  * Uses enough grain to feed **three Indians for a day**
* Advanced countries (16% of world population) consume:

  * **40% of global cereals**

---

### Paragraph: Within developing countries

* Middle and upper classes:

  * Capture most grain
  * Consume more meat
* Poor:

  * Lack even basic direct food
  * Depend on forests and fishing (shrinking resources)

---

### Paragraph: India’s growth paradox

* 10% income rise → 14–16% rise in meat demand
* India:

  * GDP growth: ~8%
  * Per capita income growth: ~6%
* Result:

  * Animal product demand ↑ 9% annually
  * Feed grain demand ↑
  * Food available for poor ↓

## SECTION 3: FALLING CEREAL CONSUMPTION DESPITE GROWTH

### Paragraph: Wrong explanation by Western economists

* Many blamed China & India’s rising demand for 2007–08 food inflation.
* This assumes **income distribution stayed equal**.
* That assumption is false.

---

### Paragraph: Reality in India & China

* Government cut rural spending.
* Unemployment rose.
* Costs of health, transport, electricity increased.
* Poor households cut food consumption.

👉 Result: **Average grain consumption fell**, even as rich ate more.

---

### Paragraph: Projection vs reality (India)

* IFPRI projected (for 2004–05):

  * 198–218 million tons cereal demand
* Actual availability:

  * **157 million tons**
* Shortfall:

  * 41–62 million tons

Reason: inequality was ignored in projections.

---

### Paragraph: Food availability explained

* Availability =
  Net output + imports − stock additions
* By 2002:

  * Public grain stocks = **40 million tons excess**
* Despite surplus:

  * Hunger increased due to low purchasing power

---

### Paragraph: Shocking decline in intake

* Average Indian family (2005):

  * Consumed **110 kg less grain per year** than in 1991
* NSS data:

  * Calories ↓
  * Protein intake ↓ for **80% of rural population**
