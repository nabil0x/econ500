## What Is Political Economy About?

Political Economy studies how POLITICS (power, institutions, interests) shapes ECONOMIC outcomes and vice versa. It answers: *Why are some countries rich and others poor? Who wins and who loses from free trade? Do international institutions help or hurt developing countries?*

Unlike pure economics, political economy explicitly considers power relations — between states, between classes, between the Global North and South, and between capital and labor.

## Key Definitions

| Term | Simple Explanation |
|------|--------------------|
| **Political Economy** | The study of how political power and economic forces interact |
| **Modernization Theory** | Development as a linear path from "traditional" to "modern" societies (Rostow's 5 stages) |
| **Dependency Theory** | The rich core keeps the poor periphery underdeveloped through unequal exchange |
| **World Systems Theory** | A single capitalist world-economy with core, semi-periphery, and periphery zones |
| **Washington Consensus** | 1980s neoliberal package: fiscal discipline, privatization, trade liberalization |
| **Mercantilism** | State uses protectionism to build national wealth (exports > imports, bullion accumulation) |
| **Free Trade** | No tariffs or barriers — goods flow where production is cheapest |
| **Protectionism** | Tariffs, quotas, subsidies to shield domestic industries from foreign competition |
| **Export-Oriented Model** | Growth by selling manufactured goods to world markets |
| **Import Substitution** | Grow domestic industry by replacing imports with local production |
| **Comparative Advantage** | Countries should specialize in what they produce relatively most efficiently |
| **Infant Industry Argument** | Protect new industries temporarily until they can compete globally |
| **Core-Periphery** | Spatial division of the world economy: developed core exploits underdeveloped periphery |
| **Unequal Exchange** | Periphery sells cheap raw materials, buys expensive manufactures — a net transfer of surplus |
| **OLI Paradigm** | Why MNCs exist: Ownership, Location, Internalization advantages |
| **Dutch Disease** | A resource boom (e.g., gas, oil) hurts other export sectors via exchange rate appreciation |
| **Race to the Bottom** | Countries lower labor/environmental standards to attract foreign investment |
| **Enclave Economy** | Export sector exists in isolation with few backward linkages to the rest of the economy |

## The Two Big Debates

### 1. Structure vs Agency

Do international economic structures (global capitalism, core-periphery relations, IMF/WB rules) **determine** outcomes for developing countries — or do local actors (states, social movements, entrepreneurs) have genuine **agency** to chart their own path?

| Structure Side | Agency Side |
|----------------|-------------|
| Dependency theory: the core keeps the periphery underdeveloped | Modernization theory: countries can develop with the right policies |
| World Bank/IMF conditionality limits policy space for developing countries | East Asian miracles show state-led development can succeed |
| MNCs extract surplus from host countries via transfer pricing | Some countries used MNC investment to build domestic capacity |
| WTO rules constrain protectionist options | Strategic industrial policy is still possible within WTO rules |

### 2. Global vs Local

Should developing countries integrate deeply into global markets (free trade, export-oriented growth) — or build domestic capacity first (protectionism, import substitution, local food security)?

| Global Integration | Local First |
|-------------------|-------------|
| Gains from comparative advantage and specialization | Infant industry protection before opening up |
| Export-led growth succeeded in East Asia | Domestic demand-led growth (UNCTAD 2013 report) |
| Foreign investment brings technology and jobs | Risk of enclave economies, Dutch disease, volatility |
| WTO rules create predictable trading environment | Policy space for development is shrinking under global rules |

## The Connection Chain

```
Colonial Extraction → Specialization in Primary Goods
        ↓
Modernization Theory (1940s–50s)
  "All countries follow the same linear path to development"
        ↓  (critique from the left)
Dependency Theory / World Systems Theory (1950s–60s)
  "The system is rigged — core exploits periphery systematically"
        ↓  (policy response)
Washington Consensus (1980s–90s)
  "Liberalize, privatize, stabilize — the market knows best"
        ↓  (counter-response)
Protectionism / Industrial Policy
  "Infant industries need shelter before they can compete"
        ↓  (ongoing tension)
Export-Oriented Growth  ↔  Domestic-Led Development
(MNCs, RMG, FDI)          (food security, local industry, tariff protection)
```

## The Concepts You Need

| Concept | Where It Appears |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Modernization theory's origins** | Post-WWII context, Cold War, Truman's Point Four (1949), decolonization |
| **Rostow's 5 stages** | Traditional → Preconditions → Take-off → Drive to Maturity → High Mass Consumption |
| **4 critiques of modernization** | Eurocentrism, linear stages fallacy, ignores colonial exploitation, fails to explain East Asian miracle |
| **Prebisch-Singer thesis** | Terms of trade decline for primary commodity exporters over time |
| **Core vs periphery dynamics** | Unequal exchange, "development of underdevelopment" (Frank) |
| **GATT vs WTO** | GATT: provisional agreement, weak enforcement. WTO: binding disputes, broader scope |
| **IMF & World Bank roles** | Conditional lending, structural adjustment programs, policy conditionality |
| **OLI Paradigm** | Ownership advantage, Location advantage, Internalization advantage — why firms go multinational |
| **Product life cycle theory** | Vernon: products mature and production shifts from innovating to developing countries |
| **Dutch disease mechanics** | Resource boom → currency appreciation → non-resource tradable sectors become uncompetitive |
| **Race to the bottom** | Regulatory competition for FDI — countries undercut each other on labor and environmental standards |
| **Infant industry argument** | Friedrich List vs Adam Smith; US and Germany used protectionist tariffs in the 19th century |
| **Bangladesh dairy case** | Tariff on imported milk powder: protect local farmers vs higher consumer prices |
| **RMG case** | Export-led growth but low value addition, import dependence, enclave characteristics |
