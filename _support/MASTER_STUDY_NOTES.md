## 1. NAILA KABEER — PATRIARCHY & WOMEN'S AGENCY IN BANGLADESH

### 1.1 The Bangladesh Paradox

**Definition:** Bangladesh achieved major social progress — pro-poor and gender-equitable — despite:
- Being among the poorest countries at independence (1971)
- Having "one of the most non-negotiable patriarchies in the world" (Goetz 1992)
- Political instability, weak governance, rising Islamic orthodoxy

> *"Bangladesh stands out as the shining new example in South Asia of a poor country achieving impressive gains in gender equality"* — World Bank (2008)

**Three dimensions of the paradox:**

| Dimension | Expected | Actual |
|-----------|----------|--------|
| **Economic** | Social progress requires minimum wealth (Sen's "growth-mediated" model) | Bangladesh outperforms countries at similar/higher GDP |
| **Political** | Good governance needed for pro-poor development | Corrupt state still produced pro-poor outcomes |
| **Cultural/Gender** | Islam incompatible with women's emancipation | Gender equality improved alongside rising conservatism |

**Kabeer's thesis:** The paradox is explained by **agency of ordinary women** who renegotiated patriarchy — not just by top-down actors (state, NGOs, donors).

---

### 1.2 Patriarchy in Bangladesh Context

Bangladesh sits within the belt of **"classic patriarchy"** (Kandiyoti 1988; Cain 1984) stretching North Africa → Middle East → Northern South Asia.

**Features of classic patriarchy:**
| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Patrilineal | Descent/inheritance through father |
| Patrilocal | Wives move to husband's family |
| Patriarchal | Men hold power |
| Endogamous | Marriage within same group |
| Polygynous | Occasionally practiced |

**Consequences:**
- "Missing women" (Sen 1990) — >100 million globally
- Sex ratios: 106–110 men per 100 women (vs. ≤100 normal)
- Strong son preference → high fertility
- Dowry system, early marriage, high maternal mortality
- Women secluded in domestic domain; restricted mobility

**North–South Divide within South Asia** (Dyson & Moore 1983):

| Feature | North (Indo-Aryan) — Bangladesh | South (Indo-Dravidian) |
|---------|--------------------------------|----------------------|
| Inheritance | Patrilineal | Bilateral |
| Women's mobility | Restricted/secluded | Greater freedom |
| Fertility | Higher | Lower |
| Sex ratios | 106–114 M:100F | 97–107 M:100F |

> *Key point: Bangladesh was classified closer to the north Indian model.*

---

### 1.3 Key Concepts

**Agency** — *"The capacity to define goals and act upon them within social constraints"* (Giddens 1979)

Kabeer's spectrum of agency:
| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Adaptive preferences | Compliance with norms; habituated to inequality (Sen) |
| Unconscious conformity | Habitus — internalised dispositions |
| Hidden transcripts | Scott's "weapons of the weak" — folk tales, songs, silence |
| Bargaining/negotiation | Influencing practices within normative boundaries |
| Discursive strategies | Reinterpreting boundaries of acceptable/unacceptable |
| Overt resistance | Open defiance — highest cost, most risky |

**Resources** (Bourdieu 1977):
- **Material**: land, livestock, finance, technology
- **Human**: skills, education, knowledge, health
- **Social**: networks, relationships, claims and obligations
- **Symbolic**: status, prestige, authority to interpret norms

**Patriarchal Bargains** (Kandiyoti 1988):
> *"Strategies women use to maximise security under patriarchal constraints."*
- In classic patriarchy: women pursue security **within** patriarchy, not against it
- Producing sons = status, protection, old-age security

**Household Bargaining Power:**
- Economic contribution strengthens women's voice in: fertility decisions, allocation of food/health/education
- Mothers are less likely than fathers to discriminate against daughters

---

### 1.4 Employment & Empowerment — Causal Pathways

**Two pathways** (Sen 1990b):

1. **Societal level:** Women working outside → contributions socially visible → recognized by family/society → entitled to fairer share of resources
2. **Household level:** Paid activity → financial contribution → increased ability to negotiate egalitarian resource distribution

**Evidence from India** (Drèze & Sen 1995):
- Districts with higher female economic activity + literacy → lower fertility, lower female disadvantage in child survival
- Male literacy had much smaller effect on female disadvantage

**In Bangladesh:**
- Entry into paid work (especially RMG sector) came at high symbolic/practical cost
- Rise of female labour force participation = key indicator of change

---

### 1.5 Indicators of Change — Bangladesh vs. India vs. Pakistan

| Indicator | Bangladesh | India | Pakistan |
|-----------|-----------|-------|----------|
| Fertility rate (2011) | **2.2** | 2.6 | 3.4 |
| Female life expectancy | **70 yrs** | 67 yrs | 66 yrs |
| Infant mortality (per 1000) | **37** | 47 | 59 |
| Gender gap in child mortality | **Largely disappeared by 2000** | Persisted | Some rise |
| Sex ratio at birth | **105:100 (normal)** | Rose to 111:100 | Some rise |

**Key findings:**
- Strong decline in son preference
- Most families now prefer balanced sex composition (both sons and daughters)
- People saw themselves as active participants — *"keeping up with the changing times"*

---

### 1.6 How Change Happens — Despite Patriarchy

Kabeer draws on **Giddens (structuration theory)**, **Bourdieu (habitus)**, **Ortner**:

- Social instability → gaps between internalised dispositions and social world
- Life-course disruptions — events childhood didn't prepare for
- Societal differentiation — more ways of earning/learning → habitus more open
- New resources through government, markets, civil society
- Incremental adjustments + everyday negotiations + accumulated effects over time

> *"Change within patriarchy occurs not through rupture, but through cumulative acts of renegotiation."*

## 2. VENEZUELA — ECONOMIC COLLAPSE AS A LESSON

### Core Argument
> *"Venezuela is not just an economic collapse; it is a lesson on governance and diversification."*

### Key Factors in Venezuela's Collapse
| Factor | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Oil dependency** | ~95% of export earnings from oil; economy not diversified |
| **Price shock vulnerability** | 2014 oil price crash exposed structural weaknesses |
| **Mismanagement** | Price controls, expropriations, currency manipulation |
| **Hyperinflation** | One of the worst in modern history |
| **Production collapse** | Oil output fell from 3.2M bpd to <500k bpd |
| **Humanitarian crisis** | Mass migration, food/medicine shortages |

### Lessons for Developing Countries
1. **Diversify or die** — Over-reliance on a single export is catastrophic
2. **Institutions matter** — Strong institutions buffer against political mismanagement
3. **Macroeconomic discipline** — Printing money destroys value
4. **Governance infrastructure** — Corruption + lack of checks = rapid collapse
5. **Social contracts** — When state fails to deliver basic services, society unravels

## 5. MODERNIZATION THEORY

### 5.1 Origins (1940s–50s)

| Factor | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Post-WWII context** | Western countries wanted to spread industrial progress; colonialism no longer acceptable → language shifted to "development" |
| **Cold War geopolitics** | Needed to align developing nations with capitalism vs. Soviet communism |
| **Truman 1949** | "Bold new programme for making benefits of scientific advances available for underdeveloped areas" |
| **Marshall Plan model** | Success of post-war European reconstruction became template |
| **Eurocentrism** | Western countries = advanced; developing countries = backward, needing guidance |

**Core idea:** Developing countries are poor because they are **traditional and backward**. They should follow the same path Western countries took.

### 5.2 Rostow's 5 Stages of Economic Growth (1960)

| Stage | Name | Key Characteristics |
|-------|------|---------------------|
| 1 | **Traditional Society** | Subsistence agriculture, barter, low technology, kinship ties |
| 2 | **Pre-conditions for Take-off** | Specialization begins, surplus, trade expands, savings/investment |
| 3 | **Take-off** | Industrialization, shift to manufacturing, growth becomes self-sustaining |
| 4 | **Drive to Maturity** | Diversification, less import dependence, complex technology |
| 5 | **Age of Mass Consumption** | Mass production, service sector dominates, high living standards |

**Rostow's key assumption:** Every country passes through these fixed, linear stages. The Western path is universal.

### 5.3 Critiques of Modernization Theory

| # | Critique | Details |
|---|----------|---------|
| 1 | **Eurocentrism** | Assumes Western culture/values superior; treats developing countries' history/knowledge as inferior |
| 2 | **Oversimplified linear thinking** | Development is NOT a straight line; countries can move backward (war, crisis) |
| 3 | **Top-down / Ignores the poor** | Planned by elites/foreign experts and imposed; poor treated as passive receivers |
| 4 | **Destroys local culture** | Argues traditional values must be abandoned → "deculturalization" |
| 5 | **Externally driven / Creates dependency** | Assumes developing countries cannot develop by themselves |
| 6 | **Ignores colonial history** | Underdevelopment was CREATED through colonialism, not natural |
| 7 | **Ignores how the West actually developed** | West developed by EXTRACTING wealth from colonies, not just internal effort |

## 6. DEPENDENCY THEORY

### 6.1 Origin & Core Premise

- Emerged **1950s–60s** as reaction to modernization theory
- Rooted in Marxist analysis of capitalism
- **André Gunder Frank (1967):** *"Underdevelopment is not an original state — it's a result of economic capture and control of backward regions by advanced metropolitan capitalism"*

**World economy divided into:**
- **Centre (Metropolis):** Developed countries — control technology, capital, markets
- **Periphery (Satellite):** Developing countries — provide raw materials, cheap labour, markets

> *The development of the metropolis was a result of the active underdevelopment of the periphery.*

### 6.2 Dependency Theory's Critique of Rostow

| # | Argument |
|---|----------|
| 1 | **Eurocentric** — Western experience is NOT universal |
| 2 | **Ignores colonial history** — underdevelopment was CREATED, not natural |
| 3 | **Ignores how the West developed** — through colonial extraction |
| 4 | **Assumes equal starting points** — ignores colonialism, debt, unequal trade |
| 5 | **Ignores centre-periphery relationship** — global economy KEEPS periphery dependent |
| 6 | **Ignores unequal trade** — raw materials sold cheap, manufactures bought expensive |
| 7 | **Too linear and simplistic** |
| 8 | **Blames developing countries for their own poverty** — ignores external domination |

### 6.3 Key Theorists

| Theorist | Contribution |
|----------|-------------|
| **André Gunder Frank** | *The Development of Underdevelopment* (1967) — underdevelopment created by capitalism |
| **Walter Rodney** | *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972) — detailed colonial extraction |
| **Samir Amin** | Imperialism established "total domination" of peripheral regions |
| **Immanuel Wallerstein** | World Systems Theory — global capitalism structurally advantages the core |
| **J. Matunhu** | Argued for African Renaissance theory based on African values |

## 7. THE WASHINGTON CONSENSUS

### 7.1 Core Definition

**Washington Consensus** — coordinated campaign for global diffusion of market-oriented policies, organized through IFIs (IMF, World Bank). Term coined by **John Williamson (1990)**.

"Washington" = political Washington (Congress, administration) + technocratic Washington (IMF, World Bank, U.S. Treasury, Fed, think tanks).

### 7.2 The 10 Policy Prescriptions

| # | Policy | Prescription |
|---|--------|-------------|
| 1 | **Fiscal discipline** | Budget deficits ≤ 1–2% of GNP |
| 2 | **Public spending** | Cut subsidies → redirect to health, education, infrastructure |
| 3 | **Tax reform** | Broad base, moderate rates |
| 4 | **Interest rates** | Market-determined, positive in real terms |
| 5 | **Exchange rates** | Market-determined, competitive |
| 6 | **Trade liberalization** | Remove import restrictions |
| 7 | **FDI** | Remove restrictions on foreign investment |
| 8 | **Privatization** | Sell state-owned enterprises |
| 9 | **Deregulation** | Remove excessive regulations |
| 10 | **Property rights** | Secure property rights |

> **Summary:** "Prudent macroeconomic policies, outward orientation, and free-market capitalism."

### 7.3 Historical Context

| Period | Development |
|--------|-------------|
| Pre-1980s | Import-substituting industrialization, state intervention, central planning |
| 1970s | Oil price spikes, petrodollar recycling → developing countries borrowed heavily |
| 1979 | Volcker raised U.S. interest rates → debts became unsustainable |
| 1982 | Mexico debt crisis → Latin American crisis spreads |
| 1985 | Baker Plan — financial aid conditional on market-liberalizing reforms |
| 1990s | End of Cold War → unipolar U.S. dominance |

### 7.4 Promoters & Mechanisms

| Actor | Role |
|-------|------|
| **U.S. Government** | Leading engineer; veto power in IFIs |
| **IMF** | Expanded mandate → market liberalization via conditionality |
| **World Bank** | Structural adjustment loans (~30% of disbursements by late 1980s) |
| **Bilateral donors** | Required IMF/World Bank compliance |

**Key mechanism:** **Conditionality** — loans exchanged for policy adoption.

### 7.5 Babb & Kentikelenis's Critique

**A. Not just ideas — power & coercion:**
- Driven by organizational mechanisms (conditionality), U.S. hegemony, corporate interests (Wall Street)

**B. Uneven implementation:**
- Latin America = "star pupils"
- U.S. strategic allies (Middle East) = more lenient
- China, Vietnam, India notably abstained

**C. Devastating outcomes:**
- Countries that most adopted the recipe (Latin America, Eastern Europe) **failed to thrive**
- Increased **unemployment and inequality**
- China — which **ignored** the Consensus — achieved astounding growth

**D. Erosion of legitimacy:**
- By 2006, Dani Rodrik: *"Nobody really believes in the Washington Consensus anymore"*
- Stiglitz fired from World Bank for criticizing IMF
- Krugman, Sachs recanted

**E. The "Undercover" Consensus:**
- IMF still promotes structural reforms in crisis lending (Eurozone bailouts)
- World Bank uses Ease of Doing Business rankings to influence policy
- Reforms already enacted are **difficult to reverse**

### 7.6 Impacts on Developing Countries

| Area | Impact |
|------|--------|
| **Trade** | Tariffs collapsed (Latin America: 42% → <10%); flood of imports |
| **Privatization** | Thousands of SOEs sold to foreign investors |
| **Fiscal** | Austerity; cuts to subsidies and public services |
| **Labour** | Deregulation; weakened protections |
| **Inequality** | Increased in most reforming countries |
| **Policy space** | Dramatically narrowed |

## 8. FREE TRADE IN AGRICULTURE & FOOD CRISIS

### 8.1 The Promise vs. Reality

| Promise | Reality |
|---------|---------|
| World Bank predicted $250B gains | Revised to $40–60B after signing |
| Developed countries would reduce subsidies | U.S. limits set at already-reached levels |
| Higher commodity prices for farmers | FAO index fell 53% in real terms (1997–2003) |
| Food security through trade | Import bills rose 40% for LDCs (1995–96) |

### 8.2 Arguments Against Free Trade in Agriculture

1. **Not a level playing field** — 3–5 firms control 40%+ of global trade (Cargill, Nestlé)
2. **Dumping** — Cotton: 57%, Wheat: 40%, Rice: 20% dumped below cost
3. **Selective liberalization** — Rich countries protected sugar/cotton (where South is competitive); liberalized what they don't produce
4. **Food security destroyed** — Public food stocks dismantled under IMF/World Bank pressure
5. **Only 10–15% of food traded internationally** — Yet AoA pressures ALL agriculture to be trade-oriented

### 8.3 Utsa Patnaik — Origins of the Food Crisis

**Three structural contradictions causing food crisis:**
1. Food for humans vs. feed for animals
2. Food for domestic consumption vs. food for export
3. Food vs. fuel (biofuels)

**Inefficiency of meat production:**
| Product | Grain required | Calorie loss |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| 1 kg beef | 7+ kg grain | ~95% lost |
| 1 kg chicken | 2 kg grain | Significant |
| 1 litre milk | 0.2 kg grain | Moderate |

**Key statistics:**
- U.S. grain consumption: ~900 kg/person/year (80% indirect via animal feed)
- Poor countries: ~130 kg/person/year
- Rich countries consume **7x more grain per person**
- Indian family (2005): **110 kg less grain/year** than 1991
- India's poverty line (2005): ₹356/month — not enough for 1 kg rice
- U.S. corn for ethanol: 27M tons → **100M+ tons** (2002–08)

**India's paradox:**
- GDP growth ~8%, income growth ~6%
- 10% income rise → 14–16% rise in meat demand
- Animal product demand ↑ 9% annually → feed grain ↑ → food for poor ↓

### 8.4 Bangladesh Dairy Industry Case Study

| Indicator | Data |
|-----------|------|
| Population | 160M+, 72% rural |
| Family dairy farms | 1.4 million (~7M people) |
| Typical farmer | 2 cows, income Tk 31–60/day |
| Milk consumption | 42 ml/person/day vs. FAO recommendation 280g |
| Production | 2.7M tonnes; demand may be 7.2M tonnes |
| **27% of milk** | met by imports |
| **2009 price crash** | Farmers lost up to 40% of income; poured milk on highways |

**Bangladesh's comparative advantage:**
- Bangladeshi farm (2 cows) produces milk **50% cheaper** than German farm (31 cows)
- **350 jobs per million kg** milk in Bangladesh vs. **7.6 jobs in EU**

**Arla Foods (Danish-Swedish giant):**
- Controls >20% of national milk sales (Dano brand)
- Received nearly €1 billion in EU subsidies since 2000
- 2009: exported 1.5M kg to Bangladesh at 15% subsidized price

**Policy failure:**
- Tariffs fell from 75% (2007) to proposed 5%
- Government eliminated direct subsidies to farmers (1996)
- Budget allocation to dairy: 0.27% vs. 2.7% GDP contribution

## 9. RMG SECTOR & CASH CROPS

### 9.1 Bangladesh RMG — Value Addition Eroding

| Metric | FY21 | FY22 |
|--------|------|------|
| RMG import growth | — | **+51.2%** |
| RMG export growth | — | **+24.6%** |
| Raw materials as % of export value | 69% | **83%** |
| Jan-Mar FY22 | — | **>90%** |

**Implications:**
- Value addition eroding — becoming more cutting-and-trading than manufacturing
- Capital machinery imports jumped 40.78% (textile 24%, garment 46%)
- Possible over-invoicing flagged by central banker (no investigation initiated)

### 9.2 Cash Crops vs. Food Crops in Africa (Darkoh & Ould-Mey)

**Central thesis:** Conflict between cash crops (export) and food crops (self-reliance) = conflict between **dependency and autonomy**.

**Key findings:**
- Over 3 decades, real incomes from cash crops declined
- African shares in world commodity markets worsened
- Most African countries sank deeper into debt
- **Colonial legacy:** Pre-colonial Africa was food self-sufficient; colonialism imposed cash crops

**Deteriorating terms of trade:**
- 1960: 3 tons bananas = 1 tractor
- 1970: **11 tons** bananas = 1 tractor
- Rubber: 25 tons (1960) = 6 tractors; 25 tons (1975) = 2 tractors

### 9.3 UNCTAD Recommendations for Developing Countries

Post-2008, developing countries must shift from **export-oriented → domestic-demand-oriented growth**:

1. Stronger public sector role — fiscal stance, infrastructure
2. More equal income distribution — minimum wage, tax, welfare
3. Supportive monetary/credit policies
4. Reform international monetary system
5. South-South cooperation
6. Better regulation of commodity markets
