Introduction
One of the branches of the classical school of economics is “Neoclassical economics”. This group
features some of the remarkable economists in the history of economics. For example:
Bentham, Say, Jevons, Menger etc. Neoclassical economists came up with the idea of utility
theory and added further to the discussions of classical economists with the likes of Adam
Smith, Ricardo etc. Furthermore, they also contributed to the marginal idea which is part and
parcel of modern economics. Thus, it is important to know what their contributions were. This
writing makes an attempt to shed light on some of the key ideas of prominent Neoclassical
economists.

Jeremy Bentham
Jeremy Bentham was an influential English philosopher, jurist, and social reformer, born on
February 15, 1748, in London, England. He is best known for his development of utilitarianism, a
moral theory that advocates actions that maximize happiness and minimize suffering for the
greatest number of people. Bentham believed in the principle of the greatest happiness of the
greatest number, emphasizing the importance of rational calculation in ethics and law.
Throughout his life, Bentham advocated for various social reforms, including prison reform,
animal rights, freedom of expression, and the separation of church and state. He also made
significant contributions to legal theory, particularly in the areas of legislation and
jurisprudence. Bentham's ideas greatly influenced political thought and had a lasting impact on
legal and ethical discourse. He died on June 6, 1832, but his legacy continues to shape
contemporary debates in philosophy, law, and politics.



Utilitarianism received its most distinctive, classical formulation in the writings of Jeremy
Bentham (1748-1882) whose work, that was the most powerful influence on
nineteenth-century economic theory, “An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and
legislation" published in 1780. Although it does not deal directly with economic theory, it
contains an elaborate statement of the utilitarian social philosophy which has become the
philosophical basis of neoclassical economies during the last several decades of the nineteenth
century.

Bentham started his book with the statement that, “Nature has placed mankind under the
governance of two sovereign masters, pain and Pleasure." And he began with the assertion that
all human motivation, in all times and all places, can be reduced to a single principle: the desire
to maximize one's utility. He also stated utility as: “By utility is meant that property in any
object, whereby it tends to produce benefit, advantage, pleasure, good, or happiness, to
prevent the happening of mischief, pain, evil, or unhappiness to the party whose interest is
considered".

So Bentham believed that all human motivation were merely manifestations of the desire for
Pleasure and the avoidance of Pain. Because pain was merely negative pleasure, Bentham's
principle of utility could be restated as "All human activity springs from the desire to maximize
pleasure"

By reducing all human motives to a single principle, Bentham believed that he had found the
key to the construction of a science of human welfare or happiness that could be stated
mathematically and might someday be worked out with the same numerical exactitude as the

science of physics." Pleasures. and the avoidance of pains are... ends", he argue, which can be
numerically quantified such that we can "understand their value".

He suggested a possible method for quantifying Pleasures:

"To a number of persons, with reference to each of whom the value of a pleasure or pain is
considered, it will be greater or less, according to seven circumstances:

1. Its intensity.

2. Its duration.

3. Its certainty or uncertainty.

4.Its propinquity or remoteness.

5. Its fecundity.

6. Its purity.

7. Its extent."



He then went into a discussion of the specific ways in which these circumstances might suggest
how one could rationally calculate the values of pleasures and pains. Bentham not only
conceived of human beings as calculating maximizers of pleasure, he also saw them as being
fundamentally individualistic. "In the general tenor of life," he wrote, "in every human breast,
self-regarding interest is predominant over all other interests put together. Self-preference has a
place everywhere”. He believed, People were also essentially lazy. Any kind of exertion on work
was viewed as painful, and, therefore, work would never be undertaken without the promise of
greater pleasure or the avoidance of greater pain.

Each of these ideas of Bentham was to become more important in the subsequent development
of the utility theory of value. Bentham believed: "All value is founded on utility... where there is
no use, there cannot be any value".

Smith and Ricardo would have agreed that use value was a necessary condition for exchange
value. But, as Ricardo insisted, when one sees value as created by labor, then an increase in the
productivity of labor lowers the value of a commodity while increasing the general wealth,
when Bentham stated that "it is by the degree of that value that Wealth is measured", he was
speaking from the utility theory perspective, in which an increase in utility increases a
commodity's value and hence increases its owner's wealth.

Bentham as a Social Reformer

In Bentham's earlier writings, he accepted Smith's argument that a competitive free market
would allocate productive resources to those industries in which they would be the most
productive. He therefore believed that government interference in the free market might very
likely diminish the level of production.

Bentham also accepted, during his early writings, that aggregate supply would always equal
aggregate demand in a free market. In such a market there need never be a depression or any
involuntary unemployment because any savings was automatically converted into more capital
with which to employ more labor.

However, by 1801, Bentham's opinions about government intervention in the economy had
undergone a change and he stated, "The interference of government, and often as... (it results
in) the smallest... advantage… is an event I witness with... satisfaction."

This change of opinion was prompted by two principal concerns, each of which was later to
become an important argument against complete laissez faire.



   ●      First, Bentham, like Malthus, came to see that savings might not be matched by new
       investment. In this event, production would diminish, unemployment would be created,


                                                                                            7
       and the free market would not be functioning in the best interests of the public. He
       stated that an increase in the habit of frugality willed diminish the mars of expenditure
       on consumption and disrupt the money supply. In that case, saving would lead to
       decreased prices and production; profits would decline and so would investment. In
       such a case, Bentham argued, if the government increased the amount of money in
       circulation, "then the money introduced • becomes a source of increasing Wealth"



   ● The second reason for government interference in the market was to lessen the social
     effects of great inequalities of wealth and income. Bentham believed that a person's
     capacity to get enjoyment from money declined as he got more money. In modern
     terminology, he believed in a diminishing marginal utility of money. Therefore, all other
     things being equal, a government measure that redistributed money from the rich to the
     poor would increase the total of society's aggregate utility. [Government had to be a
     benevolent, impartial institution, equally interested in every individual's welfare]


Thus, over Bentham's lifetime, there was a split or antagonism between his earlier extreme
laissez-faire attitude and his later reformist attitude. This same split was to be reflected later in
the neoclassical tradition, which was constructed on the foundation of Bentham's utilitarian
philosophy.

Jean-Baptiste Say
Jean-Baptiste Say (5 January 1767 – 15 November 1832) was a liberal French economist and
businessman who argued in favor of competition, free trade and lifting restraints on business.
He is best known for Say's law—also known as the law of markets—which he popularized.

J. B. Say, was originally a follower of Adam Smith's ideas. His thoughts were organized around
Smith's ideas and he tried to improve upon them. He believed that there were some small
mistakes in Smith's ideas, so he worked to correct those mistakes by using Smith's overall ideas.
That is, instead of creating something entirely new, Say wanted to perfect small mistakes based
on what Smith had started.

But, as it turns out, these small changes completely abandon Smith's ideas and lead to
something completely new. This led to a completely different way of thinking about economics
than Smith originally intended.

One of Smith's mistakes is that Smith believed that only human labor (people working) can
create value, and based on this, he provided the Labor Theory of Value. Say rejected Smith's
labor theory of value. According to him, this is not entirely true as there are other things
besides just human labor that can add value to goods and services.

According to Say, the value or price of something depends on how useful it is. For example, a
pencil may not cost much because it is easy to find and many people have it, but if you are
stranded on an island without any writing equipment, a pencil will be very valuable because it
will help you communicate and keep track. So basically, the more useful a product is to
someone, the more they will be willing to pay for it.

Thus, he rejected labor as the source of value and added that utility creates value. He said that,
"the utility of things is the ground-work of their value, and their value constitutes wealth.
Exchangeable value, or price, is an index of the recognized utility of a thing."

In rejecting the notion that labor was the source of value and insisting that only utility created
values, Say not only departed sharply from the ideas of Smith and Ricardo, but also he placed
the utility approach in the context of a methodological approach and a social philosophy.

According to Smith and Ricardo, the income of labor differs from the income of ownership of
the factors of production, and in looking for the source of this difference, they conclude that
class conflict characterized capitalism. But, after that, when they reverted to the exchange or
utility approach to economic theory, they were led to the conclusion that free market capitalism
was inherently a system of social harmony.



                                                                                                9
Here lies the dilemma.

Say resolved this dilemma by rejecting the labor theory of value approach to economic theory.

In his utility framework

1. He rejected the theoretical distinction between income of different social classes.

2. Rather than seeing the production process as a series of human efforts to transform various
natural raw materials into usable products, he emphasized the existence of various 'productive
agencies' that combine to produce goods.

3. These productive agencies were ultimately producing as "utility," and each agency was
coequally responsible for the production of the utility.

4. These productive agencies included "human industry, with the aid of capital and of natural
agents and properties," altogether created "every kind of utility, which works as the primary
source of value. In other words, there was no qualitative difference, in the creation of utility,
between the exertion of human labor and the ownership of capital, land, and other property.

In his utility framework, Say wanted to show that owners of the means of production received
their incomes from similar sacrifices. Smith and Ricardo had argued that commodity prices
reflected the rate of wages and the rate of profits and that these rates were determined by
other social and technical considerations, where as, Say argued that wage and profit rates were
determined by the relative contributions to utility creation made by labor and capital.

Within Say's utility approach to value and distribution theory, all notions of class conflict
disappeared. A central purpose of Say's Treatise was to demonstrate that social harmony was
the natural result of a capitalist economy, not class conflict.



Say's Law of Markets:

Say’s Law of Markets states that the supply of a good or service creates demand for that good or
service, that is - supply creates its own demand. And that economic agents must first engage in
production before they can demand goods and services in the market.

According to the Law of Markets, a person’s ability to demand goods and services is a direct
result of production activities that they’ve undertaken. They earn an income either through the
production and sale of physical assets or by supplying labor to capital owners. The productivity
of the economic agent will influence the level of income they receive, which will, in turn,
influence what goods and services they demand and how much of each good or service they


                                                                                              10
demand. By choosing to produce a certain good, they are indirectly creating the demand for
those goods and services that they intend to buy with their earned income. Conversely, if one
did not demand anything, they would not want to work. Thus, Say’s Law describes the unfolding
market process, stating that “all purchasers must first be producers, as only production can
generate the power to purchase.”

According to Say, there may be a temporary surplus of some products, but this will result in the
market not achieving equilibrium.

In this case, there will be a surplus of those products that were priced too high and
simultaneously a shortage of those products that were priced too low. Surpluses and deficits
will just cancel out as a whole. Moreover, capitalists who sell goods at a too low price will make
low profits, and those who sell at a too high price will make high profits. Then, the search for
maximum profits will cause capitalists to leave low-cost industries and enter high-cost
industries. This migration of capitalists will have two effects.

First, it will change prices, raising the prices of low-priced goods and lowering the prices of
high-priced goods.

Second, it would reduce the production of those goods for which there was a surplus and
increase the production of those goods for which there was a shortage.

Thus, throughout the process, aggregate supply will always equal aggregate demand. And
demand and supply will be equal for each product as well. Hence, there can never be a surplus
of all products in the market. Moreover, temporary shortages and surpluses between different
products will be eliminated automatically by free, competitive markets.

These conclusions form Say's Law of Markets and are still accepted by many economists and
policy makers today.

Criticism:

Violating the assumptions of Say's Law, Keynes has criticized the law. He criticized Say’s law of
markets on the subsequent grounds:

1. Supply does not create its Demand: The proposition of the law - Supply Creates Its own
demand - is not applicable to modern economies where demand does not increase as much as
production increases. It is also not possible to consume only those goods which are produced
within the economy.

2. Self-adjustment is not Possible: According to Say’s law, full-employment is maintained by an
automatic and self-adjustment mechanism in the long run. But Keynes had no patience to wait
for the long period for he believed that “In the long-run we are all dead.”

3. Money is not Neutral: Say’s law of markets is based on a barter system and ignores the role
of money in the system. But Keynes has given importance to money. He regards money as a
medium of exchange. Money is held for income and business motives.

4. Over Production is Possible: According to Keynes, all income accruing to factors of
production is not spent but some fraction out of it is saved which is not automatically invested.
So, over-production may be possible.

5. Underemployment Situation: Keynes regards full employment as a special case because
there is underemployment in capitalist economies. Millions of workers are prepared to work at
the current wage rate, and even below it, but they do not find work.

Nassau Senior
Nassau Senior (September 1790-June 1864), who was an English lawyer, played a significant role
in the early stages of modern neoclassical economics, similar to Bentham and Say. Like Say, he
took concepts from earlier classical economists, adjusted some, and introduced his own ideas to
create a consistent theoretical justification of the status quo of nineteenth-century capitalism.
His ideas about the appropriate methodology of economic theory, the place of utility in
explaining value, and the moral and intellectual justification of profit and rent constitute the
most important areas in which he influenced the later neoclassical tradition. Senior had a strong
interest in social, economic, and political issues.
In 1825, he was appointed to the first chair of political economy at Oxford University. The social
and economic problems that most consistently commanded his attention were -
   ● The general condition of the working class and
   ● The causes and consequences of poverty.
Senior's ideas about the working class and poverty underwent a dramatic change in 1830, and,
in the period after 1830, he produced the ideas that were to have the most influence both
intellectually and politically.
Prior to 1830, Senior was a politically conservative man with a keen sympathy and benevolent
concern for the poverty of the working class. His ‘Introductory Lecture on Political Economy’
was published in 1826, and his ‘Two Lectures on Population’ was published in 1828. In these
early works, he showed optimism about the future of the working class. He did not believe that
Malthus's population theory legitimately led one to the conclusion that working people would
always be at a subsistence level. He believed, rather, that increases in productivity could be
accompanied by improvements in the moral character of workers, and, consequently, the
standard of living of most workers would rise. He actively supported efforts that he believed
would uplift the intellectual and moral status of the poor, and he saw moral education as the
only hope for eliminating poverty. However, his views were to change in 1830.
Between 1829 and 1842, England experienced a long series of labor difficulties. Industrialization
had reduced the English working class to an almost subhuman level of exploitation and
degradation. In the 1820s and 1830s, the working class fought back. After 1829, there were
many massive efforts to organize labor, which frequently met with harsh repression. The
consequence was widespread strikes, riots, and industrial sabotage, all of which profoundly
frightened Senior. Senior became convinced that the poor laws and the government's dole to
the poor and the unemployed were the principal causes of poverty and a great threat to the
very existence of English capitalism.
In 1830, he published ‘Three Lectures on the Rate of Wages’, written in the early part of that
year. In this preface, he argued that capitalists had a "fund for the maintenance of laborers"
(this notion came to be known in economic literature as the "wages fund doctrine"). He


                                                                                               14
asserted that the size of this fund was determined solely by labor's productivity. Therefore,
improving the living standard of workers required either an increase in their productivity or a
decrease in the number of workers among whom the wages fund was divided. There were, he
stated, two ways of increasing labor's productivity:
   ● First, the removal of all restrictions on free commerce and accumulation of capital, and
   ● Second, the abolition of the poor laws, which had "made wages not a matter of contract
     between the master and the workman, but a right for the one, and a tax on the other."
In the preface, it is clear that Senior was no longer concerned with the misery caused by
poverty, but with "the threat of an arrogant laboring class, resorting to strikes, violence, and
combinations [unions], a threat to the foundations not merely of wealth but of existence
itself."36 The great danger, in Senior's eyes, was that labor unions would fight to maintain and
extend the notion that wages should reflect the needs of each worker's family rather than the
free play of the forces of supply and demand. The poor laws were based on a system of family
allowances for the unemployed and the destitute. Such laws, Senior believed, decreased
workers' incentives to work and created the arrogant attitude of workers that their families had
a right to exist even if the workers themselves did not or could not find work. This created an
"unnatural" relationship between capitalists and workers.
In this period, the ideas of radicals and socialists were spreading fast. Thomas Hodgskin and
William Thompson had written books in the 1820s in which they argued that profits were
generally unearned income coercively expropriated from workers.39 These ideas were being
widely disseminated and discussed. The socialist doctrines of Robert Owen were also rapidly
becoming influential. Senior deplored socialist ideas, calling them a mistaken belief that the
conditions creating inequality could be remedied. Such a mistaken belief he called "the political
economy of the poor," and he argued that these ideas had a natural appeal to the uneducated.
Equality can only involve extreme misery, he argued, because "though it is in the power of
human institutions to make everybody poor, they cannot make everybody rich; ... they can
diffuse misery, but not happiness."


The new poor law reflected the following commission views:
(1) Workers should accept any job the market offered, regardless of the working conditions or
pay involved;
(2) Any person who would not or could not find work should be given just barely enough to
prevent physical starvation; and
(3) The dole given to such a person should be substantially lower than the lowest wage offered
in the market.




                                                                                              15
It is also very clear that Senior wanted his theory to be seriously considered by those who had
decision-making power in the most important social, political, and moral issues of his era.


Senior's Theoretical Methodology
Senior believed that so much controversy existed in theories of political economy because
economists had concerned themselves with social welfare rather than merely analyzing wealth.
When one considered social welfare, one was immediately involved in normative or ethical
statements reflecting the positions of varying contending groups involved in social conflicts. It
was thus inevitable that intellectual conflicts would arise. Ethical statements, Senior declared,
were not subject to either scientific confirmation or disproof. Therefore, as long as they
remained a part of economic theorizing, scientific advancement could never result in
agreement among theoreticians. If political economy was to become a science, it was first
necessary to eliminate all of the unscientific, ethical premises contained within it. After these
were eliminated, a few clearly established empirical principles of economic life would remain.
Then, using deductive logic, economists would be able to explore scientifically all of the
theoretical and practical implications of these few empirically substantiated principles.
It is also very clear that Senior wanted his theory to be seriously considered by those who had
decision-making power in the most important social, political, and moral issues of his era.

Fedric Bastiat
Scope of Fedric Bastiat utilitarian Economics
Claude Frédéric Bastiat (1801 — 1850) is one of the greatest economists ever. His role as
organizer of the French, and inspiration of the nineteenth- century continental European
free-trade movement is not controversial, and all historians recognize him as a great
pamphleteer — some even calling him "the most brilliant economic journalist who ever lived."

Books of Bastiat:
     1. Harmonies of political economy
In the book, Bastiat writes of "the measureless disproportion" between what each of us
contributes to society and what each of us receives in return. The American economist Amasa
Walker commented that, "Of all the writers on the subject, no one seems to have been more
full and clear in the definition and illustration of value" than Bastiat, in the distinction he draws
in Economic Harmonies between value and utility.Bastiat dedicated Harmonies of Political
Economy to the youth of France.

    2. The Law
The Law (French: La Loi) is an 1850 book by Frédéric Bastiat. It was written at Mugron two years
after the third French Revolution and a few months before his death of tuberculosis at age 49.
the work for which Bastiat is most famous, followed by the candlemaker's petition and the
parable of the broken window.

Harmony and equilibrium:
Exchange is based precisely on the diversity, or, if you prefer, on the inequalities of fertility, skill,
climate, and temperature, that you are seeking to eliminate…. At the race track, if one of the
horses wins the prize, the other loses it; but when two horses work to produce something
useful, each will produce an amount in proportion to his strength; and although the stronger
will render the greater service, it does not follow that the weaker will render none at all.

Bastiat's case for the free market was however not only based on refutations of individual
government interventions, but also on a general refutation of the common premise of all
proposals for government action. He brilliantly argued that all these proposals involved some
variant of the claim that the free market by its very nature antagonizes the interests of certain
individuals or groups. This is the great subject of his unfinished Economic Harmonies. In the
preface, which he addressed to the Youth of France, Bastiat (1851, p. 3; my translation) stressed
that



                                                                                                      17
What sets the various socialist schools… radically apart from the school of the economists is
not this or that question of detail…; it is the point of departure, it is this preliminary and
paramount question: Are human interests, left to themselves, harmonious or antagonistic?

A brief look at the history of twentieth-century economic thought confirms Bastiat's insight into
the common denominator of interventionist schemes. The most important economic
justifications for government intervention stressed the existence of business cycles, monopoly,
public goods, and unemployment. In each of these cases, the perceived problem was claimed to
be a market failure, which is nothing else but the claim that the problem under consideration
springs from the very nature of the market. The market cannot possibly solve it, at any rate, it
cannot solve it as well as the government, the great deus ex machina, which is therefore
necessary to bring relief.

True to Bastiat's spirit, many Austrian and a few mainstream economists have again and again
rebutted these twentieth-century allegations of market failure case by case. These rebuttals
would certainly gain in strength if they were combined with a more general attack on the fallacy
underlying all these individual cases. And when it comes to such endeavors, present-day
economists will benefit very much from the careful study of Bastiat's doctrine of economic
harmonies.

It is noteworthy that Bastiat's economic-harmonies argument contrasts sharply with the typical
twentieth-century argument for the free market, inspired by Léon Walras's work, according to
which the market would reach or tend to reach equilibrium, or that it would maximize or tend
to maximize social utility. Thus consider how, some decades later, Louis Rougier (1938, p. 70; my
translation) puts the scientific case for the free market:

Economic science demonstrates that the maximum satisfaction for trading partners is realized in
a regime of free competition. In the exchange of one single product, the free play of demand
and supply alone permits to derive the equilibrium price from the great number of individual
bargains — the price for which the quantity of a product offered on a market will be best sold in
its entirety, given a certain distribution of purchasing power among the members of society at a
given point of time.

Utility and exchange :

Bastiat recognized utility as the driving force behind human action in the economic realm.
Utility refers to the satisfaction or benefit that individuals derive from consuming goods and
services. Bastiat argued that people act to maximize their utility, seeking to satisfy their wants


                                                                                               18
and needs. This concept of utility is foundational to understanding why individuals engage in
economic activities such as production, consumption, and exchange.Exchange: Bastiat
emphasized the importance of exchange in facilitating the satisfaction of human desires and
needs. He viewed exchange as a voluntary transaction between individuals, where each party
expects to benefit. Bastiat believed that exchange occurs because individuals have different
skills, resources, and preferences, creating opportunities for mutually beneficial trade. He
argued that exchange allows people to specialize in what they do best and to access a wider
range of goods and services than they could produce on their own.Mutual Benefit: Bastiat
contended that voluntary exchange leads to mutual benefit and overall societal welfare. When
individuals freely exchange goods and services, both parties involved in the transaction are
better off than they were before. Bastiat's emphasis on mutual benefit underscored his support
for free trade and opposition to government intervention in economic affairs. He believed that
individuals, pursuing their self-interest through voluntary exchange, contribute to the prosperity
of society as a whole.Government Intervention: Bastiat criticized government intervention in
the economy, particularly measures such as tariffs, subsidies, and regulations that restrict free
exchange. He argued that such interventions distort the natural workings of the market, leading
to inefficiency, waste, and reduced welfare. Bastiat's advocacy for free trade and limited
government intervention reflected his belief in the power of voluntary exchange to promote
economic progress and improve living standards.

Opposition to Protectionism

He argued that protectionist policies, such as tariffs and trade barriers, distorted the natural
flow of trade and hindered economic prosperity. Bastiat famously explained the concept of
opportunity cost through his "broken window fallacy," illustrating that protectionist measures
might protect certain industries in the short term but ultimately lead to inefficiency, higher
prices for consumers, and overall economic stagnation. He advocated for free trade as a means
to promote specialization, competition, and innovation, which he believed would ultimately
benefit society as a whole.

Frederic Bastiat's defense of private property, capital, profits, and rent

    1. Private property :
Bastiat argued that private property is essential for human flourishing and economic progress.
He believed that individuals have a natural right to own and control property, and that respect
for private property rights is necessary to prevent conflict and promote cooperation in society.
He saw private property as a fundamental aspect of personal freedom and individual
responsibility.


                                                                                               19
   2. Capital :
He recognized the importance of capital accumulation for economic growth and prosperity. He
emphasized the role of savings and investment in increasing the productivity of labor and
expanding the overall wealth of society. Bastiat advocated for policies that encourage capital
formation, such as low taxes, minimal government regulation, and secure property rights.

Profits: Bastiat viewed profits as a legitimate reward for entrepreneurship, risk-taking, and
innovation. He argued that profits serve as signals in the market, indicating where resources are
most valued and where opportunities for improvement exist. Bastiat rejected the notion that
profits are inherently exploitative, instead seeing them as a vital incentive for individuals to
engage in productive activities that benefit society as a whole

    3. Rent:
Bastiat recognized rent as the payment for the use of land or other natural resources. He
believed that rent should be determined by voluntary exchange in a competitive market,
reflecting the relative scarcity and desirability of different locations or resources. Bastiat
opposed government intervention in rent-setting, arguing that such interference distorts market
signals and hampers economic efficiency.



Role of government :
classical liberal thinker Bastiat believed that the role of government should be limited to
protecting individuals' life, liberty, and property. He argued that government intervention
beyond this scope often leads to unintended consequences and distortions in the economy.
Bastiat famously articulated this perspective in his essay "The Law," where he criticized
government policies that violated these principles, such as protectionism and wealth
redistribution. He advocated for a free-market economy where individuals are free to pursue
their own interests without interference from the state.

William Stanley Jevons
JEVONS’ THEORY OF MARGINAL UTILITY AND EXCHANGE

William Stanley Jevons, a prominent economist from the 19th century, made significant
contributions to economic theory, particularly through his magnum opus, the Theory of Political
Economy, published in 1871. Within this seminal work, Jevons set the stage for a new way of
looking at utilitarianism in economics. He focused on the idea of "marginal utility" and showed
how this concept helps us to understand economic behavior and exchange.



Utilitarian Foundation:

Jevons strongly supported utilitarianism as the core of scientific economic theory. He believed
that the value of things depended entirely on how useful they were. He emphasized the
importance of thinking about the economy in terms of people's pleasure and pain, taking
inspiration from Bentham's thoughts.

Exchange Value:

The ultimate truth for Jevons’ was that value depends entirely upon utility. Jevons defined value
exclusively in terms of ‘exchange value’ or ‘price’, rejecting labor theories of value. He directed
his attention solely towards prices, advocating for a market-centric economic analysis.

Economic Agents:

People, in Jevon's view, had only 2 characteristics that defined them as economic agents:

1. The first characteristic was that they derived utility from consuming commodities.
2. The second characteristic was that every person was a rational, calculating maximizer. And
rational, calculating, maximizing behavior was the only element of human action to be studied
by economics.

Jevons postulated that individuals are distinguished by their utility-maximizing behavior and
derive satisfaction from consuming commodities. Rational, calculating maximization governs
economic decision-making.

Marginal Utility:


                                                                                                21
Jevons introduced the concept of marginal utility, distinguishing between total utility (derived
from the consumption of a given quantity of some commodity) and the ‘final degree of utility’
(derived from consuming the last increment of a commodity).

Although total utility might continue to become greater as one consumes an increased quantity
of a commodity, the final degree of utility ultimately decreases as that quantity increases. It was
this “final degrees of utility” or marginal utility, that concerned Jevons.

The principle of diminishing marginal utility became foundational in neoclassical economics.

Mathematical Exposition:

By introducing the concept of "marginalism" to utilitarian economics, Jevons discovered a
method to translate the idea that people act rationally to maximize their gains into
mathematical terms.

If the total utility one received consuming a commodity depended on the quantity consumed,
then this could be written as a mathematical function, TU = f(Q). Here, TU denotes total utility
and Q represents the quantity consumed.

In calculus, the first derivative of a function tells one how much the dependent variable
(In this case, total utility) changes as a consequence of an infinitesimally small change in the
independent variable (in this case, the quantity consumed).
The first derivative of the total utility function gives one the marginal utility at any particular
quantity consumed.

Jevons employed calculus to formalize the logic of maximization, explaining how marginal utility
determines optimal consumption levels. Total utility function was maximized when the quantity
was increased to that point at which marginal utility was equal to zero. It simply meant that to
maximize one's utility from consuming a particular commodity, one should consume the
commodity until one was satiated, that is, until one could derive no more utility from another
small increment of the commodity.

Equilibrium in Exchange:

When consumption involves cost, those costs can be stated in mathematical form. For example,
if one possessed a commodity ‘y’ and could only get another commodity ‘x’ by giving up some


                                                                                                22
‘y’ in exchange, then one could compare the reaction of one's marginal utilities for the two
commodities, (MUx / MUy) and the prices of two commodities, (Px / Py).

If (MUx / MUy) > (Px / Py), then one could gain utility by treading some of his ‘y’ for some ‘x’.
The process would continue until the Individual exhausted the gain from exchange, he would
have traded to the point at which (MUx / MUy) = (Px / Py).

To put the same thing differently, the ratio (MUx / Px) would tell one how much additional
utility one could get or give up, if one purchased or sold an additional dollar’s worth of
commodity ‘x’. The two individuals would, Jevons asserted, buy and sell the commodities with
the marginal utilities of each commodity, changed to the point where (MUx / Px) = (MUy / Py).

At that point, the last dollar's worth of either ‘x’ or ‘y’ yielded the same increment to the
individual's total utility. This is referred as the ‘Equimarginal Principle’



William Stanley Jevons' Theory of Marginal Utility and Exchange changed our thoughts about
economics by combining utilitarian principles with mathematical analysis. His emphasis on
marginal utility, rational behavior, and market equilibrium set the stage for modern neoclassical
economics, shaping our comprehension of consumer choice, exchange dynamics, and resource
allocation. Jevons' work remains a key part of economic theory, influencing both academic
study and policy-making.

Carl Menger
Menger’s Theory of Marginal Utility, Prices & Income Distribution

Carl Menger was born on 23 February, 1840 in Neu-Sandec, Galicia (now Poland). He was a
prominent Austrian economist. He is renowned for developing the marginal utility theory.
Menger received his Ph.D from Jagiellonian University in 1867 and later became a professor of
Political Economy at University of Vienna. Among Menger's most important discoveries are the
solutions to the diamond-water paradox, which show that products have value because of their
multiple, varying applications, and the debunking of the labor theory of value, which shows that
goods' worth comes from meeting human needs rather than labor input.

The book "Principles of Economics," written by Carl Menger and published in 1871, is his most
famous contribution. It provided noble concepts that contradicted traditional economic
theories and served as the basis for the Austrian School of economics. He also made important
contributions in methodology, some of them are included in the English translation titled
‘Problems of Economics and Sociology’. In his economic theory, he rejected the use of
mathematical equations and expressed his theories verbally through numerical examples.

According to Menger, when a person equals the marginal utility obtained from any one

Menger's explanation of how prices are determined was better than Jeavon's. Menger used

prices of consumer goods as well as the prices of the factors of production (land, labor. capital).

creating consumer goods and the utility that consumers receive from using them drove their
demand. As a result, in this perspective, wages, rent and profits were at least partially
determined by the prices of consumer goods. There was a noticeable distinction between the
two perspectives on the pricing procedure. Menger outperformed Jeavon in this pricing
procedure because of the way he used his utility theory framework to explain it.

Menger provided an explanation for the demand for consumer goods by demonstrating that
most consumers would obtain greater utility by holding onto their money rather than spending

purchase depended on the price of the commodity, and the quantity demanded and the price
are inversely related to each other.” However, Menger’s supply explanation was less adequate.
Typically, he viewed supply as an amount that was already in the seller's possession. The vendor

Menger divided all sorts of goods into two categories:
  1. “Goods of first order” which refers to the commodities produced for consumption. and,
  2. “Good of higher order” which means the factors of production.

He tried to establish a relationship between these two types of goods .First, in order to

Regarding this, he thought that "among the most egregious of the fundamental errors that have

Menger’s Argument on Methodology:
His initial claim was that "pure science" was invariably devoid of value. He thought that
normative, moral, or ethical principles had no place in science. People's values were shaped by
their experiences, emotions, and social class; as a result, there was never much chance of broad
consensus on moral issues. But science was the study and depiction of reality as it is, not as one
would like it to be. Which means a science economist should be value free and he believed that
ethical judgements had no place in pure economic theory. He wrote the so-called “ethical
orientation of political economy’ was vague and confusing.
Menger's second methodological tenet was that only individual families or business firms ,what
he dubbed "individual economies" could be scientifically understood by economists. They were
never able to arrive at a comparable scientific comprehension of social aggregates like nations

Leon Walras
Walras’s Theory of General Economic Equilibrium

Similar to Jevons and Menger, Walras independently developed the theory of diminishing
marginal utility and discussed the equation that gave the maximum utility a consumer could
achieve through exchange. Walras’s most important and lasting contribution to economic
theory was his theory of general economic equilibrium.

Although most previous economic theorists have discussed interrelationships between different
markets, no economists prior to Walras set out a general conceptual and theoretical structure
within which to examine the multiplicity of relationships among different markets. He realized
that the forces of supply and demand in any one particular market depended in varying degrees
on the prices prevailing in innumerable other markets.

In a general theory of price determination, all prices would have to be determined
simultaneously by both the total of all consumer utilities and the interrelations that existed
among all markets. Such interrelationships did not exist only in the demand for consumer
goods. They also existed in the supply of consumer goods, as well as in the demands and
supplies of other types of commodities or assets that were exchanged. Walras attempted to
formulate a general theoretical framework within which he could show how, through the
interactions of all markets, all prices could be determined simultaneously.

The institutional setting of Walras’s theory was competitive capitalism in which “there are
land-owners, workers and capitalists.” These three classes functioned in two economically
important ways:

  i.   First, as owners of productive services (land, labor, and capital), they supplied these
       services in the market.
 ii.   Second, as consumers, they demanded consumer goods in the market.




                                                                                           29
Taking the existing distribution of “productive services” and different people's various roles in
the production process, the most important element within the socioeconomic context was, for
Walras, people’s “subjective desires” or their schedules of marginal utilities.

There were three important institutional factors in Walras’s theory:

  i.    The acceptance of the existing laws of and distribution of property as morally right and
        just. Here he simply stated that
        “The ownership of property… is legalized appropriation, or appropriation in conformity
        with justice.”
  ii.   He assumed that the economy was made up exclusively of small, relatively powerless
        business firms, and perfect competition prevailed. Although he realized that “the
        principle of free competition is not generally applicable…[to] monopolies”, he ignored
        monopolies entirely throughout his general equilibrium discussion.
 iii.   People have simply assumed to have measurable marginal utility schedules. There was
        no discussion of how their utility schedules came into existence or how they changed
        overtime. He simply assumed that during the time period under analysis “the utility…
        remains fixed for such party”, Thus utility was a metaphysically given, ultimate datum, in
        terms of which everything was to be explained.

After assuming the existence of an existing distribution of ownership, a perfectly competitive
market and metaphysically given, fixed utility schedules, Walras developed his system of
equations to show what quantities of the various commodities were exchanged and how their
prices were determined.

Walras’s law is really a definitional identity which states that with any given set up prices, the
total demand for all things must equal the total supply of all things exchanged.Therefore, every
individual demand is simultaneously a supply of the same magnitude, and so if these individual
demands and supplies are aggregated, the totals must be equal.
