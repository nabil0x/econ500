Classical Macroeconomics: Classical Monetary and Employment
Theory
The macroeconomic theories now must widely be accepted among economists constitute a marriage
of two quite diverse approaches: one commonly called Keynesian, the other Classical (1776-1930s):
the former is the product of the 1930s and the latter is an accumulation and refinement of ideas
developed during the previous century and a half. Such a marriage of Keynesian and classical
elements- sometimes described as Keynesian-classical synthesis (Samuelson termed it the
neoclassical synthesis model). Nevertheless, for purposes of presentation of this synthesis, it has
been found useful to present separately, and in its starkest form, the basic elements of the classical
theory without any Keynesian admixture.

The main body of classical economics was microeconomic in character. It dealt with the
determination of (relative) prices for and quantities of individual goods and services, the
determination of (relative) incomes to individual factors of production, and the determination of
(relative) prices of classes of assets, real and financial. Its principal explicit macroeconomic subject
was the determination of the money price level, which, however, was believed to have essentially no
significance for relative prices and incomes.

The reconstructed classical macroeconomics stands on three pillars:

    1. Say’s Law
    2. The Quantity Theory of Money
    3. Perfect Wage-Price Flexibility


    1. Say’s Law
One of the few explicitly macroeconomic propositions of classical theory was Say’s Law of Markets
(named after J.B. Say, the French economist, 1767-1832). Say’s law is usually summarized as “Supply
creates its own demand”. If goods are produced, there will automatically be a market for them. This
can be easily be seen to be true in a barter economy, although its application was not supposed to be
limited to that circumstance. What Say appears to have had in mind can be expressed in this way:
people work not for its own shake but only to obtain goods and services that yield satisfactions. The
very act of production constitutes the demand for other goods, a demand equivalent to the value of
the surplus goods each man produces. How can there ever be a general overproduction of goods?
Each man’s production (supply) constitutes his demand for other goods; hence the AD must in some
sense equal to AS. What Say’s law implies is that any increment of output will generate an equivalent
increase in income and in spending. Thus, income and product can always be at a ‘full-employment’
level.

When Say’s law is framed in terms of a barter economy, it seems self-evident. But does it also true
for an economy using money? Can we be sure that the money received will be promptly and fully
spent against other goods? We shall see latter while discussing the monetary theory contemporary
with Say’s law did explain how and why money’s role was solely that of a ‘veil’, behind which
everything operated in the same way as if all exchanges took the form of barter. But this result
depends on many specific assumptions about behavior, which might be subject to challenge.

The elementary formulation of Say’s law also paid no attention to the phenomenon of saving and
investment. Thus, to understand the entire basis for Say’s law in a real-world economy- which was
money, not barter; in which most people sell labor services, not final product; and in which people
save and invest- we need to look at a whole range of classical ideas. The first of these basic classical
ideas is the “quantity theory of money”.


    2. The Quantity Theory of Money
The quantity theory asserts that money determines only the price level, not real output. The root
idea of the quantity theory is that no rational person holds money idle, for it produces nothing and
yields no satisfaction. Rather, people promptly use all the cash they receive from the sale their goods
and services to buy other goods and services. How promptly that occurs depends on how production
is organized, how frequently incomes are paid, and other structural or institutional factors that were
judged to be quite independent of the quantity of money or the level of prices. Given these
assumptions, the theory showed how the quantity of money determines the level of money prices,
and in a way which is without impact on the demand or supply of any individual product, and thus
cannot affect relative prices nor the absolute quantities of products produced and sold. This, it
cannot affect aggregate production, either.

The quantity theory of money was discussed by the famous 18th century philosopher David Hume
and has been advocated more recently by the prominent economist Milton Friedman. Most
economists rely on this theory for explaining the long-run determinants of the price level and the
inflation rate. The value of money (like any other commodity) is determined by the supply and
demand for money. Formally, we can state the quantity theory in the following equation:

    3. Perfect Wage-Price flexibility: Employment & Production
We saw earlier that classical macroeconomics maintained that 𝑃 × 𝑌 is determined by the money
supply M. With a given stock of money, aggregate price times real GDP is a constant. More can be
sold only at proportionally lower prices. If prices are flexible, they will fall whenever there are idle
resources; this fall in prices somehow automatically absorb these idle resources and expand
economy’s output. Here we examine how price flexibility promotes full employment in an economy
in which people work for wages, rather than supply final goods directly to the market.

Note that, we have divided the above analysis into two parts- a “real” and a “monetary”. The real
wage and the level of employment and output are determined by “real” factors alone; the marginal
productivity of labor and marginal disutility of labor. Money wages and prices are determined solely
by monetary factors. Changes in the “real” side factors can affect prices and wages, but changes on
the monetary side have no effect on the “real” magnitudes. This separation of variables into these
two parts is called the classical dichotomy. This presumably justifies the classical tradition of
developing the theory of output and employment entirely in real terms (Say’s law), leaving the
theory of absolute prices to a separate treatise on monetary theory.

The essential conclusions of the “classical model” are valid so long as we assume perfect wage-price
flexibility, and money demanded only as a medium of exchanges.

        NB: This makes it clear that the conclusions of the classical model do not rest on the existence
        of an aggregate production function, a diminishing marginal product of labor, profit
        maximization, nor the existence of perfect competition in all product markets.

The classical analytical tools have weaknesses which make them not fully suitable for application to
public policy. Of their logical completeness and consistency, however, there can be no doubt. This
reconstructed “Classical theory” cannot be attacked except by attacking the behavioral or technical
assumptions on which it is based. Keynes was surely wrong in sometimes attacking it as logically
incomplete as inconsistent.
