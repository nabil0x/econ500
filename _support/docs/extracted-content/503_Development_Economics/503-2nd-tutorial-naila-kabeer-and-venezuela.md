1.Why is patriarchy a classical phenomena in Bangladesh? Explain what are the
indicator's of outlier of Bangladesh and why they exist?

2.Why are there changes in the Agency although no revolutionary changes are

Answer 1: Patriarchy as a Classical Phenomenon & Bangladesh as an Outlier

Patriarchy in Bangladesh is considered a classical phenomenon because it shares the structural features
of the “belt of classic patriarchy” identified in South Asia and the Middle East. These include patrilineal
inheritance, patrilocal residence, male dominance, restricted female mobility, and strong son
preference . Women’s security traditionally depends on sons, which leads to high fertility and gender
discrimination, reinforcing patriarchal norms. . Such structures historically limited women’s agency and
economic participation.

However, Bangladesh is identified as a “positive outlier” because it achieved significant social progress
despite these constraints. Indicators of this outlier status include:

   1.​ Economic dimension: Improved health, education, and gender equality despite low per capita
       GDP .
   2.​ Political dimension: Social progress despite weak governance, corruption, and clientelism .
   3.​ Cultural dimension: Advancements in women’s status despite conservative religious influences .

Additional empirical indicators include declining fertility, improved life expectancy, reduced infant
mortality, and disappearance of gender gaps in child mortality . Unlike India, Bangladesh did not adopt
sex-selective abortion, maintaining a balanced sex ratio .

These outcomes exist due to policy support, NGO-state collaboration, and expansion of women’s
economic roles, which enhanced women’s agency. Thus, while patriarchy remains structurally embedded,
Bangladesh’s development trajectory challenges conventional theories by demonstrating that social
progress can occur within, not after, patriarchy .

Answer 2: Change in Agency without Revolutionary Transformation

Changes in women’s agency in Bangladesh have occurred without revolutionary or abrupt
transformation because they are embedded within existing patriarchal structures rather than replacing
them. The process is best understood as incremental and negotiated change, not structural rupture.

The evidence shows that women increasingly participate in decision-making, education, and economic
activities. They make fertility decisions, invest in children, and engage in paid work, which gradually
enhances their bargaining power within households . This improves their influence over resource allocation
and reduces discrimination against daughters .

However, these changes occur through “patriarchal bargains”, where women do not directly challenge
patriarchy but negotiate within its constraints for better outcomes . For example, earlier strategies
emphasized son preference for security, but with new opportunities (education, employment), dependence
on sons declines and gender norms gradually shift .

The key reason for non-revolutionary change is that social norms, kinship systems, and cultural
practices remain intact, shaping the limits of agency. As noted, “structures shape women’s choices, but
do not fully determine them” . Therefore, women exercise agency within boundaries, leading to gradual
transformation in gender relations, fertility behavior, and social attitudes.
Thus, agency evolves through cumulative everyday actions, producing significant outcomes (e.g., fertility
decline, improved gender equality) without dismantling patriarchy. This explains why Bangladesh’s
transformation is evolutionary rather than revolutionary.



Question: Venezuela is not just an economic collapse, it is a lesson on governance
and diversification.

Venezuela’s economic collapse is often described as one of the worst in modern history, but it is more than
just an economic failure—it is a clear lesson about the importance of governance and economic
diversification. Once considered one of the richest countries in Latin America, Venezuela built its prosperity
on vast oil reserves. The discovery of oil in the early 20th century transformed the economy and made
petroleum the dominant source of national income. Oill was nationalized in 1976 and became the backbone
of the economy, leading to heavy dependence on a single sector . While this brought short-term wealth, it
created long-term vulnerability. Instead of using oil revenue to develop other sectors such as agriculture,
manufacturing, or technology, the country remained overly reliant on oil exports. This lack of diversification
meant that when global oil prices fell—especially after 2014—the entire economy was severely affected.
Government revenues declined sharply, imports became difficult, and economic stability quickly collapsed.

However, the crisis cannot be explained by oil dependence alone; governance failures played an even
more critical role. Over time, Venezuela experienced weak institutions, corruption, and poor economic
management. Governments relied heavily on oil income to fund public spending, often without proper
planning or savings for the future. Policies such as price controls, excessive nationalization, and
over-regulation discouraged private investment and reduced productivity. During the leadership of Hugo
Chávez and later Nicolás Maduro, social welfare programs expanded, but they were largely funded by oil
revenue rather than sustainable economic growth . When oil income declined, the government struggled to
maintain these programs and began printing money to cover deficits, leading to hyperinflation. As a result,
the value of the national currency collapsed, prices skyrocketed, and basic goods like food and medicine
became scarce. Political instability also increased, with growing tensions between the government and
opposition, further weakening economic confidence and governance structures . This shows that even with
abundant natural resources, poor governance can lead to economic disaster.

The combined effect of weak governance and lack of diversification turned Venezuela’s economic crisis into
a broader social and humanitarian crisis. Millions of people faced unemployment, poverty, and shortages of
essential goods, forcing many to migrate to other countries in search of better opportunities. What makes
Venezuela’s case particularly important is that it demonstrates the concept of the “resource curse”—where
countries rich in natural resources fail to achieve sustainable development due to mismanagement and
overdependence. The lesson for students and policymakers is very clear: natural resources alone cannot
guarantee prosperity. A stable and transparent system of governance is necessary to manage resources
effectively, while diversification of the economy helps protect against external shocks such as price
fluctuations in global markets. In conclusion, Venezuela is not just an example of economic collapse; it is a
powerful warning. It teaches that long-term development depends on strong institutions, responsible
leadership, and a balanced economic structure. Without these, even the wealthiest nations can fall into
crisis.
