                               Export Orientation Model (EOM)

Export-led growth has been a key part of the Washington Consensus, but this heavy focus on
exports and trade liberalization has caused several problems for developing countries:

• It shifted attention away from building strong domestic markets.

• It forced developing countries into harmful competition with each other to offer the lowest
costs.

• It created tension between workers in poor and rich countries.

• It contributed to global issues like overproduction and falling prices.

While exporting is still important—especially to earn money for importing needed
goods—making it the main policy goal can actually slow down real development.

The main theoretical problem with relying too much on export-led growth is that it assumes
every country can grow by selling to others. But if all countries try this at the same time, it can
lead to a global problem where no one has enough demand, resulting in too much supply and
lower prices. Export growth might help one country grow, but if it just shifts demand from one
place to another, it doesn't help the world economy grow overall.



                               Criticism By Thomas Palley

1. Export Displacement: To explain Palley's point of view let's use the textile Industry as an
example. Export destination of this industries include- US, UK, Western Europe, Japan, Canada,
Austrelia, And Exporting countries include, China, Bangladesh, Vietnam, India, Pakistan, Sri-
Lanka, Myanmar, Eastern Africa (Senegal, Ethiopia). An important matter to be noted is that the
list of exporting countries are langer than the destination and mone countries are joining the
exporting list, as countries with cheaper labors find it lucrative to join the export industry,
becoming rivals of each-other. But the demand for textile to more or less constant. So,
Exporting countries have to offer either cheaper goods or better quality. Both options are not
desirable for profit maximizing exporters. If one country manages t increase their exports, it is
often done by crowding out the exports of another developing country.

2. Race to the Bottom: If one country wanted to keep their profit margin the same, they had to
lower their production costs. This reduction of cost came in the form of lowering wage of
laborers, increasing working hours, skipping on ETP regulations, overtime, decreasing vacations,
benefits and maternity leave.

That is, to stay competitive in global markets, countries may cut costs by reducing work
conditions and environmental standards. This leads companies to lower these requirements or
shift production to countries with fewer regulations. While this was once seen as mainly a
problem between the developed and developing countries, it is now clear that the developing
countries competition is also significant. For example, the Pakistani soccer ball industry, which
eliminated child labor, found that production just moved to India, where child labor laws were
less strict.

3. Declining "Terms Of Trade", Financial Instability & Excess Capacity: A second problem with
export-led growth is that it worsens the terms of trade for developing countries. As these
countries push more goods and commodities into global markets, prices tend to fall. This
forces them to export even more to earn the same income, which drives prices down
further—creating a vicious cycle.

This problem leads to financial instability. First, as export prices fall, it becomes harder for
countries to pay back their foreign debts, which are usually in strong foreign currencies. Loans
were taken from Banks (tax-payer's money) to finance the infrastructure development of export-
oriented industries. With degrading terms of trade, industries fail to generate revenue and
default on loans. Moreover, too much investment in export manufacturing can lead to
overproduction. The net result was too much production capacity, which made these
investments unprofitable.

4. Autonomy, dependency, quality of development: A fourth issue with export-led growth is that
it can limit a country's independence and lead to poor-quality development. When countries
focus heavily on exports, especially through export-processing zones, it often results in weak
connections to the rest of the economy. This model resembles the old "plantation"
system—where workers are exploited, wages stay low, and the local economy doesn't benefit
much. As a result, domestic markets remain underdeveloped, and long-term, self-sustaining
growth is hard to achieve. Instead, these countries become heavily dependent on foreign
demand for their exports.

This dependency also increases the risk of global economic instability. According to portfolio
theory, a more balanced global economy with multiple independent growth centers is more
stable. But if many developing countries rely on the same export markets in developed countries,
a slowdown in those markets can trigger a global downturn.



                              Criticism By Robert Blecker

1. Growth Of Export Market and Expoters: The basic flow of a global export-led growth Strategy
is that, under a given set of global demand Conditions, the market for developing country
exports of manufactures is limited by the capacity (and willingness) of the industrialized nations
to absorb the corresponding imports.

NIC→ Newly Industrilized Country: The market for imports of labor-intensive manufactures and
other NIC exports can only grow so fast, and as a result the export-led strategy can work only
for a grow limited number of countries at a time. If this market is growing at say 7% per year,
then not all of the NICs can have their exports increase at rates of 15%-10% (market shares of
other exporters on domestic producers are falling).

Reasons for Growth:

1. Population Growth.

2. Consumenism.

3. Cultural Change.

4. Aggressive Marketing Strategy.



2. The Fragile financial State of the Exporters: If all exporters try to grow faster including new-
comers, the industry will see oversupply and prices will fall. If a country's export performance is
dissapointing, they will suffer economic stagnation and become more prone to a currency
collapse on financial crisis - especially if they attempt to paper over their growth showdown with
international borrowing that creates a fragile financial position and makes the currency
overvalued.



3. Total export increase/expand more than the domestic demand of the rich countries:
Exporter countries as a group increase their productive capacity and lower their average costs,
& thus permitting them to take away market share from domestic producers in the importing
countries. In this respect, the ability of all developing country exporters to increase, their total
market share abroad is limited by two factor:

i) Protectionist policies of importers.

ii) Eventual disappearance of local producers which then constrains total import growth to the
growth rate of the domestic market in the industrialized countries.

Simplified Explanation: When developing countries expand their exports faster than the demand
is growing in rich countries, they build more factories and lower production costs. This helps
them sell more and compete with local producers in those rich countries.

However, there are two main limits to how much developing countries can grow their exports:

• Protectionist policies in rich countries (like tariffs or quotas) can block more imports.

• If local producers in rich countries shut down, imports may stop growing too—because the
total market size isn't expanding fast enough.
