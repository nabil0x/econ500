Problems With Garment Industry.

A most important concern issue of Export Oriented Model ia the autonomy, the quality of
development, and dependency. Here, the argument is that export-led growth, especially when
associated with export-processing zones, leads to shallow development with weak linkages into
the rest of the economy.

It ends up looking a lot like the old “plantation” model—where workers are exploited and income
gains aren't widely shared. As a result, domestic markets remain underdeveloped, and countries
become heavily dependent on foreign demand for their exports. This makes them vulnerable
when global markets slow down.

It also increases global economic instability. If many countries depend on the same export
markets—mainly developed nations—then a slowdown in those markets can trigger a wider
global downturn. Ideally, if growth were happening independently in multiple regions, a
slowdown in one wouldn’t cause a global issue.

Looking ahead, the flaws in this system could get worse. Export-led growth might work well for
the first few countries that try it, but it becomes ineffective when too many jump in. China’s rise
is especially problematic because of its vast, low-wage labor force, which will likely remain for
years. Other developing countries now struggle to compete with China on cost, meaning they
can’t easily join the export-led model. Without enough demand and with China dominating
supply, the whole system may no longer work.



Example From Bangladesh's Garment Industry.

Although Bangladesh's garment industry is the largest source of export earnings, its connection
with the local economy is relatively weak. This can be explained as follows:

1. Dependence on Imported Raw Materials: Most of the raw materials used in the garment
industry—such as fabric, dyes, buttons, and zippers—are imported. As a result, opportunities for
local raw material production are limited, and reliance on domestic industries does not develop.
Almost 50% of the profit margin goes to the countries from where raw materials are imported.

2. Lack of Supporting Industries: Many supporting sectors related to the garment industry (e.g.,
textiles, accessories, design, logistics) are not yet fully developed. This gap contributes to the
weak linkage between the industry and the local economy.

3. Primarily Export-Oriented: Around 80–90% of the products from the garment industry are
exported. Domestic use is minimal, which limits the industry's integration with local consumer
demand and market cycles.

4. Dependence on Low-Wage Labor: Most workers in this sector earn low wages, and their
income is often insufficient to stimulate significant consumption or investment within the local
economy. Therefore, the productivity of the sector does not have the desired impact on national
economic growth.

5. Profit Repatriation Abroad: Many garment factories have partial or full foreign ownership. As
a result, a portion of the profits is repatriated overseas, which further limits the benefits to the
local economy.

In summary, while the garment industry plays a vital role in exports, its reliance on foreign raw
materials, technology, and markets has hindered the development of strong and sustainable
linkages with the local economy.

Alternative Industries With Strong Backward & Forward Linkage.

Several industries in Bangladesh offer stronger backward and forward linkages with the local
economy compared to the RMG (Ready-Made Garments) sector. These industries not only
source inputs locally but also stimulate downstream markets and services. Here are some
notable examples:

1. Agro-processing Industry.

Backward Linkage: Strong ties with local agriculture (fruits, vegetables, dairy, fish, grains).

Forward Linkage: Supplies processed foods to domestic markets, exports, and supports retail
and hospitality sectors.

Examples: Frozen foods, fruit juices, dairy products, spices.



2. Leather and Footwear.
Backward Linkage: Uses locally available raw hides and skins.

Forward Linkage: Produces finished leather goods, shoes, bags, etc., for both export and local
markets.

Notes: Government-identified as a priority sector with high value addition potential.



3. Information and Communication Technology (ICT).

Backward Linkage: Employs local talent and training institutions.

Forward Linkage: Supports various sectors (banking, education, government, telecom) through
digital solutions.

Potential: Expanding role in digital transformation and outsourcing.



4. Jute Industry.

Backward Linkage:

• Heavily linked to local agriculture—raw jute is grown in various regions across the country.

• Provides seasonal employment to thousands of farmers.



Forward Linkage:

• Used in jute mills to produce yarn, sacks, bags, carpets, geo-textiles, and eco-friendly
packaging.

• Supports packaging for agricultural exports, food grains, and construction materials.

• Growing demand for biodegradable alternatives globally is reviving export potential.



5. Tea Industry.

Backward Linkage:

• Dependent on local plantations (mainly in Sylhet and Chittagong Hill Tracts).

• Provides rural employment, especially for women and ethnic minorities.

Forward Linkage:
• Serves a large domestic consumption market.

Supports retail, packaging, branding, and exports.

• Growing potential in value-added tea products (green tea, herbal blends).



6. Sugar Industry.

Backward Linkage:

• Linked directly to local sugarcane farming.

• Thousands of smallholder farmers depend on sugarcane cultivation to supply the mills.

• Provides employment in rural areas during planting and harvesting seasons.

Forward Linkage:

• Produces refined sugar for domestic consumption.

• By-products like keru, molasses, bagasse, and press mud have industrial and agricultural uses.
