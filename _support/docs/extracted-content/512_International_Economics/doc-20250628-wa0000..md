                                      World Bank/IMF

The World Bank, as part of its development finance operations, often attaches policy
conditionalities to its loans, especially in structural adjustment programs (SAPs). Conditions
aim to ensure that the borrower implements economic reforms that the Bank believes will lead
to long-term growth and financial stability.

Below is a discussion of the underlying motives and impacts of three commonly prescribed
policies:

1. Fiscal Discipline.

The conditions are to increase revenue by increasing VAT, to reduce public spending, to reduce
fiscal defficiencies, to increase export earnings.

Motive: The World Bank promotes fiscal discipline to ensure macroeconomic stability and debt
sustainability. The goal is to reduce budget deficits and control inflation, which are seen as
critical to maintaining investor confidence from abroad.

Fiscal discipline is also aimed at increasing the efficiency of public spending by reducing
wasteful expenditures and ensuring that government resources are used effectively. The main
incentive or agenda is to pay back the foreign loan.



Impact: While successful, fiscal discipline can lead to reduced inflation, lower interest rates, and
restored confidence in government institutions, It can also be harmful.

When VAT increases, low income consumers suffer most. In many developing countries, fiscal
tightening has led to cuts in essential public services, such as health, education, agricultural
sectors and subsidies for the poor. This can increase inequality and social unrest.

2. Removal of Trade Barriers/Promoting Free Trade.

The conditions are to reduce tariffs, reduce subsidies that deteriorate trade or pruce.

Motive: The World Bank advocates for trade liberalization to integrate developing countries into
the global economy. It is believed that the removal of tariffs, quotas and other trade restrictions
will increase economic efficiency, consumer choice & benefits and competitiveness.

By exposing domestic industries to international competition, it aims to improve productivity
and promote export-led growth.



Impact: In some countries, trade liberalization has led to economic growth, increased exports,
and reduced consumer prices.

However, in many cases, local industries have been unable to compete with cheaper imports
(low price/high value products), leading to factory closures, job losses, and de-industrialization.

Liberalization without strong institutions and safety nets has made some economies overly
dependent on a narrow range of exports or vulnerable to global price shocks. It can also leave a
country as import dependent.



3. Privatization of State-Owned Industries.

The conditions are to reduce the numbers of State Owned Enterprises (SOE) and privatize them.

Motive: The World Bank promotes privatization to improve efficiency, reduce fiscal burdens, and
encourage private sector development. It assumes that state-owned enterprises (SOEs) are
often inefficient due to political interference, corruption, and lack of competition. Privatization is
seen as a way to attract foreign investment and foster a competitive market economy. Although
the latent incentives work as that foreign loan should be paid and enhance the entry of foreign
companies (MNC's)



Impact: In cases where privatization was transparent and competitive, it has led to improved
services, increased investment, and more dynamic enterprise performance.

However, in many developing countries, privatization processes have been marred by corruption,
lack of transparency, and politically motivated sales.

Often, privatized companies downsize their workforce to cut costs, leading to mass
unemployment. Moreover, essential services (e.g., water, electricity) handed over to private
monopolies can result in price hikes and reduced access for the poor.

Privatization has also led to the concentration of wealth and power in the hands of a few elites
or foreign corporations.
