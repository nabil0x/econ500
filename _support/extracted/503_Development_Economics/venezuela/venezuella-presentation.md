## Slide 4

Spanish Conquest and Colonialization

Christopher Columbus visited in 1498; official colonization began around 1521.

Early colonization focused on mining, but the territory soon became a plantation-based economy.

In 1717, the Province of Venezuela was incorporated into the Viceroyalty of New Granada.

In 1777, the Captaincy General of Venezuela was created, uniting the provinces under one central authority in Caracas.

## Slide 6

Social Structure and Economy

Mantuanos:  White Creoles (descendants of Spanish settlers) formed the elite class, dominating the agricultural, social, and economic life, particularly through the ownership of cocoa plantations.

Economy:  Cocoa became the main export. The Guipuzcoan Company of Caracas held a strict monopoly on trade.

---

## Slide 7

Social Structure and Economy

Slavery:  African slaves were heavily imported to work on coastal plantations.

Slave Trade Volume: An estimated 101,000 enslaved Africans were disembarked in Venezuela, largely through the intra-American slave trade from other colonies like Curaçao and Jamaica.

---

## Slide 8

Road to Revolution

Early Discontent: Despite the prosperity of some, the Creoles were frustrated by Spanish trade restrictions and lack of political power compared to "peninsulares" (Spanish-born officials).

1806 Attempted Invasion: Revolutionary Francisco de Miranda attempted an invasion with American volunteers, which, though failing, signaled growing sentiment for independence.

---

## Slide 9

Road to Revolution

Influence of European Events

Napoleon’s invasion of Spain (1808) and removal of King Ferdinand VII created a power vacuum.

The Turning Point

By early 1810, the fall of the Spanish Central Junta prompted the Mantuanos to act.

---

## Slide 10

April 19, 1810: The Revolution

The Act: On April 19, 1810, the Caracas City Council (Cabildo), supported by the Creoles, deposed the Spanish Captain General, Vicente Emparan.

Formation of Junta: The Supreme Junta of Caracas was created. While technically ruling in the name of the deposed King Ferdinand VII, it was an act of self-government, not absolute independence.

This event marks the start of the Venezuelan independence struggle and the end of the colonial period

The 2026 U.S. Military Intervention in Venezuela: Operation Absolute Resolve

A concise briefing on the January 2026 operation: planning, execution, motives, regional reactions, human costs, and the geopolitical aftermath. This deck summarizes key events, evidence cited by the U.S., regional impacts, and near-term uncertainties.

## Slide 25

Prelude to Intervention: Rising Tensions and Escalation

Pressure and Accusations

Since 2024 the U.S. increased sanctions and diplomatic pressure alleging narcoterrorism, election fraud, and state capture by Maduro's inner circle.

Maritime Enforcement

Over 35 U.S. strikes on suspected drug-trafficking vessels near Venezuela resulted in 115+ suspected cartel fatalities by late 2025, shifting operational posture toward kinetic options.

Legal Precedent

November 2025: Cartel de los Soles designated as an FTO — a designation that enabled expanded authorities and military-laden law-enforcement options.

---

## Slide 26

Operation Absolute Resolve: The Capture of Nicolás Maduro

On January 3, 2026 at 0200 local time, the U.S. executed synchronized strikes to suppress air defenses, followed by a Special Operations insertion targeting Maduro’s Caracas compound. Forces captured Maduro and Cilia Flores and transferred them to U.S. custody to face narcoterrorism charges.

1

Phase 1 — Suppression

Air and electronic warfare degraded regime C2 and air defenses to enable safe insertion.

2

Phase 2 — Direct Action

SOF teams conducted a precision raid; minimal U.S. military casualties reported; Maduro transported to New York.

3

Legal Outcome

Maduro and Flores pleaded not guilty on Jan 5; prosecution framed charges around narcoterrorism and transnational organized crime.

---

## Slide 27

Precision Strike to Decapitate a Regime

A high-impact image underscores the operation’s tactical profile: rapid airborne insertion, surgical targeting, and the objective of leadership removal while avoiding wider occupation.

---

## Slide 28

Strategic Motives: Narcoterrorism, Regime Change, and Oil Interests

The U.S. presented the action as law-enforcement backed by military force to dismantle narcoterror networks. Strategic drivers cited publicly and privately included restoring regional security, reducing illicit narcotics flows, and securing access to Venezuela’s large oil reserves.

Energy Policy

Sanctions lifted post-capture; immediate contracts and a $50M/barrel supply framework announced to stabilize markets.

Economic Transition

U.S. signaled support for privatization initiatives to attract western investment in petroleum sector reforms.

---

## Slide 29

International and Regional Reactions: Controversy and Consequences

Global Institutions

UN experts and legal scholars condemned the raid as breaching sovereignty and the UN Charter, triggering debates about extraterritorial enforcement.

Regime Response

Caracas labeled the action an “imperialist kidnapping”; reported casualties included 23 Venezuelan security personnel and 32 Cuban agents.

Domestic & Diaspora Reactions

Opposition supporters and many in the diaspora celebrated; others warned intervention risks and called for sovereignty-respecting solutions.

---

## Slide 30

Diplomatic Shifts and Regional Security Dynamics

The operation prompted an abrupt normalization: U.S. embassy in Caracas reopened, and bilateral channels reactivated. Policymakers framed the move as part of a broader push to counter China and Russia’s foothold in Latin America.

Reopened embassies and intelligence cooperation touted as stabilizing measures.

Regional responses split: some governments cautiously engaged; others condemned the intrusion.

Chart: simplified regional posture — mixed alignment highlights fractured hemispheric consensus.

---

## Slide 31

The Human Cost and Political Fallout

Casualties & Displacement

Operation-related fighting resulted in 50+ reported casualties among Venezuelan and Cuban forces; noncombatant displacement and localized violence rose.

Domestic Turmoil

Protests erupted for and against the intervention; concerns grew over a power vacuum and armed groups exploiting instability.

Prisoner & Justice Issues

Acting authorities granted amnesties and released 431+ political detainees by mid-February, reshaping the political landscape.

Conclusion: A New Chapter in U.S.-Venezuela Relations and Hemispheric Security

Operation Absolute Resolve is a defining, controversial assertion of U.S. power in the hemisphere — fast, targeted, and legally complex. Its success will be judged not only by immediate tactical outcomes but by long-term stability, legitimate governance in Venezuela, and how regional and global actors respond.

1

Short Term

Diplomatic normalization and energy deals proceed amid legal disputes and domestic upheaval.

2

Medium Term

3

Long Term

Shifts in hemispheric alignments and precedent-setting implications for international law and intervention norms.
