                                      "Colonization"

Ques - 1. Explain how the British colonial agricultural system had significantly altered the land,
labor, agriculture and infrastructural pattern of colonized Africa.

Answer: Colonialism was a system of extracting wealth from the continent; agricultural
products were part of this wealth. Colonial powers normally emphasised production which met
their needs and tastes, not the needs of the local communities which affects local or colonized
agricultural system.

From the outset we should point out that pre-mercantilist Africa was self-sufficient in food
production and was neither inferior nor weaker than the rest of the Ancient World. Food
production was the primary goal of agriculture. Sophisticated techniques had evolved which
were suited to the needs of the people and their fragile environment and which helped them
cope with cyclical droughts and crop failures due to other causes. Adequate food security and
self-reliance were common before European colonization. Methods had been developed for
storage of surpluses and complex patterns of trade had been developed around food availability.

The British colonial agricultural system had also a profound and lasting impact on Africa,
reshaping land use, labor patterns, agricultural production, and infrastructure in ways that
benefited the colonial economy but often harmed local communities. Here's how these areas
were altered:

Land: Before the African colonization, all countries of Africa was self sufficient. The land of
Africa was fertile and people used to produce food crop such as maize, lentil etc for the
nutrition. After colonization, Europe forced Africa to cultivate cash crop instead of food crop.
Cash crop such as rubber, cocoa, tea, coffee etc were produced forcefully by the African labor
for the own need of Europe. Most of the arable land were therefore occupied to produce to
cash crop. Land for producing food crops were about to be destroyed.

Labor: Before colonization, African labor used to produce food crops on their own land. After
colonization, peasants who wanted to produce food crop instead of cash crop were forced to
cultivate cash chop in their own land or on the land forcefully taken by the European or settlers.
Some of labors were migrated to other place in the need of producing cash chop. Self sufficient
peasants became labor for producing carsh crop. 100 million of men and women were involved
in producing cash crop.

Agriculture/food: Since affer colonization Africa was dependent on cultivating cash crop, more
than half of the arable used to produce cash crop, food crop for the locals became insufficient.
Before the colonization, African community had a diversity of producing food crop. As there
started a scarcity of food crops after colonization, people need to import their essential food
crop. Monocropping culture destroyed the traditional agricultural pattern. Therefore, agricultural
pattern changed and african countries became food importing economy.
Infrastructure: The Europeans constructed many infrastructures with a view to the easy
collection of raw materials and cash crops. For example, a railway constructed from Senegal to
Mali through the groundnut field to collect groundnut easily and the railway ended up at the
coastal point. Similarly, in India, European constructed railway to collect cotton.

After colonization, European forced the African peasants to produce cash crop for their own
benefit. There was a phenomenon of low price extraction and high price selling. For example, in
1930's, peanuts bought by marketing board from West African peasants were sold in Britain at
more than seven times what the peasants received.

In summury, the British colonial agricultural system altered African's land use, labor pattern,
agricultural production and infrastructure, favoring the colonial economy while undermining
local communities and traditional ways of life. These changes contributed to the long lasting
socio-economic and environmental challenges faced by many African nations post-
independence.



Ques - 2. Explain how the post colonial states in Africa have continued to pursue policies which
promoted cash crop over food crop.

Answer: Most of the present-day agricultural patterns in Africa have their origins in the colonial
era. These patterns (especially cash crop plantations) were established to meet the needs of
Europe. The post-colonial governments of the 1960s inherited those agricultural patterns and
structures and continued their management.

African people made great sacrifices to get their independence from 1955 to 1965. But even if
some kind of political change has occurred, the economic structures remained almost
untouched, especially the structures of cash crops economy with cocoa and oil palm
plantations in the forest zone, cotton and groundnuts in the Savana of West Africa, and coffee,
tea, sisal and cotton in East Africa. For example, Senegal continued to export groundnuts which
occupied 55 percent of its arable land in 1976 and provided 50 percnt of its foreign exchange in
1977. In 1977, half of Ghana's arable land was planted with cocoa trees. A similar proportion of
arable lands in Kenya was devoted to coffee and tea. The concentration on a limited number of
crops is a characteristic of African agriculture.

In 1984, nine countries in Africa were dependent on just one crop for over 70percent of their
income. Over 70% of Burundi's export earnings came from coffee, which provided over 70% of
Rwanda's earnings and almost 70% of Ethiopia's. Other nations over 50% reliant on one single
crop included Mauritius (sugar), Uganda (coffee), Chad (cotton), Sudan (cotton), Ghana (cocoa),
Kenya (coffee) and Mali (cotton).

That is, after gaining independence, many African states continued to prioritize the cultivation of
cash crops over food crops, often due to a combination of historical legacies, economic
pressures, and global market dynamics. Let's draw examples from Kenya and socialist Tanzania.

Kenya: In Kenya, farmers who grow cash crops get more support, such as loans, advice on
pesticides and fertilisers, and help with marketing, than those who grow food crops. Food crop
farmers often say their boards are slow and inefficient compared to the Kenya Coffee Board.

Tanzania: In Tanzania, although the country promotes self-reliance and peasant farming, in
1983 the government cut the agriculture budget by 3.5% compared to the previous five-year plan.
Most of the money (80%) went to large-scale farms that rely on foreign supplies, while only 2%
was spent on helping small peasant farmers.

In Tanzania, loans from the Rural Development Bank to small farmers growing food dropped
from 6.7% in 1971–72 to just 1% in 1979–80. Meanwhile, loans to government-owned
companies rose from 6.4% to 70.7%. In 1978–79, most of the money for farming inputs (61%)
went to tobacco, a cash crop that harms the environment by depleting soil and causing
deforestation. This shows that, despite its support for peasant farming, Tanzania moved away
from its original policy.

These examples show that Africa's food problems are mostly caused by poor policies. Focusing
too much on cash crops hasn't solved the issue—in fact, it has made it worse.



Ques - 3. What is the impact of such policies on the economy and land pattern of the post
independent African countries?

Answer: Growing demand from industries in Europe and North America drove exponential
growth in export crops at the expense of food security. In Western Africa, exports to Britain and
France doubled five times and three times, respectively, over a 70-year period (1870 to 1940).
The area dedicated to export crops in SSA increased by 56% during the 1800s and a further
136% from 1900 to 1960. This increase was initially fuelled by high commodity prices, but after
1880 it was driven by the need to of-set the impact of declining prices and the onset of WWI.
Hence, the growth in export crop production was driven by colonial demand rather than farmers
responding to market opportunities. This subsection summarizes six inter-related impacts of
this expansion that undermined food security. The policies of prioritizing cash crops over food
crops in post-independence African countries have had significant and long-lasting impacts on
both their economies and land patterns. The impacts can be discussed as below.

1. Price Decline: All countries of Africa started to grow theirs cash crop cultivation at the same
time. Therefore the world market was flooded by over supply of cash crop. Whereas, the
demand for these cash crop was relatively smaller. Therefore, there was surplus in the world
market. As a result, price of cash crop declines.

2. Deterioration in Terms of Trade: Despite the priorities the governments give to cash crops,
their prices continued to fall over time in comparison to the prices of manufactured goods. In
1960, three tons of bananas could buy a tractor. In 1970, that same tractor costs the equivalent
of eleven tons of bananas. 25 tons of exported rubber an African country could import six
tractors in 1960. In 1975 it could import only 2 tractors. This deterioration of the terms of trade
may well continue.

3. Decline In Foreign Earnings: As price of cash сrop deсreased and as African was dependent
on cash crop, the foreign earnings declined. In 1970 to 1980 drought hit in Africa and cash crop
cultivation destroyed. It also resulted in decline in foreign currency earnings. In WWII, the total
loss of foreign earning exceeded all foreign investment, fund, loan, grants.

4. Import/debt: As African imports food crop by using this foreign currency, declining in foreign
earnings results in scarcity of food crop. Declining in food supply increased importing food
which led in taking foreign loans/debt. By 1984 1 in 4 Africans are fed by impor. External debt
reached 130 billion.

5. Loss and replacement of traditional sources of nutrition: Land clearance and mono-cropping
destroyed opportunities for grazing, hunting, fishing, and growing nutritious pulses and
vegetables. Nutrition and livestock productivity declined and income to supplement diets was
lost. Cassava and dent maize replaced more nutritious staples due to their higher calorific
content, faster growth, and lower labour requirements. Dent maize was preferred for export as it
had higher yields and larger kernels and was more suitable for mechanized milling. Serious
health issues arose when maize became the primary staple, as it is low in essential amino acids
and associated with diarrhoea, dementia, dermatitis, and cognitive deficiencies. Malnutrition
has long-term impacts on physical development and the ability to pursue education and careers
and is therefore an impediment to the prosperity of individuals and households. Food shortages
and poor nutrition continued to increase during the 1920s and the calorie intake dropped to
starvation level. In response, maize production was further encouraged, which increased the
calorie intake but made malnutrition endemic.

6. Inequality within communities: The wealthy and powerful local elite focused on achieving
colonial pursuits rather than community prosperity. In the peasant colonies, the increased
demand for export crops, such as cocoa in Ghana, forced to 'sell' land to wealthy and infuential
farmers from neighbouring areas, widening the inequality experienced by local farmers and
migrant workers. The cash-advance crop mortgage system also exacerbated inequality. This
system lent money to small-scale farmers unable to fund export production. Interest rates
varied but were typically 50-100% for cocoa, and up to 200% on the savanna. Advances were
based on crop estimates and were to be repaid at harvest, regardless of the outcome.
Considering the vagaries of late rains, locusts, price variability, disease, and personal calamity,
harvests regularly fell short of estimates, Farmers had to borrow more money at escalating
rates, creating a vicious circle of borrowing and debt re-payments. Marchant manipulation of
scales, transport prices and chiefs' timing of tax payments are further examples of how the
elites widened inequality and created dependency.
