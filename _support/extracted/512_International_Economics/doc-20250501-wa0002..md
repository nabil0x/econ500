                              Danger In Dairy Industry Of Bangladesh

In 1971, Bangladesh produced about 1 lac. metric tons of milk, which increased to 70 lac. metric
tons in 2016.

In 2023-24, milk production in Bangladesh was 13 million metric tons, while the demand for milk
was 15 million tons - a deficit of about 15%.

Due to the underdeveloped dairy sector, domestic production cannot meet domestic demand.
Therefore, milk powder has to be imported under free trade.

Consumer Benefit: Middle class consumers in Bangladesh prefer imported milk powder
because imported milk powder is much cheaper. Quality, reliability, storage issues and health
issues are other reasons, due to which consumers are benefited by imported milk powder.



                              The Problems Of Cheap Imports

A major problem facing Bangladesh’s milk industry is the large amount of imported milk powder.
Whole milk powder is sold directly to consumers, while skimmed milk powder is used to make
dairy products. This costs the country a lot — back in 2007-08, Bangladesh spent Taka 10.5
billion to import 41,000 tonnes of milk powder. Overall, about 27% of the country’s milk needs
are met through imports, with 20% to 50% of skimmed milk powder coming from the EU.

With proper support, local farmers could produce this milk instead. But imported milk powder is
often cheaper and heavily advertised, making it harder for local producers to
compete—especially in cities, where importers have strong influence. The price of fresh milk in
Bangladesh is also affected by the price of milk powder. Because, milk powder is a substitute
for fresh milk.

The presence of EU powdered milk on the global market creates unfair competition. It holds
back the growth of dairy industries in developing countries and discourages local farmers from
increasing production to meet rising demand.

Milk Vita: Bangladesh's Milk Vita corporation was established in early 1970's. By the mid-1980s,
Milk Vita cooperative had nearly collapsed, mainly due to unfair competition from cheap
imported milk powder from Europe. In the late 1970s, Milk Vita had grown steadily, collecting 15
million litres of milk a year from about 36,000 small farmers. But imports from Europe, heavily
subsidized and sold far below real cost, flooded the market. As a result, Milk Vita’s collection
dropped to under 3 million litres a year. At that time____

• Imported milk was selling at 1/3 of the cost of milk production in Bangladesh. And

• Milk powder was being sold for less than 20% of its actual cost in Europe.
Bangladesh's dairy sector is still underdeveloped, so local milk production can't meet the
growing demand. Even though, the middleclass consumers somewhat benefitted from imported
milk, poorer people usually consume less milk due to high prices and limited access. Boosting
local production of good-quality fresh milk could help address these issues.

However, milk powder imports increase competition with domestic milk producers. Local
farmers must compete with over subsidized low priced imported milk powder which reduced
incentives for domestic investment and expansion, specially for small producers from
producing more milk to satisfy local demands. As a result, dairy sector is declining over 30
years.

Looking at recent price, it is important to note that - although local milk is currently slightly
cheaper than imported milk, imported milk powder is not only comparable in terms of price, but
also more intensively marketed than local milk.



                                      The Role of state

To protect local dairy farmers, the Bangladeshi government placed tariffs on imported milk
powder.

• The tariff rate was 75% in 2007. However, these tariffs have dropped sharply—from 75% in
2007 to a proposed 5% in post 2007's budget.

• This sparked protests from local producers, as these tariffs are their main protection against
cheap, subsidized milk powder from developed countries, especially the EU.

• The tariff rate is 25% in recent budget. (2023-24)

The government no longer controls milk prices and ended direct subsidies to farmers in 1996.
Now, only Milk Vita receives government support through low-interest loans, grants, and project
funding. While this helps Milk Vita, it makes it hard for other milk companies to compete.

Despite the dairy sector contributing about 2.7% to the country’s GDP, it receives only 0.27% of
the national budget. To help dairy farmers and grow the sector, the government needs to create
a well-rounded plan and invest more in developing the industry.

Arguments in Favor of Protective Policies:

Supporting Local Farmers: The influx of cheap imported powdered milk can push the small and
medium scale farmers out of the market, causing severe financial losses. Protectionist policy
will support them.

Self-sufficiency and Food Security: Enhancing local production improves national food security
and nutritional standards. Reducing import dependence can lead to greater self-reliance.

Impact on Rural Economy: Dairy farming is a key source of income for many rural families.
Without protection, these communities risk losing their livelihoods, negatively impacting the
rural economy.

Encouraging Investment: When farmers see government support, they are more likely to invest
in expanding and modernizing their farms, increasing overall production.
