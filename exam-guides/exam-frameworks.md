## Framework A: Consumer Theory — Preference, Utility & Demand

**Use for:** Micro Tutorial 1 Q1–Q4 — questions about preferences, utility functions, demand derivation

| Paragraph | Content | Key Names/Concepts |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Intro** | Define the preference-based approach: consumer behavior derived from rational preference relations (complete, transitive, reflexive), not cardinal utility | Debreu, Arrow |
| **Body 1** | **Axioms of preference**: completeness (can compare any two bundles), transitivity (consistent ranking), continuity (sudden switches impossible), monotonicity (more is better), convexity (averages preferred to extremes) | — |
| **Body 2** | **Utility representation**: if preferences are continuous and monotonic → ∃ continuous utility function u(·); Strong Monotonicity ⇒ Monotonicity ⇒ LNS | Debreu (1954) representation theorem |
| **Body 3** | **Convexity**: two definitions — (1) if x≻y and x'≻y then tx+(1-t)x'≻y; (2) USC(x) is convex; Strong convexity = unique most-preferred bundle | — |
| **Body 4** | **UMP → Walrasian demand**: solve max u(x) s.t. p·x ≤ w; get x(p,w); properties: homogeneous degree 0, Walras' law p·x(p,w)=w, convex-valued | Walras |
| **Conclusion** | The preference approach is more fundamental than utility — utility is just a convenient representation. Cobb-Douglas is the canonical worked example: x₁ = αw/p₁ | Cobb, Douglas, Roy |

**Key result for Cobb-Douglas:** u = Kx₁^α x₂^β → x₁ = αw/(α+β)p₁, x₂ = βw/(α+β)p₂

## Framework B: Welfare Analysis — CV, EV & Consumer Surplus

**Use for:** Micro Tutorial 2 Q1 — any question comparing CV, EV, and CS

| Paragraph | Content | Key Names/Concepts |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Intro** | Define welfare measures: how to quantify welfare change from price changes using money-metric measures | Hicks |
| **Body 1** | **CV** = "Will you accept?" money after price change to keep you at *original* utility. EV = "Would you pay?" money before price change to put you at *new* utility. | Hicks (1939) |
| **Body 2** | **Relationship**: For normal goods + price decrease: EV > CS > CV. For price increase: CV > CS > EV. Intuition: CV uses new prices, EV uses old prices, CS is path-dependent average | — |
| **Body 3** | **Roy's Identity**: x(p,w) = -∂v/∂p / ∂v/∂w — get Walrasian demand from indirect utility | Roy |
| **Body 4** | **Shephard's Lemma**: h(p,u) = ∂e/∂p — get Hicksian demand from expenditure function. Slutsky: ∂h/∂p = ∂x/∂p + (∂x/∂w)·x (substitution + income effects) | Shephard, Slutsky |
| **Conclusion** | CS is an approximation of CV/EV. They diverge when income effects are large. Hicksian (compensated) demand is steeper than Walrasian for normal goods | — |

## Framework C: Solow Growth Model — Steady State & Dynamics

**Use for:** Macro Tutorial Q1 — Solow model questions (steady state, transition, shocks, convergence)

| Paragraph | Content | Key Names/Concepts |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Intro** | Define the Solow model: neoclassical production Y = K^α (AL)^(1-α); exogenous saving rate s, n, g, δ | Solow (1956), Swan |
| **Body 1** | **Steady state**: k̇ = sy - (n+g+δ)k = 0 → k* = [s/(n+g+δ)]^(1/(1-α)). In steady state, k, y, c are constant per effective worker; Y/L grows at rate g | — |
| **Body 2** | **Transition dynamics**: k above k* → k̇ < 0 → k falls to k*. Convergence speed proportional to distance from k*. | Conditional convergence hypothesis |
| **Body 3** | **Golden Rule**: c = f(k) - (n+g+δ)k. Max c* when f'(k^gold) = n+g+δ. Dynamic inefficiency if k* > k^gold (over-saving) | Phelps (1961) |
| **Body 4** | **Shock analysis (δ ↓)**: k̇=0 locus shifts right → new higher k*; transition: initially k̇ > 0, economy accumulates capital toward new steady state; c* may rise or fall depending on initial position relative to golden rule | — |
| **Body 5** | **Demographic transition (n ↓)**: Bnagladesh falling fertility → lower n → higher k* per effective worker; consumption per capita rises permanently | Barro, MRW |
| **Conclusion** | Evaluate conditional convergence: Solow predicts poor countries grow faster *if* they share same steady state. But developing countries differ in s, n, institutions → divergence possible | Barro (1991), MRW (1992) |

## Framework D: Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans — Optimal Growth

**Use for:** Macro Tutorial Q2 — questions about endogenous saving, phase diagram, policy shocks

| Paragraph | Content | Key Names/Concepts |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Intro** | Define RCK model: households choose consumption path to maximize U = ∫e^{-ρt} u(c) dt; u(c) = c^(1-θ)/(1-θ); saving is endogenous | Ramsey (1928), Cass, Koopmans |
| **Body 1** | **Equations of motion**: k̇ = f(k) - c - (n+g+δ)k; ċ/c = (1/θ)[f'(k) - ρ - θg] | — |
| **Body 2** | **Phase diagram**: k̇=0 locus is inverted-U (c = f(k) - (n+g+δ)k); ċ=0 is vertical at k* where f'(k*) = ρ+θg. Saddle-path stable: only one path reaches steady state | — |
| **Body 3** | **Comparison to Solow**: RCK has lower convergence speed; consumption smoothing slows adjustment; speed λ = (ρ - n - (1-θ)g)/(2) approximately | — |
| **Body 4** | **Government spending shock**: Permanent ↑G → ċ shifts left → lower k* → consumption falls at all levels; temporarily, ċ=0 locus moves → economy jumps to new saddle path; temporary shock has less severe welfare impact | — |
| **Conclusion** | RCK is more realistic (endogenous saving) but harder to calibrate. Solow is simpler but saving rate is ad hoc. For Bangladesh policy analysis, Solow is usually sufficient | — |

## Framework 1: Critique Modernization Theory

**Use for:** Q1, Q3 (3rd tutorial) — any question asking about critiques of modernization theory

| Paragraph | Content | Key Names/Concepts |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Intro** | Define MT: emerged 1940s–50s, Cold War context, Truman's 1949 speech, belief that developing countries are "backward" and need to follow Western path | Rostow, Truman |
| **Body 1** | Explain Rostow's 5 stages (Traditional → Pre-conditions → Take-off → Maturity → Mass Consumption) | Rostow (1960) |
| **Body 2** | **Critique 1: Eurocentrism** — assumes Western experience is universal; ignores that other societies had their own histories/systems | Rodney (1972) — Africa had Mali, Songhay empires |
| **Body 3** | **Critique 2: Ignores colonial history** — underdevelopment was CREATED, not natural; West developed by extracting wealth from colonies | Frank (1967), Rodney |
| **Body 4** | **Critique 3: Oversimplified linear thinking** — development is not a straight line (war, crisis → backward); **Critique 4: Destroys local culture** — "deculturalization" | Matunhu |
| **Conclusion** | Dependency theory offers alternative: centre-periphery framework explains persistent underdevelopment better than MT | Frank, Wallerstein |

## Framework 3: Free Trade in Agriculture — Case Study

**Use for:** Q5b (3rd tutorial) — or any question about trade policy for local industry

| Paragraph | Content | Key Names/Concepts |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Intro** | Classical argument: comparative advantage → specialization → efficiency. But reality is different. | Ricardo (implicit) |
| **Body 1** | **Promise vs Reality**: Dumping (cotton 57%, wheat 40%), FAO index -53% real terms, LDC import bills +40% | WTO AoA |
| **Body 2** | **Bangladesh Dairy Case Study**: 1.4M farms, 7M people, 50% cheaper than EU, 46× more jobs. But 27% of milk imported. | Arla Foods, EU subsidies €1B+ |
| **Body 3** | **Mechanism of destruction**: Subsidized EU imports → price crash (Tk33→26/litre) → farmer income -40% → protests | 2009 price crash |
| **Body 4** | **Policy failure**: Tariffs 75%→5%, subsidies eliminated 1996, budget 0.27% vs 2.7% GDP contribution | Washington Consensus influence |
| **Conclusion** | **Recommendations**: Protect tariffs, restore subsidies, invest in cold chain, enforce anti-dumping, support local procurement | — |

## Framework 4: Patriarchy & Women's Agency in Bangladesh

**Use for:** 2nd Tutorial Q1, Q2 (503)

| Paragraph | Content | Key Names/Concepts |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Intro** | Define the Bangladesh paradox: poor country + oppressive patriarchy + impressive gender gains | Kabeer, World Bank (2008) |
| **Body 1** | **Classic patriarchy**: patrilineal, patrilocal, patriarchal; consequences: "missing women", son preference, restricted mobility | Kandiyoti, Cain, Caldwell, Dyson & Moore |
| **Body 2** | **Indicators of outlier**: Bangladesh outperforms India & Pakistan — fertility 2.2 vs 2.6 vs 3.4; infant mortality 37 vs 47 vs 59; sex ratio normal (105:100) | Drèze & Sen (2013) |
| **Body 3** | **How change happened — agency framework**: women renegotiated patriarchy through cumulative acts, not revolution; spectrum from hidden transcripts to bargaining | Kabeer, Scott, Ortner |
| **Body 4** | **Two pathways**: (1) Societal — visible work changes social value, (2) Household — income → bargaining power | Sen (1990b) |
| **Conclusion** | Change occurs through incremental adjustments + new resources + everyday negotiations, not rupture | Kabeer |

## General Exam Tips

1. **Always define key terms** in your opening sentence
2. **Name your theorists** — every paragraph should reference at least one
3. **Use statistics** — keep the quick reference sheet handy
4. **Connect topics** — show you understand how modernization theory connects to Washington Consensus, etc.
5. **Bangladesh examples** — always bring it back to Bangladesh context
6. **Structure clearly** — use the frameworks above as templates
